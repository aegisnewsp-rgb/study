# Reading Comprehension

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Reading Comprehension (RC) in HAT-UG English tests your ability to decode a 250–400 word passage and answer 4–6 MCQs on main idea, inference, vocabulary-in-context, tone, and author's purpose. HEC Pakistan's HAT-UG syllabus assigns roughly 5% weight to this component, and the section is computer-based (CBT) with passages drawn from humanities, sciences, and social commentary.

| Concept | What the passage gives you | What the question asks |
|---|---|---|
| **Main idea** | Topic sentence of para 1 + recurring theme | The central claim of the whole passage |
| **Inference** | Clues spread across 2+ sentences | A conclusion that *must* follow |
| **Tone** | Diction, adjectives, punctuation | How the author feels (critical, neutral, sarcastic) |
| **Vocabulary in context** | Surrounding 1–2 sentences | Meaning of a word *as used here*, not in general |
| **Purpose** | Verb choices and rhetorical structure | Why the author wrote (inform, persuade, critique) |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"SQ3R + 3A"** — **S**kim for gist, **Q**uestion the stem, **3R** = Read the relevant chunk, Refer back, Re-read if stuck; **3A** = **A**void outside knowledge, **A**ntonym/synonym hunt, **A**bsolute words (always/never) are in most keys wrong.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Skill Map
RC questions on HAT-UG fall into six recurring types. Train yourself to classify each stem in under 5 seconds before re-scanning the passage:

1. **Main Idea / Primary Purpose** — in most keys the *first or last* sentence of para 1, or the *last* sentence of the passage.
2. **Supporting Detail** — keywords from the question will appear verbatim or as a paraphrase near the answer.
3. **Inference / Implication** — no single sentence states it; combine two clues.
4. **Tone / Attitude** — read adjectives, modal verbs (must, can, ought), and punctuation.
5. **Vocabulary in Context** — ignore the dictionary meaning; read 1 sentence before and 1 after.
6. **Author's Purpose / Function** — ask "why this sentence here?" and link to the main idea.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix
Students regularly confuse these pairs. Train the contrast before exam day:

| Easily confused | Main idea | Specific detail | How to tell apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Main idea** vs **Detail** | Covers the whole passage in one sentence | Refers to one fact, name, or example | If the option drops one detail, the passage still makes sense → main idea |
| **Tone** vs **Topic** | How the author writes (adjective) | What the passage is about (noun) | Tone = "skeptical", "admiring"; Topic = "climate change", "AI ethics" |
| **Inference** vs **Assumption** | Logically drawn from given evidence | A hidden premise the author takes for granted | Inference = conclusion you reach; Assumption = belief that *makes* the conclusion possible |
| **Fact** vs **Opinion** | Verifiable in the passage | Judgement word (should, best, worst) | Look for evaluative adjectives or comparatives (more, less) |
| **Paraphrase** vs **Inference** | Same meaning, different words | Goes beyond what is stated | Paraphrase = synonym swap; Inference adds logical step |

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

#### Question:
*(Adapted from past HAT-UG pattern)*

> *"The modern city, once celebrated as the engine of opportunity, now grapples with housing shortages that have pushed rents beyond the reach of middle-income families. Planners propose densification, but critics argue that cramming more units into existing neighbourhoods erodes the very sense of community that makes urban life worthwhile. A more equitable solution, they suggest, lies in revitalising peri-urban settlements rather than stacking buildings skyward."*
>
> **The author's primary purpose is to:**
> (A) advocate densification as the best remedy for housing shortages
> (B) present a balanced view while subtly favouring peri-urban revitalisation
> (C) celebrate the historical achievements of modern cities
> (D) argue that community is irrelevant to housing policy

#### Solution:

1. **Scan the structure (10 sec):** Para opens with a problem ("housing shortages"), introduces one solution ("densification"), then immediately labels it with "but critics argue" — a contrast marker. Final sentence proposes an alternative ("peri-urban revitalisation").
2. **Detect tone in diction:** Words like "celebrated," "grapples," "beyond the reach," "equitable," "worthwhile" lean critical of densification and sympathetic toward the alternative.
3. **Eliminate wrong options:**
 - (A) is the opposite — author does *not* advocate densification.
 - (C) misreads the opening nostalgic clause; it is a foil, not the focus.
 - (D) is contradicted by "the very sense of community… worthwhile."
4. **Pick (B):** the passage uses "but critics argue" (balanced) while the diction and the closing suggestion tilt toward peri-urban revitalisation (subtle favour).

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Option (A) is the trap for students who stop at the first mention of "densification" without reading the contrast marker "but critics argue." Always read at least one sentence *after* any proposed solution in argumentative passages.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Advanced Traps and Exceptions

| Trap type | Pattern in options | How to defeat it |
|---|---|---|
| **True-but-irrelevant** | Factually correct from real-world knowledge | Verify the claim is *stated or supported* in the passage, not just true in life |
| **Extreme language** | "always," "never," "all," "none," "completely" | Replace the extreme word with "in many papers" — does it still hold? If not, drop the option |
| **Scope shift** | Same topic but at a smaller or larger scale than asked | Check whether the question asks about "the passage," "the author," or "a particular paragraph" |
| **Negation stem** | "NOT," "EXCEPT," "LEAST likely" | Underline the negation; the *other* three options will be true |
| **Tone misread** | Neutral wording paraphrased as "critical" or vice-versa | Re-read the passage's adjectives and modal verbs; trust diction over gut feel |
| **Inference over-reach** | Plausible but not *must* follow | Ask: can a reasonable reader disagree? If yes, it is not a valid inference |

#### Edge Cases and Adjacent Topics

- **Two-author passages:** When two voices appear (e.g., quoted expert vs. author), attribute tone and purpose to the *correct speaker*. HAT-UG has begun mixing short attributed quotations since 2023.
- **Negative factual questions:** "Which of the following is NOT mentioned?" — three options are explicitly stated; one is absent. Scan each option literally against the passage.
- **RC and Verbal Ability linkage:** Vocabulary tested in RC (context meaning) overlaps with the Antonym/Synonym section. Build a daily habit of noting 5 in-context words from each RC you practise.
- **Time budget:** In HAT-UG English, RC consumes ~12–15 minutes across 1–2 passages. Cap yourself at 90 seconds per question; if stuck, mark and move — never leave an RC question unanswered (no negative marking).

#### Two Advanced Practice Prompts

1. Take any HAT-UG practice passage and write the *opposite* main-idea option in your own words — then explain why it is still wrong. This trains you to spot scope and direction.
2. For each passage, list three inference questions *without* looking at given options, then check whether the given options stay strictly inside the text. This exposes the "true-but-irrelevant" trap in real time.
