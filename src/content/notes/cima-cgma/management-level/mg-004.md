# The Management Case Study

The Management Case Study is one of three Case Study Examinations in the CGMA Professional Qualification, one at each level. AICPA & CIMA describes them as computer-based, human-marked, three hours long, using pre-seen material, and available four times a year at Pearson VUE centres or online. Results are published 6 to 8 weeks after the relevant window closes.

Those five details decide how you should prepare. Three hours of continuous writing is an endurance test as much as a knowledge test. Human-marked means nobody is looking for a right answer — they are looking for reasoning that holds together. Pre-seen material means the documents are available to you before the exam and the marks are in them. Four windows a year means you can choose. And a six-to-eight-week wait for results means a retake costs you a quarter, so preparation matters more than here.

## What pre-seen material is actually for

It is not reading material. Reading it once produces a feeling of familiarity that evaporates in the exam room.

The productive use is to build an analytical note of two or three pages per document: what the document is, what question it can answer, what figures matter and what they mean, and where the conflicts between documents sit. Conflicts are usually where the marks are, because a case study that agrees with itself is a reading comprehension test.

Then write the structure. Most candidates fail the Case Study on organisation rather than on content — they produce three hours of technically accurate analysis with no spine, and the marker cannot find the judgement.

## A structure that survives three hours

Set the frame first. Spend the opening ten to fifteen minutes reading the requirement and writing the skeleton: the question asked, the sub-questions, the structure of the executive summary, and your order of argument. Everything after that fills slots you have already made.

Front-load the summary. Write it while the analysis is fresh rather than last, and reserve the final fifteen minutes to revise it against what you actually wrote — a summary that does not match the answer is the single most expensive error available to you.

Work in sections against the clock rather than in an unbroken flow. Three hours of continuous unstructured writing degrades sharply after the second hour, and the fix is mechanical: decide in advance how long each section gets and stop when the time is up, even mid-argument.

Answer the requirement literally. "Evaluate" means evaluate — state the position, give the reasons for it, give the reasons against it, and reach a judgement supported by the material. A balanced essay with no conclusion is the most common failure, and it is entirely avoidable.

## Reading for marks

Case Study marking rewards evidence used and analysis of it, in roughly that order. A claim with no reference to the material scores little however correct it is; a reference with no analysis scores a little more; a reference plus analysis plus a consequence scores most.

So for every point, do three things: say what the material shows, say what it means for the question, and say what follows for the organisation's decision. The third step is what separates a strong script from a competent one.

Where the material is silent, say so explicitly and state the assumption you are making. Markers reward a recognised limitation more than a confident invention, and an unstated gap in the evidence is a deduction.

## Common mistakes

Reading pre-seen material once and treating familiarity as preparation. The productive artefact is an analytical note, not a memory of having read it.

Writing the summary last. It is the highest-read part of the script and it goes stale fast.

Running out of time on the final section. Decide the structure, allocate the minutes, and stop.

Giving an opinion with no evidence from the material. The Case Study assesses applied judgement, not personal opinion.

Ignoring a contradiction between two documents because it complicates the argument. It is usually the intended centre of the question.

Ignoring a stated limitation in the case. "The material does not disclose X, so assuming Y" is a scoring sentence.

Over-writing the financial analysis and under-writing the recommendation. A case study that never answers the question asked is a long answer to a different question.
