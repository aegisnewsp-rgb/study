# Constructions, Transformations, Congruence and Similarity — Junior Cycle Mathematics Notes

This block covers three things the Junior Cycle Geometry and Trigonometry strand treats together: producing a required figure with straightedge and compass, moving figures around the plane, and deciding when one shape is an exact copy of another. All three end with the same requirement — a justification that names the reason, not a drawing on its own.

## Constructions

Every construction has two parts and both are marked: **the construction**, and **the justification**.

The constructions in the specification:

**Bisector of an angle.** From the vertex, arc to cut both arms at two points. From those two points, arcs of equal radius intersecting inside the angle. Join the vertex to the crossing point.

*Justification:* the two radii used from the crossing point are equal, so the triangle formed is isosceles, and the line from the vertex bisects the angle.

**Perpendicular bisector of a line segment.** Equal-radius arcs from both endpoints, above and below the line, meeting at two points. Join those two points.

*Justification:* the crossing point is equidistant from both endpoints, so it lies on the bisector; two such points fix the line.

**Perpendicular from a point to a line.** Arc from the point and from a point on the line with the same radius; then the bisector procedure above.

**Tangent to a circle at a point on the circle.** Join the centre to the point. Construct the perpendicular to that line at that point. Extend it.

*Justification:* a tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact.

**Parallel to a given line through a point.** Copy the angle at a point on the original line, then construct equal corresponding angles.

**Equilateral triangle on a given side.** Arcs of radius equal to the side from both endpoints; join the crossings.

**Bisector of an angle formed by a line and a point** — drop a perpendicular from the point to the line, then bisect the resulting segment.

State the arcs and name the intersection points in words. A drawing alone, however accurate, does not earn the justification marks.

## Transformations

Four transformations, each with a named centre or axis:

| Transformation | Named by | What is preserved |
|---|---|---|
| **Translation** | A vector (Δ*x*, Δ*y*) | Length, angle, orientation |
| **Reflection** | A line (the axis or mirror line) | Length, angle; orientation reversed |
| **Rotation** | A centre and an angle | Length, angle, orientation |
| **Enlargement** | A centre and a scale factor | Angle; lengths multiplied by the factor |

Work the same four questions for any transformation, and the marking scheme rewards all four:

1. What is the centre, axis or vector?
2. What is the angle or scale factor?
3. What happens to the coordinates — new position of each vertex?
4. What happens to lengths, angles and orientation?

For an **enlargement with a negative scale factor**, the image is on the opposite side of the centre and the orientation is reversed; with a positive factor it is on the same side and orientation is preserved. That sign behaviour is a named outcome and is asked directly.

An **enlargement of scale factor 1** is a translation-like move that preserves everything; a scale factor of −1 is a point reflection. Know these two edges.

## Congruence

Two triangles are congruent when they are identical in size and shape — same side lengths and same angles. To prove it you must name the criterion and match the pairs.

| Criterion | What is equal |
|---|---|
| **SSS** | Three sides |
| **SAS** | Two sides and the included angle |
| **ASA** | Two angles and the included side |
| **AAS** | Two angles and a non-included side |
| **RHS** | Right angle, hypotenuse, one side (a special case of SAS) |

Two rules that cost marks every year:

- **The included angle matters.** SAS requires the angle *between* the two named sides. Two sides and a non-included angle does not give congruence — it gives the ambiguous case in trigonometry.
- **Name the criterion.** "Angle, side, angle" is not the same entry as "SAS" in a mark scheme, and the reverse of the triangles is what proves it: show the sides and angles that match in triangle ABC to those in triangle DEF, then conclude.

The writing convention: *AB = DE (given), ∠BAC = ∠EDF (alternate angles), ∠ABC = ∠DEF (isosceles triangle), so ΔABC ≅ ΔDEF by ASA.*

## Similarity

Similar triangles have the same shape — equal angles, proportional sides — but not necessarily the same size.

**Criteria:** AAA, with two angle equalities sufficient.

**Ratio of corresponding sides equals the ratio of corresponding areas**, and the area ratio is the square of the length ratio. This is assessed and is easy marks once you know which way round: linear ratio 2 : 1 means area ratio 4 : 1, never 2 : 1.

**Be careful with direction.** If ΔABC ~ ΔDEF, then *AB* corresponds to *DE*, *BC* to *EF*, and *CA* to *FD*. The order of the letters is not decorative; it tells you the correspondence, and reading it the wrong way round gives a scale factor that is the reciprocal of the correct one.

**Areas and perimeters** scale differently — linearly for perimeter, squared for area. Problems about a model or a map turn on exactly this.

## Self-check

1. You construct a perpendicular bisector of a 6 cm segment. What is the justification? *(The two crossing points of the equal-radius arcs are each equidistant from both endpoints, so both lie on the perpendicular bisector; two points fix the line. Stating that the arcs are equal-radius is what makes the equidistance argument valid.)*
2. An enlargement has scale factor −2. Where does the image sit relative to the centre? *(On the opposite side, at twice the distance. A negative factor reflects through the centre as well as scaling, which reverses the orientation.)*
3. You know two sides and a non-included angle of a triangle. Can you conclude congruence? *(No. SAS requires the included angle. Two sides and a non-included angle leave two possible triangles — the ambiguous case — so it cannot establish congruence.)*
4. A triangle is enlarged by a factor of 3. What happens to its area? *(It becomes nine times as large. Area scales with the square of the linear factor; using a factor of 3 gives the wrong answer and is the standard error.)*
5. ΔABC ~ ΔDEF and *AB* = 6, *DE* = 9. What is *AC* if *FD* = 12? *(The scale factor is 9/6 = 3/2 from the first triangle to the second, so *AC* = 12 ÷ (3/2) = 8. The correspondence runs A→D, so *AC* pairs with *DF*, which is what the order of the letters is telling you.)*
