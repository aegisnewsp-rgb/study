# Comparing — Leaving Certificate (Established) English Notes

Comparing is the comparative study strand of the NCCA Leaving Certificate English specification: the skill of reading across multiple texts to draw sustained connections of theme, style, context and concern. Under the revised specification, candidates at both Ordinary and Higher level study *three* texts together in the comparative mode and a fourth text studied individually, and the SEC examination reflects this on Paper 2. At Higher level, Shakespeare is a mandatory component of the comparative mode — candidates are expected to study at least one Shakespeare play alongside two further texts in a sustained comparative argument.

The strand carries a weight rating of 3 out of 5 in the topic catalogue because the comparative section of Paper 2 is one of the two large answer blocks on the paper, but the marks attached to the strand also flow into the comprehension questions on Paper 1 and the close-reading skill that runs through both papers. A candidate who can read *across* texts — naming a theme in one writer and tracing the same theme, transformed, in another — outscores a candidate who has memorised one writer deeply but cannot make the connection. The marks for synthesis are explicitly higher than the marks for separate-text coverage.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Strand weight & spread**: Weight 3/5 in the topic catalogue. Comparative marks sit on Paper 2 and indirectly on Paper 1 comprehension items — under-preparing it depresses both papers.
- **The new comparative brief**: Three texts studied together for sustained comparison, plus one text studied individually. Confirm the live prescribed rotation on examinations.ie — the SEC updates the comparative list each year.
- **Higher level and Shakespeare**: Shakespeare is mandatory at Higher level within the comparative mode. At Ordinary level Shakespeare is optional. Confirm the live prescription on curriculumonline.ie for each year.
- **First move on any comparative prompt**: Identify the *theme or concern* the prompt names (e.g. memory, identity, conflict, power) before naming any text. The theme is the bridge across the three texts.
- **Comparative paragraph structure**: Each comparative paragraph should name *at least two* of the three texts and use *short, precise quotation* from each. A paragraph that quotes only one text is not yet comparative.
- **PESTO for comparative openings**: *Position (my argument), Example (a short quotation from text A), Statement (the link to text B), Topic sentence (what this paragraph argues), Orientation (how it serves the thesis)*. Skip the generic introduction; open on the comparison.
- **Contextual contrast**: A comparative paragraph that contrasts the *contexts* of two writers (period, culture, audience) earns higher marks than a paragraph that simply contrasts their *themes*. The mark scheme rewards candidates who can hold both axes at once.
- **The conclusion sentence**: The comparative answer should end on a sentence that synthesises — "across these three writers, X is the central concern, but each arrives at it through a different Y". That sentence is the synthesis mark.

#### Examiner traps

- Treating the three comparative texts as three separate essays and stitching them together — the examiner credits *comparison*, not coverage.
- Quoting long passages (six lines or more) when one short phrase would do. Comparative answers earn marks for *precise* quotation across multiple texts, not for volume.
- Letting one text dominate the answer. The mark scheme requires that each of the three texts receives roughly equal attention; a candidate who writes two pages on text A and a paragraph each on texts B and C forfeits the balance marks.
- Forgetting the *studied individually* text. The specification requires that one text be studied on its own and brought into the comparative framework as a counterpoint. Confirm the live brief on examinations.ie each year.
- Treating Shakespeare as "set text to memorise" rather than as a comparative *voice*. The mark scheme rewards candidates who can connect Shakespeare's choices to those of the other two writers.

#### 30-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Can you name one shared theme across all three of your comparative texts in under 30 seconds?
- [ ] Have you written at least three timed comparative paragraphs in the past month that quote from two texts each?
- [ ] Do you know which of your studied texts is the *individual* text and how it slots into the comparative argument?
- [ ] Can you write a synthesis sentence that names all three comparative texts in one go?

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### Building a comparative reading habit

Comparative reading is a *habit*, not a memory test. The candidate who reads each of the three texts once and then tries to memorise plot summaries has lost the strand before starting. The candidate who reads each text twice and then *re-reads* the moments where a shared theme (memory, identity, conflict) appears in each writer's work has built the muscle.

A four-week study pass that builds the comparative habit:

- **Week 1**: Re-read each of the three comparative texts, marking the moments where the named theme appears. One colour per text; one symbol (e.g. star) for the moments where the theme appears most forcefully.
- **Week 2**: Pick the three strongest theme-moments per text and write a single paragraph on each — *without* referring to the other texts. This is the close-reading half.
- **Week 3**: Combine the close-reading paragraphs into a comparative grid: theme on one axis, text on the other. Where two or three texts share the theme, write a comparative paragraph that quotes from each.
- **Week 4**: Time-bound a full comparative answer under SEC conditions. Read the prompt, plan two minutes, write the synthesis paragraph first, then the comparative body, then a closing sentence.

#### The four axes of comparison

A strong comparative answer works across at least two of the following four axes:

1. **Theme** — the shared concern (memory, identity, conflict, power, love, exile). The most accessible axis; the axis most candidates default to. Strong answers use theme as the *entry point* and then bring in further axes.
2. **Style** — the writer's *choices* with language (diction, syntax, imagery, structure). The mark scheme rewards candidates who can name the technique and then explain its effect in *both* texts.
3. **Context** — the period, culture, audience, form. Comparative paragraphs that contrast Victorian Ireland with contemporary Nigeria, or Shakespearean tragedy with modern drama, earn higher marks than those that ignore context.
4. **Form** — the genre and structure (sonnet, novel, drama, film, monologue). The mark scheme rewards candidates who notice, for example, that two writers handle the same theme through different forms.

The H1-grade comparative answer moves across at least three of these four axes. The Ordinary-grade answer usually sticks to theme and form; the Higher-grade answer adds style and context.

#### Studying Shakespeare as a comparative voice

Shakespeare at Higher level is not a separate component — he is one of the three comparative voices. A useful framing is to treat Shakespeare as a writer working *within* a particular medium (the early-modern stage) with particular conventions (the soliloquy, the prose/verse shift, the shared cultural knowledge of the audience). The other two writers on the comparative list will be working in different mediums (the novel, the contemporary poem, the film), and the mark scheme rewards the candidate who can contrast those mediums.

A practical drill for Shakespeare: pick one scene, identify three techniques (verse/prose shift, imagery, rhetorical structure), then write two comparative paragraphs — one that connects Shakespeare to a 19th-century writer on the comparative list, and one that connects him to a contemporary writer. After three such drills, Shakespeare stops being a "set text" and becomes a comparative voice.

#### Building a synthesis sentence

The synthesis sentence is the closing move of a comparative answer. Three rules:

1. **Name all three texts** in the same sentence. A synthesis sentence that names only two is a partial synthesis.
2. **State the *difference* as well as the *similarity*.** "All three writers explore memory, but each arrives at it through a different form" is a stronger synthesis than "All three writers explore memory".
3. **Land on a *judgement*.** A synthesis sentence that ends with "and this shows that literature can illuminate many things" is a non-sentence. End on a specific claim: "and in doing so each writer reveals that memory is not a record but an act of construction".

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### The comparative brief under the new specification

The new NCCA specification (in force from 2025 onwards and assessed in the live SEC paper from 2026) restructured the comparative brief. Where the older specification asked candidates to compare *three* texts in the comparative mode with no further requirement, the revised specification asks for three comparative texts *plus* one further text studied individually. Confirm the live prescription on curriculumonline.ie each year — the SEC rotates the prescribed texts annually, and a comparative plan built on last year's list will not survive the next exam cycle.

The *individual* text is the candidate's opportunity to demonstrate *depth*. The comparative texts demonstrate *breadth*. A Higher-grade answer uses the individual text as a *counterpoint* — it names what the comparative texts share, then shows how the individual text complicates or resists that shared concern. This is the synthesis move that earns the top band.

#### Common mistakes that depress the grade

- **The "three separate essays" failure mode** — writing one paragraph on text A, then one on text B, then one on text C, with no comparative thread. The mark scheme credits *connection*, not coverage.
- **The "biographical detour" failure mode** — opening a comparative paragraph with a paragraph on the writer's biography, then trying to connect it to the theme. Biographical detail is context; it is not comparison.
- **The "long quotation" failure mode** — quoting six lines when one short phrase would do. Comparative answers earn marks for *precision*, not for length of quotation.
- **The "no synthesis" failure mode** — ending the comparative answer with a summary of each text rather than a synthesis sentence. The synthesis mark is the easiest recoverable mark on the section.
- **The "studied individually text forgotten" failure mode** — writing the comparative section as if all four texts were in the comparative frame, ignoring that the specification asks for *three* comparative and *one* individual. Confirm the live brief each year.
