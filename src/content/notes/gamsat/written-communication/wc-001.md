# Argument Structure and Thesis

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review

GAMSAT's Written Communication section is two essay responses, and ACER describes it as assessing "the ability to generate and develop ideas in writing". Two essays inside a fixed allowance is a small number of chances to make an impression, and there is no partial credit for effort. What gets marked is whether an argument exists, whether it holds together, and whether it develops past its first paragraph.

**The section is two essay responses, so it is a small number of chances with no partial credit for effort.**

- Nothing is marked because an essay is long; two tightly built paragraphs beat four vague ones
- An essay that surveys without committing has not argued, however much it covers
- Padding is the most visible failure: a paragraph that restates the introduction in new words advances nothing
- Unsupported assertion is the single most common failure, and it costs a whole paragraph

**Turn the prompt into a thesis before you write anything.** Prompts arrive as topics, not questions, so compress it into one argument-bearing sentence. If you cannot write that sentence, you do not yet know what the essay is and everything after it will drift.

> Topic: competition in sport.
> Argument: without competition, standards fall, because the incentive that drives improvement is removed.

Some prompts admit several positions. That is deliberate: you are marked on taking a position and developing it, not on which position you take — but you must take one, and a prompt that allows several still penalises the essay that refuses to.

**Every body paragraph does the same four jobs, in this order.**

1. Claim — one sentence stating what this paragraph argues, as a point rather than a topic
2. Reasoning — where the marks actually live
3. Support — example, case, mechanism or consequence; concrete beats abstract every time
4. Link back to the thesis, so the paragraph is doing work rather than floating

**Then order the claims.** Strongest first is right in a short essay where you risk not finishing; least-to-most contested carries the reader to your real point; a contrast (objection, then reply) uses the prompt's tension instead of ignoring it. Do not save your best paragraph for last unless you are sure you will reach it — an essay that ends mid-thought loses more than a slightly weaker final paragraph would.

**A prompt you dislike still gets written.** Take the defensible side, argue the topic honestly and usefully, or refine an over-broad prompt in one early sentence and then commit to the narrowed question rather than retreating back into surveying.

**Two minutes of checking beats two extra minutes of writing.** Name what each paragraph argues in order. Confirm you answered the question asked, not the one you preferred. Find the paragraph you can delete without breaking the essay. And confirm you left time for the conclusion at all.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study

#### What an essay is actually being marked on

Four things, roughly in order of how frequently they fail.

A position — did you actually answer the question, or did you write about the topic? An essay that surveys without committing has not argued. Then development: did each paragraph advance the argument, or restate the introduction in different words? Padding is highly visible here. Then reasoning: are the claims supported, and do the supports follow? Unsupported assertion is the single most common failure. And finally control: can the reader follow it, and did you say it in the time allowed?

Nobody marks your essay because it is long. Two tightly built paragraphs beat four vague ones.

#### Find the question under the prompt

Prompts are phrased as topics, not questions. "Discuss the value of competition" is not asking for a summary of both sides. Before writing, turn the topic into an argument in one sentence:

> Topic: competition in sport.
> Argument: without competition, standards fall, because the incentive that drives improvement is removed.

That sentence is your thesis. If you cannot write it, you do not yet know what your essay is, and every paragraph after it will drift.

Some prompts have no single right answer. That is the point: you are being marked on whether you take a position and develop it, not on which position you take. But take one. A prompt that admits several positions still penalises the essay that refuses to.

#### Paragraph shape that works

Every body paragraph does the same four jobs in the same order. It opens with a claim — one sentence stating what this paragraph argues, which is a point rather than a topic. It then does the reasoning, and this is where the marks live. It follows with support: an example, a case, a mechanism, a consequence, and concrete beats abstract every time. Last, it links back to the thesis, so the paragraph is doing work rather than floating.

The common failure is four paragraphs that each begin with a different claim. Read the paragraph openings on their own and ask whether they build toward each other. If they do not, you have not written an essay, you have written a list of observations — nothing is accumulating.

#### Ordering matters more than you think

Once you have three or four claims, put them in an order that does some work. Strongest first is the right call in a short essay where you risk not finishing. Building from the least to the most contested carries the reader to your real point. And when the prompt contains real tension, a contrast — an objection, then a reply — uses it instead of ignoring it.

Do not save your best paragraph for last unless you are confident you will reach it. An essay that ends mid-thought loses more than a slightly weaker final paragraph would.

#### Handling a prompt you dislike

You will get an idea you find dull or one you disagree with, and you still have to write it. Three moves work.

Take the defensible side. There is almost always a reasonable position available, and finding it and arguing it properly beats spending the essay fighting the prompt.

Or argue the topic honestly and usefully. Even one paragraph of genuine substance is worth more than three of padding.

Or refine the question. A single early sentence that narrows an over-broad prompt is legitimate and often the strongest move available — but you then have to commit to the narrowed question rather than retreating into surveying everything again.

#### Before you move on

Two minutes of checking is worth more than two extra minutes of writing. Can you name what each paragraph argues, in order? Have you answered the question that was asked, or one you preferred? Is there a paragraph you can delete without the essay breaking? And did you leave time to write the conclusion at all?

#### Common mistakes

The most frequent one is writing an essay that explains the topic rather than arguing a position — it reads as informative and scores badly, because there is nothing for the marker to weigh.

Next is the conclusion that repeats the introduction with "in conclusion" added, which is padding dressed as a formality. Six paragraphs making the same point at increasing length is the same failure at larger scale. Asserting a claim and then offering a restatement of it as if it were support wastes a paragraph that should have been your strongest.

And never naming the strongest objection reads as not having thought about the topic, which is exactly what it is.

### 🔴 Extended — Mastery

#### Reading your paragraph openings on their own is the fastest diagnostic there is
Take the first sentence of every body paragraph and read them consecutively, with no other text. If those sentences build toward each other, you have an essay. If each states a different claim with nothing connecting them, you have a list of observations, and nothing accumulates — which is precisely what the marker is looking for when they ask whether the argument develops past its first paragraph. This test costs about a minute and catches the padding failure that is otherwise invisible in the finished draft.

#### Distinguish the four failure types, because they need different repairs

| Failure | What it looks like | The repair |
|---|---|---|
| Topic mistaken for argument | Reads as informative; nothing for the marker to weigh | Commit to a position in one thesis sentence |
| Restatement as support | A claim followed by the same claim in other words | Replace with example, mechanism or consequence |
| Paragraph that floats | Opens a claim that connects to nothing | Link it back to the thesis explicitly |
| Conclusion as summary | Introduction restated with a stock closing formula bolted on the front | Delete it, or state what the argument now establishes |

Six paragraphs making the same point at increasing length is the restatement failure at larger scale. Asserting a claim and then offering a restatement of it as support wastes a paragraph that should have been your strongest.

#### Never naming the strongest objection is a tell
An essay that engages no objection reads as not having thought about the topic, which is exactly what it is. The move is cheap: state the best objection in a sentence, then answer it. This also solves ordering, because an objection-then-reply pair is itself an ordering principle that uses the prompt's tension rather than ignoring it.

#### Ordering is a decision about risk, not about elegance
Three or four claims can be ordered several defensible ways, and the right choice depends on your confidence and your clock. Strongest first is correct when the real risk is not finishing. Least-to-most contested is correct when the reader needs carrying. And a contrast structure is correct when the prompt contains genuine tension. The error is leaving the order to drift, so that your strongest material ends up after the point where you run out of time.

#### The clock beats the draft you did not finish
An essay that ends mid-thought forfeits marks that were already earned in earlier paragraphs, so budgeting for the conclusion is a correctness issue rather than a finishing touch. Reserve the last few minutes for it explicitly, and remember that ACER publishes no per-task mark breakdown for the writing section — so the section is marked as a whole and an unfinished essay cannot be compensated for elsewhere in the response.
