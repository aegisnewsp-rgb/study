# Chemical Bonding — ECAT (Engineering)

Chemical Bonding is the second topic of the ECAT (Engineering) Chemistry syllabus, sitting between atomic structure and the reactions-of-organic-and-inorganic-chemistry block. The paper tests ionic, covalent and metallic bonding along with the geometry rules that follow from VSEPR, the hybridisation scheme, and the intermolecular forces that decide melting points, boiling points and solubility. MCQs lean on three recurring patterns: rank melting points of a small set using intermolecular-force logic, identify the geometry of a given central atom from its steric number, and decide whether a covalent bond is polar/non-polar from an electronegativity difference. This topic also feeds forward into reaction mechanisms in Organic Chemistry and into lattice-energy calculations.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

**Three primary bond types.**
- **Ionic** — transfer of electrons between metal and non-metal; held together by Coulomb attraction in a lattice. Examples: NaCl, MgO, CaF₂.
- **Covalent** — sharing of electrons between non-metals; can be polar (between unlike atoms) or non-polar (between like atoms). Examples: H₂, O₂, CH₄, H₂O.
- **Metallic** — delocalised "sea" of electrons around a lattice of metal cations. Examples: Cu, Fe, alloys.

**Electronegativity (Pauling scale).** Fluorine is the highest at 4.0; caesium is the lowest at 0.7. A difference $\Delta\chi \geq 1.7$ is usually ionic; $\Delta\chi < 1.7$ but $> 0$ is polar covalent; $\Delta\chi = 0$ (same element) is non-polar covalent.

**VSEPR geometries (steric number, SN).** SN = (number of bonded atoms) + (number of lone pairs on the central atom).

| SN | Lone pairs | Geometry | Bond angle | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0 | Linear | 180° | BeCl₂ |
| 3 | 0 | Trigonal planar | 120° | BF₃ |
| 3 | 1 | Bent (V-shape) | < 120° | SO₂ |
| 4 | 0 | Tetrahedral | 109.5° | CH₄ |
| 4 | 1 | Trigonal pyramidal | < 109.5° | NH₃ |
| 4 | 2 | Bent | < 109.5° | H₂O |
| 5 | 0 | Trigonal bipyramidal | 90° / 120° | PCl₅ |
| 5 | 1 | See-saw | varies | SF₄ |
| 5 | 2 | T-shaped | 90° | ClF₃ |
| 5 | 3 | Linear | 180° | XeF₂ |
| 6 | 0 | Octahedral | 90° | SF₆ |
| 6 | 1 | Square pyramidal | 90° | BrF₅ |
| 6 | 2 | Square planar | 90° | XeF₄ |

**Hybridisation** (central atom): SN 2 → sp, SN 3 → sp², SN 4 → sp³, SN 5 → sp³d, SN 6 → sp³d². A double bond still counts as one domain for VSEPR but uses one p orbital, so SN stays at 3 for an sp² carbon with one double bond.

**Intermolecular forces (weakest → strongest, for similar-sized molecules).**
1. **London dispersion** — present in all molecules; increases with molar mass and contact surface area.
2. **Dipole-dipole** — present in polar molecules; needs a permanent dipole.
3. **Hydrogen bonding** — present when H is bonded to F, O or N; a special strong dipole-dipole interaction.

**Lattice energy trends.** Lattice energy $U \propto q^+ q^- / (r^+ + r^-)$; high charge + small ionic radii → very strong lattice. Born-Haber cycles give $U = \Delta H_{sub} + IE + EA + \Delta H_f$ (with signs).

**Quick check before the exam.**
- Can you sketch each VSEPR geometry and write the central-atom hybridisation?
- Can you rank the boiling points of HF, HCl, HBr, HI from hydrogen-bonding vs dispersion logic?
- Can you decide whether a bond is ionic, polar covalent or non-polar covalent from $\Delta\chi$?

### 🟡 Standard — Exam Prep (3d-3w)

#### Ionic bonding and lattice energy

An ionic bond forms when the energy released by forming the lattice exceeds the sum of ionisation energy and electron affinity for the gaseous ions. For NaCl:

$$\text{Na}(s) + \tfrac{1}{2}\text{Cl}_2(g) \longrightarrow \text{NaCl}(s),\quad \Delta H_f = -411\,\text{kJ/mol}.$$

The Born-Haber cycle partitions this into five steps: sublimation of Na, dissociation of Cl₂, ionisation of Na, electron affinity of Cl, and lattice formation. The lattice energy of NaCl is about $-787\,\text{kJ/mol}$; the other four steps contribute a smaller positive cost. The cycle is a bookkeeping device: the actual physical process is single-step, but the cycle lets you compute quantities you cannot measure directly.

**Lattice energy trends.** Within a group of cations with the same charge, $U$ decreases down the group because the ionic radius grows (e.g., LiF > NaF > KF > RbF > CsF). Within a period with the same charge, $U$ increases as charge grows (NaCl < MgCl₂ < AlCl₃). Higher charge + smaller radius = stronger lattice = higher melting point, lower solubility (mostly).

**Polarising power and Fajans' rules.** A small, highly charged cation distorts the electron cloud of a nearby anion (polarisation), producing partial covalent character in what would otherwise be an ionic bond. Fajans' rules: small cation size, high cation charge, large anion size, and an anion with low electronegativity all favour covalent character. AgCl is more covalent than NaCl because Ag⁺ is a much stronger polariser than Na⁺.

#### Covalent bonding, polarity, and resonance

A covalent bond is polar if the two atoms differ in electronegativity. The dipole moment $\mu = q \cdot d$ (C·m, or Debye where $1\,\text{D} = 3.336 \times 10^{-30}\,\text{C·m}$) is non-zero for any bond with $\Delta\chi > 0$. For a polyatomic system, the **net molecular dipole** is the vector sum of the bond dipoles; symmetric geometries (CO₂, BF₃, CCl₄) can have zero molecular dipole despite polar bonds, if the geometry cancels them.

**Resonance** appears when a single Lewis structure cannot capture the bonding. Benzene, the carbonate ion CO₃²⁻, the nitrate ion NO₃⁻, and ozone O₃ are the textbook examples. The actual bond order is intermediate between the resonance structures; in benzene, all six C–C bonds are identical with length 140 pm (between a typical single bond of 154 pm and double bond of 134 pm). To predict whether a molecule shows resonance, count electrons and check whether the sum of bond orders matches the available valence electrons.

**Formal charge.** Assign formal charge = (group valence electrons) − (non-bonding electrons) − (1/2 × bonding electrons). The best Lewis structure minimises formal charges; charges of the same sign on adjacent atoms are a red flag; negative formal charge should sit on the more electronegative atom.

**Coordinate (dative) bonds.** A coordinate bond is a covalent bond where one atom contributes both electrons. Examples: NH₄⁺ (N→H⁺), H₃O⁺ (O→H⁺), and the bonding in metal complexes (ligand→metal). Once formed, it is indistinguishable from an ordinary covalent bond; the "coordinate" label describes how it was formed, not its current strength.

#### VSEPR and molecular geometry

The VSEPR model has three rules:
1. Electron domains (bonding or non-bonding) arrange to maximise their separation.
2. Lone pairs occupy more space than bonding pairs (because they are held by only one nucleus), so bond angles are compressed near lone pairs.
3. Multiple bonds count as one domain but are bulkier than single bonds, so they cause slight compression of adjacent bond angles.

Practical procedure to apply VSEPR:
1. Draw the Lewis structure, count the total electron domains on the central atom.
2. Subtract the lone pairs to get the molecular geometry name (vs. the electron-domain geometry).
3. Predict the bond angles — lone pairs reduce angles, multiple bonds reduce angles slightly.

For example, NO₂⁻ has SN = 3 with one lone pair on N, giving a **bent** geometry with a bond angle near 115°. (NO₂ has an unpaired electron and is therefore a radical — different geometry story.) For SO₃, SN = 3 with no lone pairs on S, giving trigonal planar with all angles 120°.

#### Hybridisation

The simple mapping is:
- SN 2 → **sp** (one s + one p)
- SN 3 → **sp²** (one s + two p)
- SN 4 → **sp³** (one s + three p)
- SN 5 → **sp³d** (one s + three p + one d)
- SN 6 → **sp³d²** (one s + three p + two d)

A double bond on the central atom still counts as one VSEPR domain but uses one p orbital, so the hybridisation goes up by one. Example: the carbon in formaldehyde (H₂C=O) has SN = 3 and is sp² hybridised. The C=O π bond uses the unhybridised p orbital on C and one on O.

Resonance does not change hybridisation: all carbons in benzene are sp², regardless of which resonance structure you draw.

#### Intermolecular forces

**London dispersion** is always present. It is caused by instantaneous dipoles that induce opposite dipoles in neighbouring molecules; the resulting attractive force scales roughly as $1/r^6$ at short range. Larger, more polarisable molecules have stronger dispersion. This is why I₂ is a solid while F₂ is a gas at room temperature, and why long-chain alkanes have higher boiling points than short ones.

**Dipole-dipole** interactions require a permanent molecular dipole. They are typically a few kJ/mol stronger than dispersion for similar-sized molecules. They are responsible for the fact that acetone (μ = 2.88 D) boils at 56 °C while the non-polar butane (C₄H₁₀) boils at −1 °C, despite butane having a higher molar mass.

**Hydrogen bonding** is a particularly strong dipole-dipole interaction that occurs when H is bonded to one of N, O, or F. The lone pair on a neighbouring N, O, or F accepts the H. It explains the anomalously high boiling points of H₂O, HF, and NH₃ relative to their heavier congeners. It is also why DNA forms a double helix (base pairing through H-bonds) and why proteins fold into specific shapes.

**Ionic interactions in solution.** When an ionic compound dissolves, ion-dipole forces between the ions and water molecules must overcome the lattice energy. Smaller, more highly charged ions with higher charge density generate stronger ion-dipole forces. NaCl dissolves easily in water (high hydration energy compensates for the lattice energy), while AgCl does not (large Ag⁺ polarisation plus lower hydration).

#### Worked example

**Problem.** Rank the boiling points of CH₄, C₂H₆, C₃H₈, and CH₃OH. Explain your reasoning, and identify which intermolecular force dominates for each.

**Solution.** All four are molecular substances, so boiling points reflect intermolecular forces, not bond strengths.

**Methane (CH₄)**, 16 g/mol: non-polar, only London dispersion. Smallest, weakest dispersion → lowest boiling point (−161 °C).

**Ethane (C₂H₆)**, 30 g/mol: non-polar, only London dispersion. Larger surface area than methane → stronger dispersion than CH₄ (−88 °C).

**Propane (C₃H₈)**, 44 g/mol: non-polar, only London dispersion. Even larger surface area → stronger dispersion than ethane (−42 °C).

**Methanol (CH₃OH)**, 32 g/mol: polar, with O–H capable of hydrogen bonding. Hydrogen bonding is much stronger than the dispersion holding propane together, despite methanol being smaller → highest boiling point of the set (65 °C).

**Order:** CH₄ < C₂H₆ < C₃H₈ < CH₃OH. The reasoning hinges on the *type* of force, not the molar mass — methanol has hydrogen bonding, the others do not.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (1mo+)

#### Molecular orbital theory for diatomics

For diatomic molecules, MO theory combines the atomic orbitals into bonding and antibonding MOs. For homonuclear diatomics of the second period:

- **H₂ (and He₂⁺):** $\sigma_{1s}^2$; bond order 1.
- **He₂:** $\sigma_{1s}^2 \sigma^{*}_{1s}^2$; bond order 0 (does not exist under normal conditions).
- **Li₂:** $\sigma_{1s}^2 \sigma^{*}_{1s}^2 \sigma_{2s}^2$; bond order 1.
- **Be₂:** would have bond order 0 — does not exist as a stable molecule.
- **B₂, C₂, N₂:** the 2p levels split into $\sigma_{2p}$ (lower) and $\pi_{2p}$ (two degenerate, slightly higher) for B₂ and C₂; for O₂ and F₂ the order reverses. N₂ has the configuration $\sigma_{2s}^2 \sigma^{*2s}_2 \pi_{2p}^4 \sigma_{2p}^2$ and a bond order of 3.
- **O₂:** has two unpaired electrons in the $\pi^{*}_{2p}$ orbitals → **paramagnetic**; this is one of MO theory's most celebrated successes.

**Bond order** = (bonding electrons − antibonding electrons)/2. Bond order correlates with bond energy (stronger bond) and bond length (shorter bond). A bond order of 0 means no stable molecule.

#### Metallic bonding and band theory

A metallic crystal is held together by delocalised electrons moving through the lattice of cations. The **electron sea model** explains malleability, ductility, and high thermal/electrical conductivity: the electrons are free to move under an applied field and to accommodate the slip of one plane of cations past another.

**Band theory** sharpens this picture. In a metal, the valence band (filled) and the conduction band (partially filled, or overlapping with the valence band) are both available for electron motion; in an insulator, the valence band is full and a large band gap ($> 4\,\text{eV}$) separates it from the next empty band; in a semiconductor, the gap is smaller ($\sim 1\,\text{eV}$). Doping a semiconductor (e.g., adding P or B to Si) introduces donor or acceptor levels that bridge the gap and dramatically increase conductivity.

#### Coordinate bonds and complex ions

Transition metals form complex ions with ligands (Lewis bases) donating electron pairs to the metal (Lewis acid). Example: [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺ forms when Cu²⁺ is treated with excess NH₃; the deep blue-violet colour is a charge-transfer absorption.

Crystal field theory splits the d-orbitals in a ligand field:
- **Octahedral:** $t_{2g}$ (lower, 3 orbitals) and $e_g$ (higher, 2 orbitals); $\Delta_{oct}$ is the splitting energy.
- **Tetrahedral:** reverse order, with $\Delta_{tet} \approx 4/9 \Delta_{oct}$.
- **Square planar:** for d⁸ systems (Pd²⁺, Pt²⁺, Au³⁺), gives strong field splitting.

High-spin vs low-spin depends on $\Delta$ vs the pairing energy. This is rarely tested in ECAT directly, but complex-ion formation is sometimes asked in inorganic MCQs.

#### Practice prompts

1. **Geometry.** Predict the geometry, hybridisation and approximate bond angles around the central atom in: (a) ICl₄⁻, (b) ClF₃, (c) XeOF₄, (d) SO₃²⁻.
2. **Polarity.** Without calculating, decide whether each is polar or non-polar, and explain using symmetry: (a) CH₂Cl₂, (b) CCl₄, (c) cis-1,2-dichloroethene, (d) trans-1,2-dichloroethene.
3. **Ranking.** Rank the boiling points of HF, HCl, HBr, and HI, and rank the lattice energies of LiF, NaF, KF, RbF, and CsF. Justify each ranking.
4. **MO theory.** Give the bond order of O₂, O₂⁺, O₂⁻, and O₂²⁻. Which is most stable? Which is paramagnetic and which is diamagnetic?

#### Connections to adjacent topics

Chemical bonding is the foundation for **organic chemistry** (every reaction mechanism is a bond break / bond form; hybridisation explains the geometry of every intermediate), for **states of matter and solutions** (intermolecular forces decide melting/boiling points and solubility), and for **electrochemistry** (the standard electrode potentials correlate with lattice energies and ion-solvation enthalpies). A student who masters this topic removes half the uncertainty from FSc Part II chemistry.

### Common traps

- **Ignoring lone pairs when assigning hybridisation.** The SN counts both bonding and non-bonding domains; NH₃ has SN 4 (not 3) and is sp³ (not sp²), so its geometry is trigonal pyramidal with a bond angle around 107°, not 120°.
- **Conflating bond polarity and molecular polarity.** A molecule with polar bonds can be non-polar overall (CO₂, CCl₄) if the geometry cancels the bond dipoles. ECAT questions often test exactly this distinction.
- **Mixing up hydrogen bonding and covalent bonding.** The H–O, H–N, H–F bonds themselves are polar covalent; the *intermolecular* hydrogen bonding is what raises the boiling point. Students who say "water boils high because of strong H–O bonds" lose the mark.
- **Forgetting that VSEPR is about electron domains, not atoms.** A double or triple bond counts as one domain. Treating the C in CO₂ as having SN 4 (with "two double bonds = four electron pairs") is wrong — it has SN 2 and is sp hybridised.
- **Ranking lattice energy by cation charge only.** Both charge and radius matter; Mg²⁺ vs Na⁺ (Mg²⁺ wins on charge) vs K⁺ (Mg²⁺ still wins on charge, K⁺ is bigger than Na⁺). The full formula $U \propto q^+ q^- / (r^+ + r^-)$ captures it.
