# Interactions of Life — Leaving Certificate (Established) Biology Notes

Interactions of Life is the ecology and environmental biology strand of the NCCA Leaving Certificate Biology specification. It bundles the study of ecosystems (the biotic and abiotic components, the flow of energy and the cycling of nutrients), population dynamics (the factors that affect population size, growth curves, interspecific interactions), nutrient cycling (the carbon cycle, the nitrogen cycle, the water cycle), and the human impact on the biosphere (pollution, climate change, biodiversity loss, conservation and sustainability). The strand carries a weight rating of 4 out of 5 in the topic catalogue and is one of the largest discrete blocks on the SEC paper.

At Higher level, Interactions of Life extends into more detailed ecosystem analysis (the calculation of energy flow through trophic levels, the measurement of biodiversity using Simpson's index, the analysis of population growth curves), more detailed nutrient cycling (the role of decomposers, the human impact on each cycle), and more detailed conservation biology (the principles of conservation, the role of habitat preservation, the ethical framework for conservation decisions). At Ordinary level the same content is examined but the mark scheme is more forgiving on the quantitative methods and the conservation vocabulary. A candidate who can read a food web, trace a nutrient cycle and discuss the human impact on the biosphere will pick up marks across the strand and across multiple units of the paper.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Strand weight & spread**: Weight 4/5 in the topic catalogue. Marks are spread across ecosystem structure, population dynamics, nutrient cycling and human impact — under-preparing any one sub-topic depresses the whole strand.
- **Ecosystem core components**: abiotic (non-living — light, temperature, water, minerals, pH) and biotic (living — producers, consumers, decomposers). The interaction between biotic and abiotic components is what defines the ecosystem.
- **Trophic levels**: producers (autotrophs — plants, algae, some bacteria), primary consumers (herbivores), secondary consumers (carnivores that eat herbivores), tertiary consumers (carnivores that eat other carnivores), decomposers (saprotrophs — bacteria and fungi that break down dead organic matter). Energy flows through the trophic levels; only about 10% is transferred between levels.
- **Population dynamics**: population size is affected by natality (births), mortality (deaths), immigration (arrivals) and emigration (departures). Population growth follows an S-shaped (logistic) curve when resources are limited; a J-shaped (exponential) curve when resources are unlimited.
- **Nutrient cycles the strand reaches for**: the carbon cycle (photosynthesis fixes CO₂ into organic compounds; respiration releases CO₂; combustion releases CO₂; ocean-atmosphere exchange), the nitrogen cycle (nitrogen fixation by bacteria converts N₂ to ammonia; nitrification converts ammonia to nitrate; denitrification converts nitrate back to N₂).
- **Human impact on the biosphere**: pollution (air, water, soil), climate change (greenhouse effect, anthropogenic CO₂ emissions), biodiversity loss (habitat destruction, overexploitation, invasive species), conservation (preservation, restoration, sustainable use).
- **First move on an ecology question**: identify the *level* of the question (individual, population, community, ecosystem, biome) before answering. The level determines the vocabulary.
- **Food web reading**: identify the producer (the organism at the base of every food chain), trace the energy flow up the trophic levels, identify the apex predator (the organism with no natural predators in the web).

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing population and community. A population is one species; a community is multiple populations. The vocabulary distinction is a mark.
- Treating decomposers as part of the food chain rather than as a separate functional group. Decomposers break down dead organic matter; they occupy a different position in the ecosystem. The mark scheme reads for the distinction.
- Stating the 10% rule as a fixed number without explaining what it represents (energy lost between trophic levels as heat, in movement and in undigested material). The explanation is a mark; the number alone is not.
- Confusing weather and climate. Weather is short-term; climate is long-term. Climate change is a long-term shift in the statistical distribution of weather patterns.
- Treating conservation as an emotional position rather than a scientific framework. The mark scheme reads for the *principles* of conservation (preserving habitat, preventing overexploitation, restoring degraded ecosystems) and the *evidence* for biodiversity loss.

#### 30-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Can you draw a labelled food web with at least four trophic levels in under five minutes?
- [ ] Can you describe the carbon cycle and the nitrogen cycle with the role of bacteria named?
- [ ] Can you sketch a population growth curve and label the carrying capacity?
- [ ] Can you name three causes of biodiversity loss and one conservation measure for each?

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### Ecosystem structure and energy flow

The ecosystem sub-topic at Higher level extends into quantitative analysis. The mark scheme reads for:

- **Energy flow**: energy enters the ecosystem as sunlight, is fixed by producers through photosynthesis, flows through the trophic levels, and is lost at each level as heat (respiration), in movement and in undigested material. Only about 10% is transferred between levels — the 10% rule.
- **Pyramids of numbers, biomass and energy**: each can be drawn for a given ecosystem, but they have different shapes. A pyramid of numbers can be inverted (one tree supporting many insects); a pyramid of energy is always upright (energy is lost at each level).
- **Productivity**: gross primary productivity (GPP) is the total energy fixed by producers; net primary productivity (NPP) is GPP minus the energy used by producers in respiration. Standing crop biomass is the total mass of organisms at a given trophic level.
- **Decomposition**: decomposers (bacteria and fungi) break down dead organic matter, releasing nutrients back into the abiotic environment. The rate of decomposition is affected by temperature, moisture and the composition of the dead material.

A candidate who can sketch an energy-flow diagram and label the inputs (sunlight) and outputs (heat, undigested material) at each trophic level picks up the ecosystem-structure mark on any SEC paper.

#### Population dynamics

The population sub-topic at Higher level extends into growth curves and interspecific interactions. The mark scheme reads for:

- **Population growth**: J-shaped (exponential) curves occur when resources are unlimited; S-shaped (logistic) curves occur when resources are limited and the population approaches the carrying capacity (K) of the environment.
- **Limiting factors**: food supply, predation, disease, competition, climate. The carrying capacity is the maximum population size the environment can sustain indefinitely.
- **Interspecific interactions**: competition (two species compete for the same resource), predation (one species eats another), mutualism (both species benefit), parasitism (one species benefits, the other is harmed), commensalism (one species benefits, the other is unaffected).
- **Intraspecific competition**: members of the same species compete for the same resource; the result is often a stable population at or near the carrying capacity.

A candidate who can sketch both growth curves and label the carrying capacity reliably picks up the population-dynamics mark.

#### Nutrient cycling

The nutrient-cycle sub-topic is one of the most consistently tested areas of the strand. The mark scheme reads for:

- **Carbon cycle**: atmospheric CO₂ is fixed by photosynthesis into organic compounds; the organic compounds are broken down by respiration, releasing CO₂ back to the atmosphere; combustion of fossil fuels releases additional CO₂; oceans absorb and release CO₂ in a dynamic equilibrium.
- **Nitrogen cycle**: atmospheric N₂ is fixed by nitrogen-fixing bacteria (in root nodules of legumes and free-living in soil) into ammonia; ammonia is oxidised by nitrifying bacteria into nitrite and then nitrate; nitrate is absorbed by plants and used to synthesise amino acids and nucleic acids; decomposers break down dead organic matter, releasing ammonia; denitrifying bacteria convert nitrate back to atmospheric N₂.
- **Water cycle**: water evaporates from oceans, lakes and rivers; water vapour condenses to form clouds; precipitation returns water to the surface; runoff and groundwater flow return water to the oceans. Plants play a role in transpiration.

A candidate who can sketch a nutrient cycle and label the role of bacteria and other decomposers reliably picks up the cycling mark.

#### Human impact and conservation

The human-impact sub-topic is the strand's distinctive mark — the bit that asks the candidate to consider the *implications* of human activity for the biosphere. The mark scheme reads for:

- **Pollution**: air pollution (greenhouse gases, particulates, acid rain), water pollution (eutrophication from agricultural runoff, sewage, industrial effluent), soil pollution (pesticides, heavy metals, plastic).
- **Climate change**: the greenhouse effect (CO₂, methane and other gases trap infrared radiation in the atmosphere); anthropogenic emissions from fossil fuel combustion and agriculture; the consequences (rising sea levels, more extreme weather, ecosystem disruption).
- **Biodiversity loss**: habitat destruction (deforestation, urbanisation), overexploitation (overfishing, hunting), invasive species (competitors and predators that displace native species), pollution, climate change.
- **Conservation**: preservation (protecting habitats in their natural state), restoration (repairing degraded ecosystems), sustainable use (using resources at a rate that allows regeneration). Conservation measures include national parks, marine reserves, breeding programmes, international agreements (CITES, the Convention on Biological Diversity).

A candidate who can name three causes of biodiversity loss, three conservation measures, and the *principles* on which conservation is based reliably picks up the conservation mark.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Measuring biodiversity at Higher level

At Higher level, the strand extends into the quantitative measurement of biodiversity. The most common measure is **Simpson's index of diversity**:

D = 1 - Σ (n/N)²

where N is the total number of organisms and n is the number of organisms of each species. A higher value of D indicates greater diversity. The mark scheme reads for:

- **Calculating the index** from a dataset of species counts.
- **Interpreting the value** — a high D indicates a diverse community; a low D indicates a community dominated by one or a few species.
- **Discussing limitations** — Simpson's index does not account for spatial distribution, seasonal variation, or the conservation status of individual species.

A candidate who can calculate the index, interpret the value and discuss the limitations picks up the biodiversity-measurement mark.

#### The carbon cycle and climate change in detail

At Higher level, the carbon cycle is tested with the human impact in detail. The mark scheme reads for:

- **Sources of anthropogenic CO₂**: fossil fuel combustion (coal, oil, natural gas), cement production, deforestation (loss of photosynthetic carbon fixation).
- **Sinks of anthropogenic CO₂**: oceans (dissolving CO₂, forming carbonic acid — the cause of ocean acidification), forests and other vegetation (carbon storage in biomass), geological reservoirs (fossil fuel formation — but this is a slow process).
- **Feedback loops**: warming reduces the solubility of CO₂ in seawater (positive feedback); warming increases respiration by decomposers (positive feedback); warming increases photosynthesis in some ecosystems (negative feedback, but limited by other limiting factors).
- **Mitigation and adaptation**: mitigation (reducing emissions, increasing sinks — renewable energy, afforestation, carbon capture), adaptation (preparing for the consequences — sea walls, drought-resistant crops, ecosystem-based adaptation).

A candidate who can describe the cycle, the human impact and the mitigation measures picks up the climate-change mark.

#### Conservation biology in practice

At Higher level, conservation biology extends into the practical and ethical dimensions. The mark scheme reads for:

- **In-situ conservation**: preserving species in their natural habitat (national parks, marine reserves, wildlife corridors).
- **Ex-situ conservation**: preserving species outside their natural habitat (zoos, seed banks, captive breeding programmes).
- **Sustainable use**: using resources at a rate that allows regeneration (sustainable forestry, sustainable fisheries, sustainable agriculture).
- **Ethical frameworks**: utilitarian (greatest good for the greatest number), deontological (duty to preserve species), eco-centric (inherent value of nature), anthropocentric (value of nature to humans).
- **International agreements**: the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species (CITES), the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), the Ramsar Convention on Wetlands, the EU Habitats Directive.

A candidate who can name three conservation measures, describe the principles on which they are based, and discuss the ethical framework for a given conservation decision picks up the conservation mark.

#### Common mistakes that depress the grade

- **The "10% is exact" trap** — quoting the 10% rule as if it were a fixed number. The 10% rule is an *approximation*; the actual transfer efficiency varies between 5% and 20%. The mark scheme reads for the *order of magnitude* and the explanation.
- **The "decomposers are not part of the food web" trap** — treating decomposers as separate from the food web. Decomposers are a *functional group* within the ecosystem; they break down dead organic matter from all trophic levels.
- **The "climate change = warming only" trap** — forgetting that climate change involves changes in precipitation patterns, the frequency of extreme weather events, and ocean acidification, not just a rise in average temperature.
- **The "biodiversity = number of species" trap** — equating biodiversity with species richness. Biodiversity includes genetic diversity within species, species diversity, and ecosystem diversity. The mark scheme reads for all three levels.
- **The "conservation = emotion" trap** — treating conservation as a personal value rather than a scientific framework. The mark scheme reads for the *principles* and the *evidence* on which conservation decisions are based.
