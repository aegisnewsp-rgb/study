# Epidemiology and Biostatistics

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Epidemiology** quantifies the *distribution* and *determinants* of disease in populations; **biostatistics** supplies the inference tools — confidence intervals, χ², p-values — that make those numbers defensible. FMGE PSM regularly pulls 3–5 MCQs from this combined unit, so four anchors cover ~70% of the marks:

- **Incidence** counts *new* cases over person-time at risk; **prevalence** counts *all existing* cases at a point or period (Prevalence ≈ Incidence × Average duration).
- **Relative Risk (RR)** comes from cohort/RCT designs; **Odds Ratio (OR)** from case-control studies — OR only approximates RR when the outcome is rare (<10%).
- **Sensitivity vs Specificity** are test properties (fixed); **PPV/NPV** depend on the prevalence in the screened population.
- **Type I error (α)** = false positive; **Type II (β)** = false negative; **Power = 1 − β**.

Memorise the 2×2 layout (a = exposed cases, b = exposed non-cases, c = unexposed cases, d = unexposed non-cases) — almost every numeric problem reduces to that table.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Measures of Disease Frequency
**Incidence rate** tracks the *velocity* of new disease: (new cases / population at risk) × 10ⁿ, expressed per 1,000 or per 100,000 person-years. **Cumulative incidence (risk)** is the proportion of disease-free individuals who become cases within a period. **Point prevalence** is a snapshot; **period prevalence** widens the window to a defined interval.

The mnemonic *"Prevalence = Incidence × Duration"* explains why improved survival (longer duration) raises prevalence without changing incidence — a classic FMGE distractor on TB, diabetes, or chronic hepatitis.

#### Measures of Association from a 2×2 Table

| Measure | Formula | Best used in |
|---|---|---|
| Relative Risk (RR) | (a/(a+b)) ÷ (c/(c+d)) | Cohort / RCT |
| Odds Ratio (OR) | (a×d) ÷ (b×c) | Case-control |
| Attributable Risk (AR) | Iₑ − I₀ | Cohort — excess risk in exposed |
| Population AR | (Iₜ − I₀) × Pₑ | Public-health impact |
| NNT | 1 / ARR | Translating RCT into practice |

#### Study Designs and Strength of Evidence
Descending causal strength: **RCT → Cohort → Case-control → Cross-sectional → Ecological**. Matching the measure of association to the design (RR with cohort, OR with case-control) is one of the most-tested FMGE micro-skills.

#### Screening Test Validity

| Index | Formula | Depends on prevalence? |
|---|---|---|
| Sensitivity | TP/(TP+FN) | No — intrinsic |
| Specificity | TN/(TN+FP) | No — intrinsic |
| PPV | TP/(TP+FP) | **Yes** |
| NPV | TN/(TN+FN) | **Yes** |
| Youden's J | Sens + Spec − 1 | No |

> Trap: a sensitive test in a low-prevalence community can still have a poor PPV — don't quote sensitivity as if it equals PPV.

#### Hypothesis Testing
**H₀** = no effect; **H₁** = effect present. **Type I (α)** rejects a true H₀; **Type II (β)** accepts a false H₀. **Power = 1 − β** rises with larger samples, larger true effect sizes, and lower background variability.

#### Sampling Methods
Probability techniques — *simple random, systematic, stratified, cluster, multistage* — each give every member a calculable chance of selection, supporting external validity. Convenience, purposive, and snowball sampling cannot.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Bias, Confounding and Standardisation
**Selection bias** distorts who enters the study (Berkson, Neyman). **Information bias** distorts the measurement (recall bias in case-control studies of diet; interviewer bias when the enquirer knows exposure status). **Confounding** is a third variable associated with both exposure and outcome; control it at *design* (randomisation, restriction, matching) or *analysis* (stratification, multivariate regression).

When groups differ in age structure, use **direct standardisation** (apply stratum-specific rates of the study population to a standard population) or **indirect standardisation via SMR** = (Observed ÷ Expected) × 100, where Expected deaths come from applying standard rates to the study population's person-years.

#### Chi-Square and Sample Size
**χ² = Σ[(O−E)²/E]** with df = (r−1)(c−1) requires raw counts, not percentages, and expected values ≥ 5 in ≥ 80% of cells; otherwise use Fisher's exact test.

For estimating a single proportion in an infinite population:

> n = Z²α/2 × P(1−P) / d²

Z = 1.96 at 95% confidence, P = expected proportion, d = absolute precision. For finite populations apply the correction factor (N−n)/(N−1).

#### Levels of Prevention and Screening Criteria
**Primordial** prevention tackles risk factors *of* risk factors (e.g., childhood obesity policy); **primary** reduces incidence (vaccination); **secondary** catches disease early via screening; **tertiary** limits disability; **quaternary** protects from over-medicalisation.

**Wilson and Jungner's 10 principles** — the screening blueprint FMGE quotes: the condition must be an important health problem, have an accepted treatment, a recognisable latent phase, a suitable and acceptable test, and a known natural history, with cost-effectiveness and a continuous process.

#### Common Exam Pitfalls

- **CFR vs Mortality rate:** CFR = deaths/cases × 100 (severity indicator); mortality rate uses the general population denominator.
- **NNT vs ARR:** report absolute risk reduction before NNT; a "50% relative reduction" can still be clinically trivial.
- **Temporality is non-negotiable** in Hill's criteria — exposure must precede outcome, a frequent assertion-reason MCQ.
- **Confidence interval width** shrinks as √n grows; halving the width needs quadrupling the sample size.

#### Practice Prompts
1. In a cohort study, 40 of 1,000 smokers and 10 of 1,000 non-smokers develop lung cancer. Calculate RR, AR, and the number of lung cancers attributable to smoking in a population where 30% are smokers.
2. A screening test has 95% sensitivity and 90% specificity. Compare PPV when disease prevalence is 1% versus 10%, and state which prevalence condition makes mass screening worthwhile.
