# Statement and Assumptions

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

- **Definition:** A *Statement* is a given premise; an *Assumption* is an unstated, hidden belief that must be true for the statement to be logical. The **assumptions test** asks you to find the hidden presupposition.
- **The Universal Rule:** A valid assumption is one that (a) **must** be true, (b) is not contradicted by the statement, and (c) is **not** already stated or a direct conclusion drawn from it.
- **Core question to ask:** *"What does the author take for granted, without which the statement would collapse?"*
- **Exam pointer (LAT):** ~4% weightage under Analytical Reasoning. Typically appears as 3–5 MCQs where 4–5 options are given and you must pick the *implicit* belief.
- **High-yield traps:** "Should/must do" recommendations, restatements, inferences, and opinions are **not** assumptions. Assumptions describe what *is* presumed, not what *should be* done.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Anatomy of the Topic

In every Statement-and-Assumptions question, three objects exist:

| Element | Role | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Statement** | The stated premise | "Buy X brand shoes — they last longer." |
| **Assumption** | The hidden, unstated belief | "Other shoe brands do *not* last as long." |
| **Conclusion** | What is drawn from the statement | "Therefore, X brand is worth the price." |

The **assumption is presupposed**, not argued. If the author had to *prove* it, then it would not be an assumption — it would be a claim.

#### The Necessity Test

Ask: *"If this assumption were FALSE, would the statement still make sense?"* If the answer is *no*, the assumption is necessary. If the statement survives intact, the option is not an assumption — it is a red herring.

#### Elimination Heuristics

1. **Reject restatements.** Options that paraphrase the statement are conclusions, not assumptions.
2. **Reject "should/must/ought" statements.** Recommendations are actions, not presuppositions.
3. **Reject inferences.** A deduction drawn from the statement is a **conclusion**, not an assumption.
4. **Reject contradictions.** An option that conflicts with the statement can never be the assumption.
5. **Reject possibilities.** "May" or "could be" options fail the necessity test.

#### Common LAT Question Patterns

- **Advertising claims** ("Use this, it works better") — assumption: the competing option is inferior.
- **Warnings/Advisories** ("Don't swim here, it's unsafe") — assumption: people might otherwise swim there.
- **Government circulars** ("All employees must wear ID") — assumption: employees may forget or refuse otherwise.
- **Editorial recommendations** ("Read book X, it transforms thinking") — assumption: the reader is open to changing their thinking.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Subtle Distinctions

**Necessary vs. Sufficient:** An assumption is a *necessary* condition, not a sufficient one. The statement may hold even if the assumption is not the only thing true. Example: Statement — "We should abolish the death penalty." Assumption: *"The death penalty is currently in use."* The abolition can still be advocated even if other conditions hold, so the assumption is only necessary, not sufficient.

**Implicit vs. Explicit:** An assumption is **never** explicitly stated. If a word or phrase appears in the statement, it cannot be the assumption in identical wording — it must be a hidden extension.

**Should vs. Is:** This is the highest-yield trap in LAT. *"We should ban plastic"* expresses a recommendation; the assumption is *"Plastic is harmful"*, not *"We ought to act."* The first is what the author takes for granted; the second is the action they advocate.

#### Worked Micro-Example

**Statement:** *"The municipal corporation has decided to impose a night curfew in the city to curb rising crime."*

- Option A: "Crime rates have risen." — ✅ *Assumption (it must be true for the curfew to be justified).*
- Option B: "Curfews effectively reduce crime." — ❌ *A conclusion drawn from the policy, not a presupposition.*
- Option C: "Citizens should support the curfew." — ❌ *A recommendation, not a hidden belief.*
- Option D: "Crime only happens at night." — ❌ *Contradicts the framing of "rising crime" generally.*

#### Common Mistakes in LAT

1. **Marking a conclusion as an assumption** because it logically follows.
2. **Choosing an extreme option** ("only," "always," "never") when a milder version also holds — the mild version is the correct assumption.
3. **Missing the audience factor**: assumptions often depend on the **listener's behaviour** (e.g., warnings assume people would otherwise do the thing).
4. **Confusing argument with assumption**: an *argument* is a chain; an *assumption* is a single hidden link in that chain.

#### Practice Prompts

1. **Statement:** *"Private schools should reduce fees as online classes are cheaper to deliver."*
   Identify: (i) the assumption, (ii) one option that is a conclusion, and (iii) one option that is a recommendation. Which of the three is the **correct** LAT answer?
2. **Statement:** *"Do not feed the monkeys in the zoo — they may become aggressive."*
   State the assumption in one sentence, then rewrite it as a *conclusion*. Why can the rewritten version **not** be the correct option?
