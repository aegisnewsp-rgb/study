# Pacing Across a Five-Hour Examination

The ADA states plainly that the Dental Admission Test "total administration time is five hours and 15 minutes". Four sections share it: Survey of the Natural Sciences (100 items, 90 minutes), Perceptual Ability Test (90 items, 60 minutes), a scheduled 30-minute break, Reading Comprehension (50 items, 60 minutes) and Quantitative Reasoning (40 items, 45 minutes), with an optional 15-minute tutorial at the start and an optional post-test survey at the end.

That schedule tells you something no amount of content revision will. Reading Comprehension and Quantitative Reasoning come after the break, at the four-and-a-half-hour mark, and they are the two sections a candidate is most prepared for and least able to perform well on. Fatigue, not ignorance, is what caps the score for a substantial share of candidates.

## The arithmetic of the day

Per section the budget works out at roughly one item a minute or better: Natural Sciences 100 items in 90 minutes, Perceptual Ability 90 in 60, Reading 50 in 60, Quantitative 40 in 45. Those are not comfortable rates — they assume no single question takes much more than a minute.

That assumption breaks in two directions. Perceptual Ability has items where a candidate will spend three minutes on a keyhole problem, and Reading Comprehension has passages long enough that a single question can absorb a minute. Both eat into the sections that follow.

So make the pacing decision in advance, not in the moment. Decide the per-section cut-off now, write it next to the section name, and hold to it. A section left with six unanswered items because you ran out of time is a worse outcome than the same section with six items answered in the first half of the allotted minutes and guessed quickly at the end.

## Treat the break as part of the plan

The 30-minute break falls after Perceptual Ability and before Reading Comprehension.

Reset attention. If the final Perceptual Ability block was grinding, the first two Reading passages will be read badly, and that cost is larger than the last few Perceptual Ability items would have been worth. Decide in advance that the break is spent on food and water and nothing else.

Eat and hydrate. The advice is banal and it is also worth marks, because a blood-sugar drop in hour four costs more than an extra thirty minutes of revision would have bought.

## Section order, since you cannot change it

You cannot reorder the sections, so what you control is what you bring into each one.

Perceptual Ability is the section that most often breaks a candidate's rhythm. Whatever concentration is left after the Natural Sciences arrives at Perceptual Ability, and whatever remains after that arrives at Reading. Practise the whole sequence rather than the sections individually, because the transition between a spatial section and a prose section is a real cost.

Reading Comprehension is where preparation has the least effect on pace. You cannot read faster than you read. What you can do is practise abandoning a passage that is not yielding, because that decision costs five seconds and the alternative costs a whole passage.

Quantitative Reasoning is last and the shortest. It is also where a tired candidate makes unit errors, so protect it hardest — by not over-investing in Natural Sciences.

## Rehearse the length

Two full-length sittings is the minimum, and they should be back to back so the second is taken on fatigue rather than on preparation. Nothing else replicates what the fifth hour feels like.

Sit them in one block with no pause beyond the scheduled break, because a break you take when the test does not have one is one you will not get back. The rehearsal exists to find out which section you would abandon at hour four, while it is still cheap to change your mind.

## Common mistakes

Rehearsing sections individually and never the sequence. Transition costs only appear in a full sitting.

Treating the last hour as a formality. Reading and Quantitative Reasoning at four-and-a-half hours in is where unprepared candidates lose sections they could pass.

Letting one Perceptual Ability item consume three minutes. That is three minutes taken from a section you are better at.

Spending the scheduled break trying to squeeze in revision. You will be worse at both.

Answering the last items slowly because the section is nearly over. Speed at the end of a section is worth more than accuracy, because an unanswered item is a certain zero.

Not booking early. The ADA recommends contacting Prometric at least 60 to 90 days before your desired test date because schedules fill up.
