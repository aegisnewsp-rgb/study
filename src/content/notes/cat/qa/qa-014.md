# Probability

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Probability** measures how likely an event is, on a scale of 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). For finite, equally likely outcomes, P(E) = favourable ÷ total.

Two conditional-probability tools appear almost every CAT year: P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B), and Bayes' theorem, P(A|B) = P(B|A)·P(A) / P(B).

- **Classical**: P(E) = n(E) / n(Ω); valid only when every outcome is equally likely.
- **Conditional**: P(A∩B) = P(A)·P(B|A); collapses to P(A)·P(B) only if A, B are independent.
- **Counting**: nPr = n!/(n−r)!, nCr = n!/(r!(n−r)!); use with-replacement vs. without-replacement carefully.

| Term | Quick meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Sample space Ω | Set of every possible outcome |
| Mutually exclusive | A∩B = ∅, so P(A∪B) = P(A)+P(B) |
| Independent | P(A∩B) = P(A)·P(B) |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Definitions and Axioms

A **probability function** P on sample space Ω satisfies Kolmogorov's three axioms: P(E) ≥ 0, P(Ω) = 1, and countable additivity for disjoint events. The classical formula P(E) = n(E)/n(Ω) is a special case when outcomes are equally likely and Ω is finite.

#### Addition and Multiplication Rules

The **addition rule** P(A∪B) = P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B) prevents double-counting when A and B overlap. For mutually exclusive events the intersection term vanishes. The **multiplication rule** P(A∩B) = P(A)·P(B|A) handles dependent events; for independent events the conditional drops out.

#### Bayes' Theorem and Total Probability

When you know P(B|A) but need P(A|B), Bayes' theorem reverses the conditioning: P(A|B) = P(B|A)·P(A) / [P(B|A)·P(A) + P(B|A')·P(A')]. The denominator is the **total probability** of B, built by partitioning Ω through {A, A'}.

#### Counting Support

Permutations nPr count ordered selections; combinations nCr count unordered selections. CAT word problems force a choice: drawing two cards without replacement is dependent (multiply by remaining counts), while tossing a coin twice is independent (square the single-toss probability).

| Concept | Formula | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Classical P(E) | n(E)/n(Ω) | Dice, coins, equally likely draws |
| Conditional P | P(A∩B)/P(B) | "Given that B occurred…" |
| Bayes' P(A|B) | P(B|A)·P(A)/P(B) | Reverse a known conditional |
| nPr / nCr | n!/(n−r)! ; n!/[r!(n−r)!] | Arrangements vs. selections |

- Distinguish **with-replacement** (independent) from **without-replacement** (dependent) sampling.
- "At least one" problems are usually fastest via the complement 1 − P(none).
- Mutually exclusive ≠ independent: if P(A)>0 and P(B)>0 and A∩B=∅, then P(A|B)=0≠P(A).

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Worked Solution

A bag holds 5 red and 3 blue balls. Two balls are drawn without replacement. Find P(both red).
P(1st red) = 5/8. Given the first is red, 4 red remain out of 7: P(2nd red | 1st red) = 4/7.
P(both red) = (5/8)·(4/7) = 20/56 = 5/14.

If instead the question asks P(at least one red), use the complement:
P(no red) = (3/8)·(2/7) = 6/56 = 3/28, so P(at least one red) = 1 − 3/28 = 25/28.

#### Common Mistakes and Traps

- **Independent vs. mutually exclusive**: two events with positive probability cannot be both; this confusion appears in 2–3 mark CAT traps yearly.
- **Forgetting the intersection** in P(A∪B) when word problems say "A or B (or both)".
- **Wrong direction in Bayes**: IIM sets often give P(defective | machine 1) and ask P(machine 1 | defective); mixing these up is the most common losing-mark error.
- **Sampling scheme**: "selected one after another" without "replaced" means without replacement, hence dependent.

#### Expected Value and Discrete Distributions

For a discrete random variable X, E(X) = Σ xi·P(xi) and Var(X) = E(X²) − [E(X)]². The **Binomial(n, p)** model — E(X) = np, Var(X) = np(1−p) — covers repeated independent Bernoulli trials and appears in CAT word problems framed as "tossed n times" or "n independent components".

| Distribution | Mean | Variance | CAT trigger words |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bernoulli(p) | p | p(1−p) | Single success/failure |
| Binomial(n, p) | np | np(1−p) | n independent trials |
| Geometric(p) | 1/p | (1−p)/p² | Trials until first success |

1. A diagnostic test: 1% of population has disease; test has 95% true-positive and 2% false-positive rates. Compute P(disease | positive test) using Bayes — the answer surprises most students.
2. Five coins are tossed. Find P(exactly 3 heads) via Binomial(5, 0.5): C(5,3)·(0.5)⁵ = 10/32 = 5/16.

Probability contributes 1–2 CAT QA questions (about 4% of the section). Master Bayes and the with/without-replacement split, since together they account for the majority of marks tested from this topic.
