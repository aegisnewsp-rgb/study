# Chemical Kinetics

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Chemical kinetics studies **how fast** reactions proceed and the **factors** that change their speed. The average rate is measured as:

$$\text{rate} = -\frac{\Delta[\text{Reactant}]}{\Delta t} = +\frac{\Delta[\text{Product}]}{\Delta t}$$

The **rate equation** has the form rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ, where **k** is the rate constant and **m + n** gives the overall order. **Order is found by experiment**, never read off the balanced equation. For a first-order reaction, the **half-life** t₁/₂ = 0.693/k is constant, independent of starting concentration. The **Arrhenius equation** k = A·e^(–Eₐ/RT) tells you that a temperature rise sharply increases k because more molecules acquire energy ≥ **activation energy (Eₐ)**. **NABTEB pointers**: (1) expect a 2–3 mark question drawing/ interpreting concentration–time graphs, (2) the tangent method for instantaneous rate, (3) distinguishing **order** from **molecularity**.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Rate of reaction
The rate measures concentration change per unit time. For a reaction A → products, the rate can be written using any species: rate = –(1/a)Δ[A]/Δt = (1/b)Δ[B]/Δt, where the stoichiometric coefficients convert species into a common scale. NABTEB tests often require the student to compute **average rate** from tabular data or extract **instantaneous rate** from the slope of the tangent to a concentration–time curve.

#### Rate equation and order
The experimentally determined rate law is rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ. The powers **m** and **n** are the orders with respect to each reactant; their sum is the **overall order**. Orders are integers (0, 1, 2) or occasionally fractional. A **zero-order** reaction proceeds at a constant rate regardless of [A]. A **first-order** reaction halves the concentration every half-life; rate halves if [A] is halved. A **second-order** reaction: doubling [A] quadruples the rate.

#### Half-life summary

| Order | t₁/₂ expression | Depends on [A]₀? |
|-------|-----------------|------------------|
| 0 | [A]₀ / (2k) | Yes |
| 1 | 0.693 / k | No |
| 2 | 1 / (k[A]₀) | Yes |

A reaction whose successive half-lives stay constant as the reaction proceeds is **first order** — NABTEB often frames this as a multiple-completion question.

#### Collision theory and catalysis
Reacting particles must collide with **E ≥ Eₐ** and with proper **orientation**. A **catalyst** provides an alternative pathway with a lower Eₐ, increasing the fraction of successful collisions. Catalysts do **not** alter ΔH, equilibrium constant K, or final yield — they only shorten the time to reach equilibrium. NABTEB candidates frequently lose marks by claiming catalysts increase product amount.

#### Effect of temperature
Raising T shifts the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution so a larger fraction of molecules exceed Eₐ. A rough rule: a 10 °C rise **approximately doubles** the rate for many reactions near room temperature.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Arrhenius equation in detail
Plotting **ln k** against **1/T** gives a straight line of slope –Eₐ/R, allowing Eₐ to be read from experimental data. Two-temperature data let you write:

$$\ln\frac{k_2}{k_1} = \frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_1}-\frac{1}{T_2}\right)$$

A useful worked line: for a reaction with Eₐ = 50 kJ mol⁻¹, going from 300 K to 310 K gives k₂/k₁ ≈ 2.1, matching the "rule of thumb" doubling.

#### Molecularity vs order
**Molecularity** counts reacting particles in a single elementary step (unimolecular, bimolecular, termolecular). It must be a small integer and applies to a mechanism step. **Order** is empirical and applies to the overall rate law. A reaction's **rate-determining step** controls the observed order, so the slow step's stoichiometry often predicts the order — but only for mechanisms that sum to the overall equation.

#### Common mistakes NABTEB candidates make
- Reading m and n directly from stoichiometric coefficients instead of from experiment.
- Using t₁/₂ = 0.693/k for second- or zero-order reactions.
- Saying catalysts "increase the yield" or "shift the equilibrium".
- Mixing up the rate of disappearance (negative slope) with the rate equation, which is always written as a positive quantity.
- Reporting units of k wrongly: **mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹** for zero order, **s⁻¹** for first order, **L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹** for second order.

#### Adjacent connections
Kinetics links to **chemical equilibrium** (K gives position, k forward and k reverse give rates to that position), **electrochemistry** (current proportional to electron-transfer rate), and **organic chemistry** (SN1 first-order in substrate, zero-order in nucleophile; SN2 second-order overall).

#### Practice prompts
1. For A → products, [A] drops from 0.80 mol L⁻¹ to 0.20 mol L⁻¹ in 30 min. Find the average rate and, assuming first order, calculate k.
2. Eₐ = 75 kJ mol⁻¹. By what factor does the rate change when T rises from 300 K to 320 K? (Use R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.)
