# Climate of Rajasthan

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Rajasthan has a subtropical continental climate, classified largely as **arid to semi-arid**, controlled by its latitude (23°3′N–30°12′N), the **Thar Desert** in the west, and the **SW–NE trending Aravalli Range** that blocks the south-west monsoon and creates a rain-shadow in its eastern lee. Three seasons dominate: hot dry **summer (March–June, 45–50 °C)**, monsoon **(July–September, ~90% of annual rain)**, and mild **winter (October–February, 5–15 °C)**.

- **Aridity Index**: AI = P / PET (mm); values below **0.2 mark arid zones** (Jaisalmer, Barmer).
- **Rainfall gradient**: ~100 mm in Jaisalmer → ~900 mm in Bharatpur/Kota; state mean ≈ **575 mm**.
- **Winter rain** in northern districts comes from **Western Disturbances**, not the north-east monsoon.
- **Loo** = hot, dusty westerly/north-westerly summer winds from Thar causing heatstroke.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Drivers and Spatial Pattern

Rajasthan's climate is shaped by four controls: continental interior position, the Thar Desert heat low, the Aravalli orographic barrier, and the weak influence of both monsoons at this latitude. The **south-west monsoon** enters from the Arabian Sea branch, but moisture is progressively lost as winds rise over the Aravallis in the south-west, leaving western districts rain-starved. Eastern districts receive relatively higher rainfall because the moist current re-intensifies over the plains beyond the range.

#### Temperature and Rainfall

| Parameter | Western desert (Jaisalmer/Barmer) | Eastern plains (Bharatpur/Kota) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mean annual rainfall | <150 mm | 700–900 mm |
| Summer max temperature | 45–50 °C | 40–44 °C |
| Winter min temperature | 4–8 °C | 8–12 °C |
| Climatic class (Aridity Index) | <0.2 (arid) | 0.5–0.65 (dry sub-humid pockets) |
| Dominant rain source | Weak SW monsoon | SW monsoon + Western Disturbances |

#### Seasonal Phenomena

- **Loo winds (May–June)**: hot, dusty westerly/north-westerly gusts up to 30–40 km/h from the Thar, often causing heatstroke deaths.
- **Mango showers**: April–May pre-monsoon thunderstorms in the south-east (Dungarpur, Banswara, Udaipur) that aid mango flowering.
- **Western Disturbances (Nov–March)**: extratropical cyclones travelling east from the Mediterranean that give 30–60 mm winter rainfall to Ganganagar, Churu, Jhunjhunu, Alwar, and Bharatpur — critical for **rabi** crops like mustard and wheat.

#### Common Misconceptions

- Rainfall is **not uniform** — inter-annual variability CV = (σ_P / mean_P) × 100 exceeds **25%** in western districts.
- Winter rain is from **Western Disturbances**, not the north-east monsoon.
- The Aravallis run **SW–NE**, not east-west; the rain-shadow falls on their eastern slope.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Quantifying Aridity and Drought

RPSC papers frequently ask candidates to compute or interpret the **Aridity Index AI = P / PET**, where P is mean annual precipitation (mm) and PET is potential evapotranspiration (mm). For Jaisalmer (P ≈ 150 mm, PET ≈ 2200 mm), AI ≈ 0.07, classifying the district as **hyper-arid**. Bharatpur (P ≈ 700 mm, PET ≈ 1500 mm) yields AI ≈ 0.47, sitting at the **semi-arid / dry sub-humid** cusp.

The **Drought Index DI = (P − P_mean) / σ_P** is used by IMD to declare drought years. A DI below −1.0 in two consecutive weeks indicates **meteorological drought**; persistent negative values across the kharif season mark **agricultural drought**. Western Rajasthan experienced severe drought in **1987, 1999, 2002, and 2014**, each halving pearl-millet yields.

#### Climate Change and Desertification

IMD observatory trends (1980–2024) show mean temperature rising about **0.6 °C per decade** in Rajasthan, with night-time minima increasing faster than day maxima. Monsoon rainfall shows no significant trend in totals, but **intensifying variability** (more dry spells punctuated by heavy bursts). The **Indira Gandhi Canal command area** in Ganganagar and Bikaner faces **waterlogging and secondary salinisation** from over-irrigation, while **overgrazing** in Chotanagpur-style commons accelerates aeolian deflation west of the Aravallis.
