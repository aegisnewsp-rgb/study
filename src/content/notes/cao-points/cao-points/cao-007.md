# Course Points Cut-offs

A points cut-off is the lowest aggregate at which a course filled its places in a given admission year. It is published after the fact, it is specific to one course and one year, and it is the single most misused number in Irish university applications — because the thing candidates actually need is not the last number on the list but the *shape* of the list across years.

## What a cut-off table is actually telling you

The CAO publishes points history for Level 7 and Level 8 courses so that applicants can see how entry has moved. Read a single row and you learn one thing: the final aggregate in that course that year. Read four rows for the same course and you learn something usable: the direction, the volatility, and the gap.

Three patterns show up repeatedly, and each demands a different response.

**Stable.** The figures sit within a narrow band across years. Treat the range, not the single figure, as the target. A candidate planning to the midpoint of a stable band is making a defensible plan; a candidate planning to its lowest point is gambling.

**Rising.** Every year the figure is higher than the last. This is usually a demand effect — international fee-paying applicants, a subject restriction, a placement signal. A rising cut-off means the figure you remember from a school newsletter is stale, and it means your planning number should sit above the most recent one, not at it.

**Volatile.** The figures jump around. Volatility usually reflects a course whose applicant pool is small, or one whose places depend on a handful of restricted-entry decisions. For a volatile course, a cut-off is close to meaningless as a target and the only honest planning move is to treat it as a preference you would be lucky to reach rather than a line you are steering for.

## The gap between the cut-off and the offer

The final cut-off is not the same as the points at which offers were made, and the difference is the most important thing on the page.

Offers are made in rounds. In each round the CAO allocates places from the top of the list downward, and a candidate who accepts an offer removes that candidate's demand from later rounds — which frees a place that can then drop to a lower-scoring candidate. The lowest score to receive an offer in the final round is therefore frequently **below** the cut-off published for that year, and how far below depends on how many accepted offers there were earlier.

The corollary matters for planning: a candidate sitting slightly below last year's cut-off is not necessarily out of contention, and a candidate sitting slightly above it is not necessarily in. The cut-off is an output of the whole allocation process, not an input to it.

## Why the last place is decided by a random number

Where two or more candidates are tied at the points level for the last available place, the tie is broken by the random number allocated to each application — not by the order the courses were listed, not by the date the application was made, and not by any judgement about the candidate. Read topic cao-006 for the mechanism.

For planning, the implication is uncomfortable but clear: a candidate who reaches the final available place has, at that margin, an outcome determined by a lottery. Treat any plan that leaves you needing to win a tie as a plan with a real chance of failure, and hedge by holding preferences that are genuinely reachable.

## How to use the table properly

Work in this order, not the reverse.

1. **Establish your own aggregate honestly.** Model it with the Common Points Scale Calculator before you start reading other people's numbers. Predicting a cut-off without knowing what you will actually score is backwards.
2. **Take the most recent year first**, then the years before it. If the figure moved, the direction matters more than the level.
3. **Read the range, not the point.** Plan against the range.
4. **Check the course's own entry requirements separately.** A cut-off is a points number; it says nothing about subject requirements, restricted entry, or minimums (see cao-005 and cao-010). A candidate can clear the cut-off and still not be eligible for the course.
5. **Sanity-check against restricted status.** A restricted-entry course (cao-010) can carry a cut-off driven by a very different applicant pool from an identical open course at the same institution. Comparing them is a category error.

## What the table cannot tell you

It cannot tell you your own result. Leaving Certificate grades are not predictable with the precision that a points-planning exercise implies — modular exams, teacher predictions and a single bad afternoon all sit between the plan and the result. It cannot tell you what a course is like to study, whether the places on offer are the ones you want, or whether a lower-ranked course would suit you better than a higher-ranked one you do not get.

That last point is the strategic one. Cut-offs exist to help you rank your preferences accurately. They are not a target to be cleared at any cost. A candidate who reorders their list every round to chase a course they have no realistic route into will end up with worse offers than one who set an honest order and let the allocation work.

> Pull the current points history from cao.ie before you model anything. Course cut-offs are published per admission year and the page carries courses that open, close and change their entry requirements between cycles.

## Self-check before you close the page

1. A course shows cut-offs of 512, 540, 531 and 566 across four years. What do you plan against, and why not the last figure? *(The range and the direction. The trend is upward and the spread is wide, so a single figure — especially the most recent — is a poor planning number.)*
2. A candidate receives an offer at points below the published cut-off for that course. How? *(Earlier-round acceptances removed candidates from the demand pool, freeing places that dropped to lower-scoring candidates in later rounds. The cut-off is the final round's closing figure, not the opening threshold.)*
3. Two candidates are tied on points for the last available place. What decides it, and what does *not*? *(The random number allocated to the application. Not the order of preference and not the application date — see cao-006.)*
4. A course's cut-off is comfortably below your aggregate. Is your application safe? *(No. Subject requirements, restricted-entry conditions and the minimum requirements are separate filters. Clear the cut-off and you are eligible, not placed.)*
5. Why is a rising cut-off often treated as a demand signal rather than a syllabus difficulty? *(Because course difficulty does not change year to year, while applicant composition does — fee-paying numbers, restrictions, graduate entry and placement signals all move the pool.)*
