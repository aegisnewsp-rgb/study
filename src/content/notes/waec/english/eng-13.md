# Conditional Sentences

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **conditional sentence** links a condition (the **if-clause**, also called the *protasis*) with a consequence (the **main clause**, or *apodosis*). WAEC WASSCE Paper 2 (Theory/Objective) tests conditionals chiefly under *Lexis and Structure* (Section A) — expect 1–2 items in 50 multiple-choice questions, often an error-spotting stem such as *"If he **was** here, he **would help** us."*

The four core types:

- **Type 0** — *If* + Simple Present, Simple Present (general truths: *If ice melts, it becomes water.*).
- **Type 1** — *If* + Simple Present, *will/can/may/imperative* (real/likely future: *If it rains, we will stay indoors.*).
- **Type 2** — *If* + Simple Past, *would/could/might* (unreal present/future: *If I **were** rich, I would travel.*).
- **Type 3** — *If* + Past Perfect, *would/could/might have + past participle* (unreal past: *If she had studied, she would have passed.*).

**Key trap:** never put *will* inside the if-clause of Type 1.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Anatomy of a Conditional
Every conditional carries two parts: the **conditional clause** introduced by *if* (the condition) and the **main clause** (the consequence). In *If you study hard, you will pass*, the first half is the condition; the second half is the result. The verb tense pairing — not the word *if* alone — decides the type.

#### The Four Types at a Glance

| Type | If-clause | Main clause | Meaning |
|------|-----------|-------------|---------|
| 0 | Simple Present | Simple Present | Laws, habits, facts |
| 1 | Simple Present | *will/can/may/imperative* | Real/likely future |
| 2 | Simple Past | *would/could/might* | Unreal/hypothetical now or future |
| 3 | Past Perfect | *would/could/might have + V3* | Unreal past |

#### Why the Tense Pairing Matters
The if-clause of **Type 1** stays in the Simple Present even when the action is future (*If he **comes** tomorrow…*). Adding *will* here is the single most common WAEC error: *"If it **will rain** tomorrow, we will cancel."* → wrong. **Type 2** uses the past to signal unreality, so *If I **was*** is technically loose; the formal subjunctive is ***If I were*** for all subjects. **Type 3** pushes the unreal condition back in time — both the condition and its imagined result are now impossible.

#### Substitutes for *if*
*Unless* means *if … not*. *Provided that*, *as long as*, and *on condition that* carry a sense of requirement. *Even if* concedes the condition. *In case* speaks of preparation, not causation (*Take an umbrella **in case** it rains* — different from *…if it rains*).

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Mixed Conditionals
WAEC occasionally combines time frames. **Past cause → present result:** *If she had taken the medicine, she would be alive now* (Past Perfect in the if-clause, *would* + base verb in the main clause). **Present state → past result:** *If I were braver, I would have challenged him* (Simple Past in the if-clause, *would have* + past participle in the main clause). Recognising which half is real and which half is imagined is the diagnostic step.

#### Inversion (Formal Style)
In written or formal English, *if* can be dropped by moving the auxiliary in front of the subject:
- *Were* I you, I would apologise. (← If I were you…)
- *Had* we arrived earlier, we would have seen him. (← If we had arrived…)
- *Should* the rain stop, we shall leave. (← If the rain should stop…)

This inversion is rare in WAEC passages but appears in summary/comprehension cloze items.

#### Common Mistakes to Avoid
1. Using *will* in the if-clause of Type 1.
2. Mixing tenses within a single type (*If he would have come, he would see her*).
3. Writing *If I was* instead of the subjunctive *If I were* for hypothetical cases.
4. Confusing *if* with *in case* — the latter presupposes the event may or may not happen.
5. Using *would* in the if-clause of any real conditional.

#### Practice Prompts
1. Rewrite correctly: *"If he will come early, we will start the meeting."* Identify the type and correct tense.
2. Convert *If I had known about the test, I would have prepared* into formal inverted form beginning with *Had*.
