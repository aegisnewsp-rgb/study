# Fluid Mechanics

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Fluid mechanics studies **liquids and gases** under static (fluid statics) and moving (fluid dynamics) conditions. For JEE Advanced, focus on **Pascal's law**, **Archimedes' principle**, the **equation of continuity**, **Bernoulli's theorem**, **Stokes' law** with terminal velocity, and capillarity from surface tension.

Key formulas to memorise:

- **Hydrostatic pressure:** P = ρgh, where ρ is fluid density, g is 9.8 m/s², h is depth below the free surface.
- **Continuity:** A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ for incompressible steady flow through varying cross-section.
- **Bernoulli:** P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant along a streamline.
- **Stokes' drag:** F = 6πηrv for a sphere of radius r moving at speed v through fluid of viscosity η.

Most-tested traps: applying Bernoulli across different streamtubes, using ΔP = 2T/r for a soap bubble (correct value is 4T/r), and forgetting that buoyant force equals the weight of *displaced* fluid, not the immersed body.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Fluid Statics

Pressure at depth h below a free surface of density ρ is **P = P₀ + ρgh**, where P₀ is atmospheric pressure (~1.013 × 10⁵ Pa). **Pascal's law** states that pressure applied to an enclosed incompressible fluid is transmitted undiminished in all directions, which is the operating principle of hydraulic lifts and hydraulic brakes.

**Archimedes' principle** gives buoyant force as **F_b = ρ_fluid · V_submerged · g**, directed upward. An object floats when F_b equals its weight; it sinks when weight exceeds F_b. In a fluid accelerating with acceleration **a**, the effective gravity becomes √(g² + a²), altering apparent weight.

#### Fluid Dynamics

For a steady, incompressible, non-viscous flow, **mass conservation** between two cross-sections of a tube gives A₁v₁ = A₂v₂. Applying the **work-energy theorem** to a fluid element moving along a streamline yields Bernoulli's equation, where each term has units of pressure:

| Term | Physical meaning |
| --- | --- |
| P | Static pressure at the point (Pa) |
| ½ρv² | Dynamic pressure from bulk flow |
| ρgh | Hydrostatic contribution from elevation |

Practical applications include the **venturimeter** (flow rate Q = A₂√[2(P₁ − P₂)/{ρ(1 − (A₂/A₁)²)}]), the **Pitot tube** for aircraft airspeed, the **atomizer/spray**, and dynamic lift on aerofoils.

#### Viscous Flow

Newton's law of viscosity gives shear stress **τ = η(dv/dx)**, where η is the dynamic viscosity coefficient measured in Pa·s. The **Reynolds number Re = ρvD/η** distinguishes laminar flow (Re < ~1000 in pipes) from turbulent flow. For laminar flow in a circular pipe, **Poiseuille's equation** gives pressure drop ΔP = 32ηLv/D².

A sphere falling under gravity reaches **terminal velocity** when viscous drag balances net gravity:

- v_t = (2r²(ρ − σ)g) / (9η), where ρ is sphere density and σ is fluid density.

#### Surface Tension and Capillarity

Surface tension T (N/m) represents excess energy per unit area. A liquid drop has excess pressure **ΔP = 2T/r**, while a soap bubble (two surfaces) has **ΔP = 4T/r**. Capillary rise is given by Jurin's law: **h = 2T cosθ / (ρgr)**, where θ is the contact angle between liquid and tube wall.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Derivation of Bernoulli's Equation

Consider an incompressible fluid element of volume dV moving from region 1 to region 2 along a streamline. The work done by pressure forces minus the change in potential and kinetic energy equals zero (work-energy theorem with conservative forces). Substituting dV = A·ds and applying continuity A₁ds₁ = A₂ds₂ produces:

- P₁ + ½ρv₁² + ρgh₁ = P₂ + ½ρv₂² + ρgh₂

This holds only along a single streamline, only for steady flow, and only when viscous dissipation and compressibility are negligible. Applying it across different streamlines, or in turbulent regions, is a classic JEE Advanced trap.

#### Worked Example — Venturimeter

Water (ρ = 1000 kg/m³) flows through a horizontal venturimeter whose inlet area is A₁ = 20 cm² and throat area is A₂ = 5 cm². A U-tube manometer shows a 10 cm height difference of mercury (σ = 13600 kg/m³). Find the volume flow rate.

Solution: Pressure difference ΔP = (ρ_Hg − ρ_water) · g · h = (13600 − 1000) · 9.8 · 0.10 ≈ 12348 Pa. Using Bernoulli with h₁ = h₂:

- v₂² − v₁² = 2ΔP/ρ
- By continuity v₁ = (A₂/A₁)v₂ = v₂/4, so v₂²(1 − 1/16) = 2ΔP/ρ
- v₂² · (15/16) = 2 · 12348 / 1000 = 24.696
- v₂ ≈ 5.13 m/s, Q = A₂v₂ = 5 × 10⁻⁴ · 5.13 ≈ 2.56 × 10⁻³ m³/s

#### Common Mistakes Table

| Mistake | Correction |
| --- | --- |
| Buoyant force = weight of body | Buoyant force = weight of *displaced* fluid |
| ΔP = 2T/r for bubble | Bubble has two surfaces, use ΔP = 4T/r |
| Bernoulli across streamlines | Valid only along a single streamline |
| Continuity uses diameter | Continuity uses cross-sectional *area* |
| Stokes' drag in poise | Convert η to SI: 1 poise = 0.1 Pa·s |

#### Exam Strategy

JEE Advanced typically sets 1–2 fluid mechanics questions, often merged with the Mechanics (~4%) quota. Expect **single-correct MCQs** on Bernoulli with venturimeter, **integer-type** numerics on capillarity or terminal velocity, and rare **multi-correct** items pairing buoyancy with viscosity. Memorise derivations, keep SI units consistent, and verify ideal-fluid assumptions before applying Bernoulli.

Practice prompts:

1. Derive the terminal velocity of a raindrop falling through air, given η_air and radius r. State the Reynolds number regime you assumed.
2. A U-tube contains mercury; water is poured in one limb. Find the height difference and explain using Pascal's law why the system reaches equilibrium.
