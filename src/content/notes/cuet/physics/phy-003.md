# Motion in 2D

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

Two-dimensional kinematics analyzes motion where position changes along two independent orthogonal axes simultaneously. By Galileo's principle of superposition, horizontal and vertical components evolve independently without mutual interference.

For a projectile launched from ground level with speed $ at angle $\theta$ above the horizontal:
- Initial velocity components:  = u\cos\theta$ (constant, as  = 0$),  = u\sin\theta$ (constant downward acceleration  = -g$).
- Time of flight:
1166557T = \frac{2u\sin\theta}{g}1166557
- Maximum vertical height:
1166557H = \frac{u^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}1166557
- Horizontal range:
1166557R = \frac{u^2\sin 2\theta}{g}1166557

Key geometric symmetry: complementary launch angles ($\theta$ and 0^\circ - \theta$) produce identical horizontal ranges for the same initial launch speed. Maximum range occurs strictly at $\theta = 45^\circ$, where {\max} = \frac{u^2}{g}$.

In uniform circular motion of radius $ at tangential speed $, the magnitude of velocity remains constant while its direction rotates continuously. The radial centripetal acceleration pulls inward toward the center:
1166557a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r1166557

⚡ **CUET UG Exam Trap:** At the absolute peak of a parabolic flight, speed does not equal zero. The vertical component drops to zero ( = 0$), but the horizontal component remains fully intact ( = u\cos\theta$). The acceleration at the peak remains exactly $ directed downward.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

CUET UG questions in two-dimensional kinematics emphasize projectile trajectory equations, vector relative velocity, and circular turning dynamics.

#### 1. Parabolic Trajectory Formulation

Eliminate time $ from horizontal displacement  = (u\cos\theta) t$ into vertical displacement  = (u\sin\theta) t - \frac{1}{2}g t^2$:
1166557y = x\tan\theta - \frac{g x^2}{2u^2\cos^2\theta} = x\tan\theta\left(1 - \frac{x}{R}\right)1166557

The factored form  = x\tan\theta(1 - x/R)$ simplifies CUET objective problems where a projectile clears obstacles of known height at specific horizontal coordinates.

#### 3. Step-by-Step Worked Example: Parabolic Obstacle Problem

**Problem:** A football is kicked from ground level at an elevation angle of 5^\circ$. It passes over a defensive wall of height \text{ m}$ located 2\text{ m}$ away horizontally and lands on the pitch 0\text{ m}$ from the kick point. Calculate the initial launch speed $ and verify whether the ball clears the crossbar if the goalpost is located at 8\text{ m}$ with height .44\text{ m}$ (take  = 10\text{ m/s}^2$).

**Solution:**
1. Identify given boundary conditions: launch angle $\theta = 45^\circ$, horizontal range  = 20\text{ m}$.
2. Apply the factored trajectory relation:
1166557y = x\tan\theta\left(1 - \frac{x}{R}\right)1166557
3. Substitute  = 12\text{ m}$ and  = 3\text{ m}$ to verify consistency:
1166557y(12) = 12\tan(45^\circ)\left(1 - \frac{12}{20}\right) = 12(1)\left(\frac{8}{20}\right) = 4.8\text{ m}1166557
Because .8\text{ m} > 3.0\text{ m}$, the ball easily clears the wall with .8\text{ m}$ of clearance.
4. Calculate initial launch speed $ from the range formula:
1166557R = \frac{u^2\sin(2\theta)}{g} \implies 20 = \frac{u^2\sin(90^\circ)}{10} \implies u^2 = 200 \implies u = 10\sqrt{2}\text{ m/s} \approx 14.14\text{ m/s}1166557
5. Evaluate height at the goal line ( = 18\text{ m}$):
1166557y(18) = 18\tan(45^\circ)\left(1 - \frac{18}{20}\right) = 18(1)\left(\frac{2}{20}\right) = 1.8\text{ m}1166557
Because .8\text{ m} < 2.44\text{ m}$, the ball passes beneath the crossbar, scoring inside the goal frame.

#### 2. Dynamics of Circular Curves and Road Banking

When a vehicle negotiates a turn of radius $ on a level road, static friction provides the sole centripetal acceleration:
1166557f_s \le \mu_s N = \mu_s mg \implies \frac{mv^2}{r} \le \mu_s mg \implies v_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu_s rg}1166557

To eliminate reliance on lateral tyre friction, highways and tracks bank their surface at angle $\theta$. Resolving normal force $ and weight $:
1166557N\sin\theta = \frac{mv^2}{r}, \quad N\cos\theta = mg \implies \tan\theta = \frac{v_0^2}{rg}1166557
where  = \sqrt{rg\tan\theta}$ is the rated design speed.

If the vehicle travels above design speed ( > v_0$), friction acts down the incline to prevent skidding outward:
1166557v_{\max} = \sqrt{rg\left(\frac{\tan\theta + \mu_s}{1 - \mu_s\tan\theta}\right)}1166557

If the vehicle travels below design speed ( < v_0$), friction acts up the incline to prevent slipping inward:
1166557v_{\min} = \sqrt{rg\left(\frac{\tan\theta - \mu_s}{1 + \mu_s\tan\theta}\right)}1166557

#### 3. Step-by-Step Worked Example: Rain and Moving Commuter

**Problem:** Raindrops fall vertically downward with a terminal speed of 0\text{ m/s}$. A cyclist rides horizontally due East at 0\text{ m/s}$. At what angle to the vertical must the cyclist hold an umbrella to stay dry?

**Solution:**
1. Express rain velocity relative to ground: $\vec{v}_R = -30\hat{j}$.
2. Express cyclist velocity relative to ground: $\vec{v}_C = 10\hat{i}$.
3. Calculate relative velocity of rain with respect to the cyclist:
1166557\vec{v}_{R/C} = \vec{v}_R - \vec{v}_C = -10\hat{i} - 30\hat{j}1166557
4. The apparent path of raindrops is tilted toward the cyclist. Let $\theta$ be the angle with the vertical:
1166557\tan\theta = \frac{|v_x|}{|v_y|} = \frac{10}{30} = \frac{1}{3}1166557
1166557\theta = \arctan\left(\frac{1}{3}\right) \approx 18.4^\circ1166557
5. The cyclist must tilt the umbrella 8.4^\circ$ toward the East (into the oncoming wind) to shield against the slanted relative rain stream.
