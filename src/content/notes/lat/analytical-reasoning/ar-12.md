# Puzzles and Ranking

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Puzzles and Ranking** form the heaviest cluster in LAT Analytical Reasoning, typically accounting for 12–16 of the 20 AR questions. The task is to fix the order of named entities along a line, a circle, floors of a building, days of a week, or rows of a grid, using qualitative clues.

**Must-know combinatorial baselines:** n distinct people in a row give n! arrangements; n people around a round table give (n−1)! distinct orders once rotation is normalised; (n−1) independent comparison clues are the minimum needed to fully fix a ranking of n items. LAT favours **linear seating**, **circular tables**, **floor-building** and **scheduling** set-ups of 5–7 entities with 4–5 clues, followed by 3–4 linked MCQs.

**Three high-yield pointers:** (1) convert every clue into a direct **A → B** relation before drawing; (2) flag every **floating variable** because LAT specifically tests unfixed positions; (3) always evaluate **Maximum and Minimum** position cases — they appear in at least one MCQ per set.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Concept

A Puzzles and Ranking problem gives you **entities** (people, places, days, floors) and **constraints** (relative-position statements). Your job is to deduce a unique order, or, when uniqueness is impossible, to enumerate every consistent case. The defining LAT skill is the disciplined translation of English sentences into directed relations, then into a diagram.

#### Types of Arrangements Tested

- **Linear seating (single row):** 5–7 people in a row of chairs; clues such as *A sits to the left of B* or *C is at an extreme end*.
- **Circular arrangement:** 6–8 people around a table; rotational symmetry is killed by fixing one person's seat, after which (n−1)! orders remain.
- **Floor / building puzzles:** distinct floors with constraints on who lives above, below, or on the same floor as whom.
- **Day-based scheduling:** Monday → Sunday, treated as a **linear** sequence, not cyclic.
- **Grid arrangement:** two parallel rows facing each other or facing north/south; column alignment is a hidden second constraint.
- **Pure ranking:** items ordered by height, age, marks, or seniority; solved with pairwise comparisons.

#### Clue Taxonomy

| Clue type | Example | What it yields |
|---|---|---|
| Direct positional | "A is at the extreme left" | Fixed slot |
| Comparative | "C is taller than D" | Inequality chain |
| Conditional (if–then) | "If A is first, then B is last" | Branching case |
| Negative | "E is not adjacent to F" | Eliminates slot pairs |
| Either–or | "Either A or B sits at the right end" | Dual case to test both |

#### Solving Procedure

1. **Extract variables and slots.** Count entities, count positions, note whether the frame is linear or circular.
2. **Convert each clue to a directed arrow** A → B (A before/left-of/below B) or a forbidden pair.
3. **Anchor the most specific clue first** — any clue pinning a person to an extreme, corner, or fixed reference point.
4. **Build a chain** of definite relations; float the rest.
5. **Test negative and conditional clues** as filters on the partial order.
6. **Enumerate cases** whenever two clues are mutually compatible but not jointly sufficient — LAT questions probe both branches.

#### Typical LAT Question Patterns

- *"Which of the following must be true?"* — answer is invariant across all cases.
- *"Who could be at the third position?"* — appears in at least one valid arrangement.
- *"Who cannot occupy X?"* — eliminated in every branch.
- *"If P is fixed at an extreme, how many arrangements exist?"* — apply permutations to the remaining slots.
- *"What is the maximum number of people who can sit between M and N?"* — extreme-case reasoning.

#### Worked Micro-Example (Linear, 5 people)

Clues: (i) A is at the left end. (ii) B is immediately to the right of C. (iii) D is to the right of B. (iv) E is not at the right end.

Fixing A at position 1. B and C occupy a consecutive pair in slots {2,3}, {3,4}, or {4,5}. D must be to the right of B, so the pair sits at {2,3} (giving B=2, C=3, D ∈ {4,5}) or {3,4} (giving B=3, C=4, D=5). The {4,5} pair is invalid because D cannot sit right of B=4 in slot 5. E is then placed in the only remaining slot, and clause (iv) is checked. Two valid orders survive, demonstrating why LAT asks *could-be* and *must-be* questions rather than expecting a single answer.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Hidden Constraints

- **Same-row grids with a fixed column** — clues like *"P and Q sit in the same column"* must be tracked separately from row clues; treating the grid as two independent rows loses marks.
- **Facing-centre vs facing-outside circles** — left/right reverses between the two orientations; a clue that says *"A is to the immediate left of B"* means different things at the two setups.
- **Multi-floor buildings with vacant floors** — when a 7-person problem is set across 8 floors, one floor stays empty; the empty slot can float, so don't assume every floor is occupied.
- **Conditional chains** — *"If A is on floor 3, then B is on floor 5; if B is on floor 5, then C is on floor 1"* allows you to transit through B even when A's position is not yet fixed.

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics

Puzzles share logical machinery with **Blood Relations** (transitive reasoning: A is B's brother, B is C's father ⇒ A is C's uncle) and with **Syllogisms** (if-then elimination). Ranking questions also use the same comparison-graph approach as **Scheduling** problems. Mastering direct-relation notation therefore feeds two or three other LAT areas.

#### Common Traps in the Exam Hall

- **Rotation misread in circular sets** — counting (n−1)! cases as n! inflates the count by a factor of n and costs the "how many arrangements" question.
- **"Immediately" ignored** — *immediately to the left* is stricter than *somewhere to the left*; a question that toggles the word changes the answer.
- **Negative clues underused** — *"M is not adjacent to N"* rules out only two slots per person, but in a tight set it can eliminate an entire branch.
- **Floating variables forced into a slot** — a person with no incoming or outgoing arrow can sit anywhere left; do not pin them prematurely.
- **Maximum-Minimum skipped** — LAT explicitly tests "What is the maximum possible value of X?"; without enumerating extremes you cannot answer.

#### Exam Strategy and Weightage

Puzzles and Ranking routinely contribute 60–80% of the AR section in LAT. Within a 20-question paper, expect **four or five standalone sets of four to five questions each**, plus one or two isolated ranking items. The recommended split is roughly **8–10 minutes per set** (1.5–2 minutes per MCQ), leaving a 2-minute buffer for the trickiest Maximum-Minimum sub-question.

#### Two Practice Prompts

1. *Seven people A, B, C, D, E, F, G sit in a row. A is not at either end. B sits immediately to the right of D. C sits third from the left. E is between F and G, and F is to the left of E. How many valid arrangements are possible, and which person must occupy the centre (4th) seat?* (Anchor C at slot 3; use the F–E–G chain to fill slots 4–6; test both directions; count.)
2. *Six friends sit around a circular table facing the centre. P is opposite R. Q is to the immediate left of P. S is neither adjacent to P nor opposite Q. T sits between R and U. If P is fixed at the 12 o'clock position, determine the unique seating order, then answer: who is to the immediate right of U, and which seat is impossible for S?* (Fix P, place R opposite, place Q, then eliminate S's forbidden seats; the remaining slot is forced.)
