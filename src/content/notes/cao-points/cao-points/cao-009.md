# Offer Acceptances and the Allocation Cycle

An offer is not an offer of a place. It is an offer of a place *if everybody above you declines it*. That single sentence is the whole of this topic, and almost every mistake candidates make with CAO offers comes from forgetting it.

## What an offer actually is

When the CAO allocates, it works down each course's ranked list and offers places until the places run out. The offers that go out in Round 1 are provisional offers of places that may still be refused. They become firm only when the accepting candidate's acceptance is recorded before the course's acceptance deadline for that round.

That produces the central asymmetry: **an offer tells you where you stood when it was made. It does not tell you where you stand after.** Every acceptance in Round 1 moves one course's Round 2 threshold down, potentially far down. A candidate who held a Round 1 offer at a given course and then watched it become a Round 2 offer at a much lower number is not having something go wrong; that is the system working normally.

## The decision you are actually making

When a Round 1 offer arrives you are choosing between two things, not between "accept" and "reject":

- **A place at this course**, secured by accepting, with the acceptance deadline fixed.
- **A different, better outcome later**, which depends on Round 2 shifting places downward across the whole system.

Both are real. Neither is guaranteed. The comparison is between a certain outcome and an uncertain one, and the discipline is to make the certain-versus-uncertain trade explicitly rather than letting anxiety make it for you.

Three questions decide it in practice.

**How large is the gap between the offer and your expectation?** If the offer is at or above what you expected, the uncertainty is small and accepting is correct. If the offer is far below your expectation, you are being asked to give up a substantial part of your likely result for a later round that may not deliver.

**How many courses are below you on the list that you would prefer?** If Round 2 has a realistic chance of improving your position, you have a decision to make. If your preferred courses were all above you on the Round 1 list and have now been passed, Round 2 has little to offer you.

**What does the course actually offer?** Some courses are genuinely good fits at modest points. A realistic offer from a course you would be glad to attend can be worth more than an unreached ambition, and only you can weigh that. The CAO will not do it for you.

## Declining, and what it costs

Declining a Round 1 offer removes you from that course's demand for the following round. Your application continues: you remain in the pool for every course on your list that you are still entitled to, and your random number and your points are unchanged. Declining is not a penalty and it does not damage the rest of your application.

What declining does change is the arithmetic for everyone else — including, occasionally, you. Accepted Round 1 places release places that fall to lower-scoring candidates in Round 2. A candidate who declines a good offer to chase a better one is betting that the better one will materialise; if it does not, the fallback in Round 2 is the course the first offer came from, at a points level you may no longer clear.

## The counter-intuitive case for declining

The advice "always accept your Round 1 offer" is wrong in a specific and predictable situation: when a candidate holds a Round 1 offer at a course they do not want, while the courses they do want are crowded above them. In that situation, the accepting population in Round 2 is likely to be smaller than in Round 1, so thresholds drop, and declining is a rational bet.

The advice "always decline and wait" is wrong in the mirror situation: when your Round 1 offer is at a course you would be happy to attend and the courses above you are not going to fall to you.

## Acceptance deadlines are absolute

Acceptance is recorded on a date set for the round. A deadline passed is a deadline passed — there is no grace period and no appeal, and an unaccepted offer simply lapses. This is the one area of the process where a single mis-managed evening costs a full admission year.

Practically: put the deadline in a calendar with a reminder a week earlier. Do not rely on remembering it. Do not assume the deadline is the same as the date the results or the offer arrived.

## What you cannot do

- You cannot accept two offers in the same round.
- You cannot accept after the deadline and expect it to count.
- You cannot add a course to your list and expect it to be considered retroactively for a round already processed.
- You cannot negotiate. The CAO does not run a waiting list and does not reopen places on request.

## Self-check before you close the page

1. A Round 1 offer arrives at a course you would be genuinely happy to attend. Why is "accept immediately" right here? *(Because the offer is provisional and can be displaced by later acceptances. Once you accept, the place is held for you and the outcome is certain.)*
2. You decline your Round 1 offer to chase a course above you. What has to be true for that to pay off? *(That the threshold at the higher course falls in a later round — which depends on acceptances elsewhere being high enough. If it does not fall far enough, you have surrendered a real offer and the fallback may no longer be reachable at your points.)*
3. Does declining penalise your application in later rounds? *(No. Points and random number are unchanged and you remain in the pool for every course on your list. It changes the system arithmetic, not your standing.)*
4. The acceptance deadline is a Tuesday. You are certain you can act Wednesday. What happens? *(The offer lapses. Deadlines are absolute; there is no grace period.)*
5. Why is a Round 1 offer a weaker guarantee than a Round 2 acceptance? *(In Round 1 the offers going out at the same course are competing against each other for the same places, and only accepted offers become firm. By a later round the accepting population is known and the place is secured the moment your acceptance is recorded.)*
