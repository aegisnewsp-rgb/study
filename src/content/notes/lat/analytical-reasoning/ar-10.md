# Critical Reasoning

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Critical Reasoning** is the disciplined evaluation of arguments — weighing premises against conclusions, exposing hidden assumptions, and separating valid inferences from logical fallacies. On the **LAT (Law Admission Test)** conducted by HEC Pakistan, this skill lives inside the **Analytical Reasoning** section (5% of paper weight, but a high-yield scoring component because passage-based MCQs reward careful reasoning more than rote memory). Every argument on the test has a skeleton: a **premise** (evidence), an **assumption** (unstated bridge), and a **conclusion** (the claim). The question types reduce to four moves — find the **main point**, spot the **necessary assumption**, **strengthen** or **weaken** the link, or **identify the fallacy**.

| Term | Definition | LAT Signal Word |
|---|---|---|
| Premise | Evidence offered in support of a conclusion | "because", "since", "given that" |
| Conclusion | The claim the argument tries to prove | "therefore", "thus", "hence" |
| Assumption | Unstated link without which the conclusion collapses | "must be true", "depends on" |
| Inference | What logically follows from the passage | "it can be concluded that" |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **PACE-FS** — **P**remise → **A**ssumption → **C**onclusion → **E**vidence-weight → **F**allacy → **S**trengthener/Weakener. When you read a passage, tag each sentence with one of these six labels in pencil; the question almost always maps to one of them.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Anatomy of an LAT Argument

A passage in standard papers presents 4–8 sentences arguing for one main point. The first sentence is rarely the conclusion — it is in most keys a *premise* or *context*. The conclusion in most keys sits at the end or is signalled by summary words ("clearly", "the real lesson is"). **Implied conclusions** never appear word-for-word; you must synthesise them from repeated ideas. **Necessary assumptions** are the load-bearing premises: remove one and the argument falls. **Sufficient assumptions** are merely helpful — they boost the argument but are not strictly required.

#### Strengthen vs Weaken — Direction-of-Effect

A **strengthener (booster)** adds new evidence that pushes the conclusion closer to certainty. A **weakener (underminer)** introduces an alternative explanation, a counter-example, or a factor that breaks the causal chain. The examiner's favourite trap is reversing the direction: students pick the booster when "weaken" is asked, or vice versa. Always read the question stem twice and underline the verb.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Question Type | What the Answer Must Do | Typical Stem Phrasing |
|---|---|---|
| Main Point | Restate the author's central claim in different words | "Which one is the main conclusion?" |
| Necessary Assumption | State what the argument cannot live without | "The argument depends on which assumption?" |
| Strengthen | Make the conclusion more probable | "Which would most strengthen?" |
| Weaken | Introduce doubt or alternative cause | "Which would most weaken?" |
| Inference | Be defensibly drawn but not guaranteed | "Which follows logically?" |
| Flaw | Identify the specific fallacy committed | "The reasoning is flawed because…" |

#### The Seven Fallacies Tested on LAT

1. **Ad Hominem** — attacking the person, not the argument.
2. **Straw Man** — distorting the opposing view to make it easier to refute.
3. **Circular Reasoning** (begging the question) — the conclusion is smuggled into the premise.
4. **False Cause** (*post hoc*) — assuming sequence proves causation.
5. **Hasty Generalisation** — drawing a universal rule from one or two cases.
6. **Slippery Slope** — claiming one step inevitably triggers a chain reaction.
7. **Red Herring** — introducing an irrelevant but emotionally appealing distraction.

#### Syllogism and Quantifier Watch

In syllogistic reasoning, watch quantifier words: **all, most, some, none, only**. A valid chain like "All A are B; All B are C; therefore All A are C" holds, but shifting a quantifier ("All A are B; Some B are C; therefore All A are C") is the **illicit minor** fallacy. The same shift breaks **causal claims** — *"most people who drank the water got sick"* does **not** prove the water caused the sickness.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

#### Question:
*A university dean claims that because students who use the new tutoring centre score 15% higher on practice tests, the centre is the cause of improved performance. Which of the following, if true, most weakens the dean's argument?*
(A) Students who visit the tutoring centre also report studying more hours than those who do not.
(B) The new centre employs tutors who scored in the top decile on their own entrance tests.
(C) Practice-test scores do not correlate with final-exam scores in this programme.
(D) Most students using the centre had below-average entrance scores to begin with.

#### Solution:
This is a **causal reasoning** argument (X happened, so X caused Y). To weaken it, we need an **alternative explanation** for the improved scores — that is, another variable correlated with centre use that can be the real cause.

- **(A)** introduces exactly such a variable: increased study hours. If centre-users also study more, the centre is not necessarily the cause — the extra hours can be. ✔
- **(B)** describes tutor quality, which would *strengthen*, not weaken.
- **(C)** challenges test validity, but the dean's claim is about the centre's effect on the specific practice-test measure, so this misses the causal link.
- **(D)** actually *strengthens* by showing the gains came from a lower baseline — better candidates for the centre.

Answer: **(A)**.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Option (D) reads like a weakener because "below-average entrance" sounds negative. It is actually a **strengthener** — students with low baselines had more room to improve, making the 15% gain more impressive. Always check whether a fact makes the conclusion *more* or *less* credible before selecting.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Boundary Conditions

**Necessary vs Sufficient Assumptions** is the most mis-scored question type. A *necessary* assumption is one without which the argument fails — negate it and the conclusion becomes possible but no longer guaranteed. A *sufficient* assumption, if true, would make the conclusion true on its own but is not required by the current argument. LAT answer keys almost always reward the necessary one. To test quickly: mentally **negate** each option; if the argument survives, it is not the necessary assumption.

**Correlation–Causation traps** come in three flavours — alternative cause (above), **reverse causation** (Y can have caused X, not the other way around), and the **third-variable** fallacy (a hidden Z drives both X and Y). A strong weakener either names the third variable or breaks the temporal sequence.

**Qualifier drift** is silent but lethal. If the passage says "most A are B" and the conclusion is "all A are B", the move is a quantifier shift — an **illegitimate conversion**. Spot the words *some, most, many, in many papers, in most keys, rarely, never, all, only* on every reading.

| Trap | How It Manifests | Detection Cue |
|---|---|---|
| Direction-of-effect reversal | Booster chosen for "weaken" | Underline the verb in the stem |
| Real-world truth ≠ logical necessity | Factually correct option that misses the argument | Check if option links to the actual chain |
| Sufficient mistaken for necessary | Answer would prove the conclusion, but isn't required | Negate test |
| Quantifier drift | "Most" silently becomes "all" | Circle every quantifier |
| Appeal to emotion disguised as evidence | Distracting vivid anecdote | Ask: does it speak to the stated reasoning? |

#### Analogy Questions — When Comparisons Carry Weight

Analogical arguments on LAT in many papers present Case A and Case B, then claim B will produce the same outcome as A. To strengthen, find another **shared relevant property** between A and B. To weaken, point out a **disanalogy** — a difference in the two cases that would change the outcome. The strength of an analogy scales with the **number** and **relevance** of shared features; one key disanalogy can defeat many minor similarities.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. **Necessary Assumption Drill:** Take any editorial from a Pakistani newspaper (e.g., *Dawn*, *The News*). Underline the conclusion, then list every unstated premise that must be true for the argument to hold. Convert each into an MCQ option and run the **negate test** — which option, when negated, collapses the argument?
2. **Fallacy Speed-Round:** Time yourself 90 seconds per passage. Read one short op-ed, identify the single dominant fallacy, and write a one-line justification. After ten rounds, track your miss rate; anything above 20% signals a need to re-read the seven-fallacy list above.

#### Exam Strategy and Weightage

Critical Reasoning carries **5% of the LAT paper** within the broader Analytical Reasoning section, but passage-based MCQs are where high scorers separate from the field. HEC in standard papers gives 60–90 seconds per question. Two structural habits save time: (1) read the question stem *before* the passage — it primes you to scan for the right feature, and (2) predict the answer type (booster, weakener, assumption) before looking at options, so distractors lose their pull. Most wrong answers on LAT are *plausible-but-irrelevant* rather than *false*; the discipline is to demand **logical connection**, not real-world agreement.
