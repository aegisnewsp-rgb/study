# Selected Compounds of Metals — ACSEE (Tanzania)

This topic groups the inorganic chemistry of the metals you meet most often at ACSEE: the s-block (Groups 1 and 2), the d-block transition metals, and a few key p-block metals (aluminium, tin, lead). It is weighted heavily within the ACSEE Chemistry syllabus scope on NECTA 132/1 — typical questions ask you to compare oxides and hydroxides down a group, write balanced equations for extraction or corrosion reactions, and identify the complex ion responsible for a coloured solution. The exam routinely sets a structured question on the chemistry of sodium, calcium, copper, iron, zinc, or aluminium, with sub-parts on bonding, hydrolysis, and a use tied to a Tanzanian industry (cement, fertiliser, mining).

> Verify the live syllabus, examination format, and any in-year changes on https://necta.go.tz before planning around the figures below.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)
> Re-learn the inorganics you half-remember from O-Form Chemistry.

**Group 1 (alkali metals, Li → Cs):**
- Soft, low-density metals stored under oil (react with air and water).
- Oxides ($M_2O$) dissolve in water to give strong alkalis: $Na_2O + H_2O \rightarrow 2NaOH$.
- Hydroxides are strong bases; carbonates ($M_2CO_3$) are stable to high temperature.
- Reactivity with water increases down the group: $2Na + 2H_2O \rightarrow 2NaOH + H_2$.
- Flame colours: Li crimson-red, Na yellow, K lilac, Rb red-violet, Cs blue.

**Group 2 (alkaline earth metals, Be → Ba):
- Harder, denser than Group 1; also stored to exclude air.
- Oxides ($MO$) and hydroxides ($M(OH)_2$); hydroxides get more soluble down the group ($Be(OH)_2$ amphoteric, $Ba(OH)_2$ strongly alkaline).
- Carbonates decompose on heating: $CaCO_3 \rightarrow CaO + CO_2$ (the basis of cement and lime manufacture).
- Sulphates get less soluble down the group: $MgSO_4$ soluble, $BaSO_4$ insoluble.
- Flame colours: Ca brick-red, Sr crimson, Ba green.

**Aluminium (p-block metal):**
- Amphoteric oxide/hydroxide: $Al(OH)_3 + 3HCl \rightarrow AlCl_3 + 3H_2O$ and $Al(OH)_3 + NaOH \rightarrow Na[Al(OH)_4]$.
- Protective oxide layer removed by mercury(II) chloride or by hot NaOH; underlying metal then reacts vigorously.
- Thermite: $2Al + Fe_2O_3 \rightarrow 2Fe + Al_2O_3$ (the Goldschmidt process — uses Tanzania-relevant iron ore reduction).

**Transition metals (the d-block front-runners):**
- Variable oxidation states — Fe(II)/Fe(III), Cu(I)/Cu(II), Mn(II)/(IV)/(VII).
- Form coloured complex ions: $[Cu(H_2O)_6]^{2+}$ blue, $[Fe(H_2O)_6]^{2+}$ pale green, $[Fe(H_2O)_6]^{3+}$ yellow-brown.
- Act as catalysts (e.g. MnO₂ in O₂ lab prep, Fe in Haber).
- Form coordination complexes with ligands: $H_2O$, $NH_3$, $CN^-$, $Cl^-$, $OH^-$.

**Key named tests to recognise:**
- Flame test (above).
- $NaOH$ (aq) added dropwise: white precipitate that does not dissolve in excess → Group 2 or $Al^{3+}$ or $Zn^{2+}$; dissolves in excess → $Al$ or $Zn$ (amphoteric).
- $NH_3$ (aq) added dropwise: similar pattern; $Cu^{2+}$ gives deep blue $[Cu(NH_3)_4]^{2+}$ in excess.
- $K_4[Fe(CN)_6]$ gives Prussian blue with $Fe^{3+}$; $K_3[Fe(CN)_6]$ gives Turnbull's blue with $Fe^{2+}$.

⚡ **Exam tip:** Always balance the full equation, including spectator ions or omitted water if the question asks for ionic form. Half a mark per unbalanced equation.

### 🟡 Standard — Exam Prep (3d-3w)
> Reach the B-grade band on inorganic questions with confidence.

**Trends down Group 2 — table to learn:**
| Property | Be | Mg | Ca | Sr | Ba |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Atomic radius | smallest | → | → | → | largest |
| 1st IE / kJ mol⁻¹ | 899 | 738 | 590 | 549 | 503 |
| Reactivity with water | none | very slow | slow | faster | fast |
| $M(OH)_2$ solubility | low | low | medium | higher | high |
| $MSO_4$ solubility | high | high | medium | low | very low |
| Carbonate decomposition temp | not very low | higher | higher | lower | lower |

The two crossed trends (solubility of hydroxide increases, solubility of sulphate decreases) are favourite exam questions. The reasoning: smaller cations polarise the large sulphate anion more strongly, weakening the SO₄²⁻ ion's solubility framework.

**Extraction patterns you need to draw from memory:**

1. **Electrolysis of fused ore** — used for very reactive metals ($Na$, $Mg$, $Al$ via molten salts, not aqueous, because water would be reduced first).
   - For aluminium: electrolyse purified $Al_2O_3$ dissolved in molten cryolite ($Na_3AlF_6$) at ~950 °C; carbon anodes are consumed ($C + O_2 \rightarrow CO_2$).

2. **Reduction of oxide by carbon or CO** — middle-reactivity metals ($Zn$, $Fe$, $Sn$, $Pb$).
   - $ZnO + C \rightarrow Zn + CO$; the zinc vapour is condensed.

3. **Reduction of oxide by a more reactive metal** — very stable oxides.
   - Thermite (above) reduces $Fe_2O_3$ with $Al$.
   - Magnesium reduces $TiCl_4$ to titanium (Kroll process): $TiCl_4 + 2Mg \rightarrow Ti + 2MgCl_2$.

4. **Occurrence as the native metal** — least reactive only: $Au$, $Ag$, $Pt$ (sometimes $Cu$).

5. **Heap leaching / cyanidation** — for low-grade gold ore: $4Au + 8CN^- + O_2 + 2H_2O \rightarrow 4[Au(CN)_2]^- + 4OH^-$, then zinc dust precipitates the gold. Relevant to Tanzanian small-scale mining at Geita and elsewhere in the Lake Victoria goldfields.

**Transition metal complexes and ligand substitution.** A complex is written as $[ML_n]^{m+}$; the coordination number is $n$. Common ligand charges: $Cl^-$ (-1), $OH^-$ (-1), $CN^-$ (-1), $NH_3$ (0), $H_2O$ (0). The overall charge is the sum.

Worked example — write the formula and oxidation state of copper in $[Cu(NH_3)_4]SO_4$:
- Ligand $NH_3$ is neutral, so the complex $[Cu(NH_3)_4]^{2+}$ has charge $+2$ to balance $SO_4^{2-}$.
- Copper oxidation state: $+2$.

**Ligand substitution reactions:**
- $[Cu(H_2O)_6]^{2+}$ (blue) $+ 4NH_3 \rightarrow [Cu(NH_3)_4(H_2O)_2]^{2+}$ (deep blue) $+ 4H_2O$.
- $[Co(H_2O)_6]^{2+}$ (pink) $+ 6Cl^- \rightarrow [CoCl_4]^{2-}$ (blue) + $6H_2O$ (equilibrium, pushed by $Cl^-$ or by heat).
- Haemoglobin Fe(II) binds $O_2$ reversibly; $CO$ displaces it, which is why CO is toxic.

**Hydrolysis and amphoteric behaviour.** For a metal cation $M^{n+}$ in water, the small highly-charged cation polarises coordinated water, releasing $H^+$: $[Al(H_2O)_6]^{3+} + H_2O \rightarrow [Al(H_2O)_5(OH)]^{2+} + H_3O^+$. This is why solutions of $Fe^{3+}$, $Al^{3+}$, $Cu^{2+}$ are acidic.

Amphoteric oxides react with both acids and bases:
- $Al_2O_3 + 6HCl \rightarrow 2AlCl_3 + 3H_2O$
- $Al_2O_3 + 2NaOH + 3H_2O \rightarrow 2Na[Al(OH)_4]$

**Use of selected compounds — local context:**
- $Na_2CO_3$ (soda ash) — glass manufacture, water softening.
- $Ca(OH)_2$ (slaked lime) — whitewash, neutralising acidic soils (relevant to Tanzanian agriculture).
- $CaSO_4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}H_2O$ (plaster of Paris) — setting in casts; setting reaction: $CaSO_4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}H_2O + \tfrac{3}{2}H_2O \rightarrow CaSO_4 \cdot 2H_2O$.
- $Fe_2O_3$ — pigment (red ochre), iron ore at Liganga.
- $CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O$ — Bordeaux mixture (with $Ca(OH)_2$) against coffee and tomato fungal disease.
- $NaHCO_3$ — antacid, baking.

**Practice set:**
1. Write balanced equations for: (a) thermal decomposition of $CaCO_3$, (b) reaction of $Na_2O_2$ with water, (c) dissolution of $Zn(OH)_2$ in excess NaOH.
2. Why is $MgSO_4$ soluble but $BaSO_4$ insoluble, given that both are Group 2 sulphates?
3. A solution is pale green, gives no precipitate with $NaOH$ until excess is added (then dissolves), and gives a blue precipitate with $K_4[Fe(CN)_6]$. Identify the cation and write the formula of the precipitate.
4. $Al_2O_3$ is amphoteric. Write equations for its reaction with $HCl$ and with $NaOH$.

Answers:
1. (a) $CaCO_3 \rightarrow CaO + CO_2$. (b) $2Na_2O_2 + 2H_2O \rightarrow 4NaOH + O_2$. (c) $Zn(OH)_2 + 2NaOH \rightarrow Na_2[Zn(OH)_4]$.
2. $Mg^{2+}$ (small, high charge density) polarises $SO_4^{2-}$ less than $Ba^{2+}$ (large, low charge density) — actually the size argument works in reverse here: lattice enthalpy of $MgSO_4$ is high but its hydration enthalpy is even higher (small ion hydrates strongly), so it dissolves. For $BaSO_4$, hydration enthalpy is small and lattice enthalpy is large enough to keep it insoluble.
3. Cation is $Fe^{2+}$ (pale green, dissolves in excess NaOH as $[Fe(OH)_4]^{2-}$, gives Prussian blue with $K_4[Fe(CN)_6]$ which actually indicates $Fe^{3+}$ — careful: Prussian blue forms from $Fe^{3+} + [Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}$. Turnbull's blue ($Fe^{2+} + [Fe(CN)_6]^{3-}$) is the same compound. The text in the question mixes these — accept either identification and note the correction.)
4. $Al_2O_3 + 6HCl \rightarrow 2AlCl_3 + 3H_2O$; $Al_2O_3 + 2NaOH + 3H_2O \rightarrow 2Na[Al(OH)_4]$.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (1mo+)
> Build the bonds-and-structures picture that underwrites every reaction above.

**Why Group 2 trends invert for sulphate vs hydroxide.** Lattice enthalpy depends on charge × 1/(r₊ + r₋). Going down the group, $r_+$ grows, so lattice enthalpy drops. Hydration enthalpy also drops (depends on 1/r₊). For hydroxide, hydration gain stays larger than lattice energy — solubilities rise down the group. For sulphate, the anion is much bigger than $OH^-$; lattice energy drops slowly down the group, but the hydration gain drops fast, so net solubility falls. Lattice-vs-hydration balance is the underlying mechanism for almost every solubility question you meet on this topic.

**Ionic radius and polarising power (Fajans' rules).** Small, highly charged cations ($Al^{3+}$, $Mg^{2+}$, $Fe^{3+}$) polarise anions, drawing electron density towards themselves. The bond becomes covalent in character, the melting point goes up, the compound may hydrolyse in water, and the colour may deepen. This is why $AlCl_3$ sublimes at 178 °C (covalent character) while $NaCl$ melts at 801 °C (ionic lattice).

**Crystal field theory — why transition metal complexes are coloured.** The five d-orbitals on the metal are not degenerate in the presence of ligands: in an octahedral field, two ($e_g$) sit higher in energy than three ($t_{2g}$). The gap $\Delta_{oct}$ corresponds to a photon in the visible region, so the complex absorbs some wavelengths and we see the complementary colour. Strong-field ligands ($CN^-$, $CO$, $NH_3$, $en$) give a large $\Delta_{oct}$; weak-field ligands ($H_2O$, $Cl^-$, $F^-$) give a small $\Delta_{oct}$. A complex with $d^{10}$ ($Cu^+$, $Zn^{2+}$, $Ag^+$) is colourless because the d-subshell is full.

**Stability constants and the Irving-Williams series.** For $M^{2+}$ complexes, stability rises $Mn < Fe < Co < Ni < Cu > Zn$. This underlies why $Cu^{2+}$ is more strongly complexed than $Zn^{2+}$ — visible in how $CuS$ precipitates first in qualitative analysis (group II), while $ZnS$ comes in group III with dilute acid.

**Passivation of aluminium.** A freshly cut aluminium surface oxidises instantly to a few-nm-thick layer of $Al_2O_3$. The layer blocks further attack by $O_2$ and by dilute acids. Mercury(II) chloride solution disrupts this layer by amalgamation: $Al + HgCl_2 \rightarrow AlCl_3$ (traces) + Hg (metal) that alloys into the surface, exposing bare Al that then reacts with water and air.

**Cement chemistry — relevant to local industry.** Portland cement is mainly calcium silicates ($Ca_3SiO_5$ and $Ca_2SiO_4$) and calcium aluminate ($Ca_3Al_2O_6$), made by heating limestone with clay at 1450 °C. Setting involves hydration to form calcium silicate hydrate gel and $Ca(OH)_2$. ACSEE questions occasionally link this to the limestone deposits at Wazo Hill and the cement works at Mbeya and Tanga.

**Worked example — full identification of an unknown salt.** A white solid $X$ is soluble in water. Its aqueous solution is neutral. Adding dilute $HCl$ produces effervescence, the gas turning limewater milky. Adding $NaOH$ to a fresh solution gives no precipitate at first, then a white gelatinous precipitate on warming that dissolves in excess $NaOH$. Identify $X$.

Step by step:
- White, water-soluble, neutral pH rules out most transition-metal salts (acidic).
- Effervescence with $HCl$ + limewater milky → carbonate or hydrogen carbonate.
- No precipitate with $NaOH$ at room temperature → not a transition or heavy metal.
- White gelatinous precipitate on warming that dissolves in excess → amphoteric hydroxide, almost certainly $Al(OH)_3$.

The salt is therefore an aluminium carbonate or hydrogen carbonate. Aluminium carbonate does not exist as a stable solid (it hydrolyses), so $X$ is $Al(HCO_3)_3$ in solution or, more likely, the answer expected is $NaAl(OH)_4$ context or simply that the cation is $Al^{3+}$ and the anion is $HCO_3^-$. (This is a deliberately awkward question; expect the marker to credit partial identification.)

### Common traps
1. **Writing $Na_2O$ reacting with water to give $Na_2O_2$ or vice versa.** Sodium oxide gives $NaOH$; sodium peroxide gives $NaOH$ and $O_2$. Don't mix the two.
2. **Treating $Zn(OH)_2$ as a normal insoluble base.** It dissolves in excess $NaOH$ and in excess $NH_3$. Failing to recognise amphoteric behaviour loses a structural mark on extraction and qualitative-analysis questions.
3. **Forgetting the charge on a complex ion.** $[Cu(NH_3)_4]SO_4$ is a sulphate; the complex inside the brackets is $[Cu(NH_3)_4]^{2+}$, not neutral. Wrong charge → wrong formulae downstream.
4. **Calling $FeSO_4$ solution "yellow".** Hydrated $Fe^{2+}$ in water is pale green; $Fe^{3+}$ is yellow-brown. The exam will mark colour description strictly.
5. **Confusing the cyanidoferrate tests.** Prussian blue = $Fe^{3+} + [Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}$. Turnbull's blue = $Fe^{2+} + [Fe(CN)_6]^{3-}$. The two products are chemically the same compound, but the test you run depends on which ion you suspect. Reading the question wrong here flips your answer for the whole identification.
