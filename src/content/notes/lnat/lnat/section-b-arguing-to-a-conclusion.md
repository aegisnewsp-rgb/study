# Section B — Arguing to a Conclusion — LNAT Notes

Section B is marked on the ability to argue economically and to come to a conclusion, so what is being looked for is one line of reasoning carried through to a stated position, not a balanced list of considerations. The shape that scores is simple: state your position in the opening paragraph, spend the body making the strongest case for it, deal with the obvious objection in one paragraph rather than giving it equal weight, and finish with a conclusion that answers the exact question. A shorter essay with a clear spine scores better than a longer one that hedges. The skill is the same one Section A tests on a smaller scale — pick the claim the support earns, hold it, and let the argument run to its conclusion.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### The five-part shape of a Section B essay

- **Opening (80–100 words).** State your position in plain language. Name the question. Do not paraphrase the question.
- **Reason one (120–150 words).** The strongest reason for the position. One claim, one piece of support, one sentence on why the alternative does not hold.
- **Reason two (120–150 words).** The second reason. Build on reason one — do not restart.
- **Objection paragraph (80–100 words).** The obvious objection, named and granted. Then the limit of the objection — what it does not defeat. Then back to the position.
- **Conclusion (50–80 words).** Answer the exact question. Do not introduce a new argument. Do not restate the introduction.

The shape fits inside 500–600 words, which the Consortium's official guidance recommends as the target.

#### The connective vocabulary

Plain connectives carry the movement of the argument. Keep to the small set:

- **because** — for the reason that
- **therefore** — for the consequence that follows
- **however** — for the objection you are granting
- **even so** — for the limit of the objection, where you return to the position

Avoid: "It could be argued that", "On the one hand", "It is important to note". Those phrases lengthen sentences without advancing the argument. Each one usually hides a hedge that should be cut.

#### The spine in one sentence

A Section B essay with a clear spine can be summarised in one sentence: *I take position X, for reasons A and B, granting objection Y but only up to point Z*. If you cannot write that sentence, the essay does not have a spine. Cut until it does.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### The opening paragraph

The opening paragraph does three jobs and three jobs only:

1. **Names the question.** One sentence. Not a paraphrase — a name. "The question is whether judges should be allowed to make new law."
2. **States the position.** One sentence. Plain language. No hedge. "My position is that they should, provided the law-making function is openly named."
3. **Names the reasons.** One sentence. A list, not paragraphs. "I will argue for this on two grounds: that the common law evolves by judge-made principle, and that judicial transparency about the role improves democratic accountability."

That is the opening. ~80–100 words. It is not a warm-up; it is the spine of the essay stated in full.

#### The body paragraphs

Each body paragraph carries one reason. The internal shape:

- **Claim.** The reason, stated in plain language. One sentence.
- **Support.** The case for the claim — an example, an analogy, a piece of reasoning, a citation. Two or three sentences.
- **Limit.** One sentence on why the alternative does not hold.

The limit sentence is what stops the paragraph from looking like a one-sided survey. Without it, the paragraph reads as assertion. With it, the paragraph reads as argument.

A worked example, using the opening above:

> **Reason one.** The common law has always evolved by judge-made principle; the doctrines of precedent and ratio decidendi exist precisely because earlier courts made new law and later courts followed it. To forbid judges from making new law would be to freeze the common law at the date of any given statute. *Therefore*, the question is not whether judges should make new law but whether they should do so transparently.

The paragraph carries one reason (judge-made law is unavoidable), supports it with the historical fact of common-law evolution, and ends with a limit (the question is not whether, but how). The connective is plain ("therefore"). The limit sentence redirects the question rather than restarting the argument.

#### The objection paragraph

The objection paragraph is the section's signature. Most students either skip it (which makes the essay look one-sided) or give it equal weight (which makes the essay look balanced, which is the failure the section is designed to detect). The right move is to deal with the objection in one paragraph and grant it only up to a point.

The internal shape:

- **Name the objection.** One sentence. Plain language.
- **Grant the objection.** One or two sentences. The strongest version of the objection, stated fairly.
- **State the limit.** One sentence. What the objection does not defeat.
- **Return to the position.** One sentence.

Worked example:

> **Objection.** The strongest objection to judge-made law is the democratic one: unelected judges should not bind Parliament. *However*, the objection proves too much — it would also forbid the common law itself, which is judge-made and has stood for centuries. *Even so*, the objection does point at a real risk: judge-made law can drift away from democratic consent. *Therefore*, the answer is not to abolish the power but to require judges to name it openly when they exercise it.

Four sentences. The objection is named and granted; the limit is stated; the position is returned to. The connectives ("however", "even so", "therefore") carry the movement.

#### The conclusion

The conclusion does two jobs and two jobs only:

1. **Restate the position in light of the reasons.** One sentence.
2. **Answer the exact question.** One sentence.

The conclusion is not a summary of the body. It is the position, stated one more time, in light of what the body has earned the right to say.

A worked example, in continuation:

> **Conclusion.** Judges should be allowed to make new law, but the power must be openly named and the limits acknowledged. On those conditions, the democratic objection is met and the common law can continue to do the work Parliament cannot always do quickly enough.

Two sentences. The position is restated. The question is answered. No new argument.

#### Annotated paragraph: hedging version rewritten to committed version

Below is the same paragraph in two versions. The first is a typical hedging version; the second is the same argument committed to a spine.

**Hedging version (do not write this).**

> There are arguments on both sides of the question of whether judges should be allowed to make new law. On the one hand, it could be argued that the common law has always evolved by judge-made principle, and there is a case for saying that without this the law would be frozen. On the other hand, it could also be argued that unelected judges should not bind Parliament, and this is an important consideration. It is therefore difficult to come to a definitive conclusion, although there are some points that could be made.

The paragraph lists considerations on both sides, opens with a hedge, ends with a refusal to commit. The connective vocabulary ("it could be argued", "on the other hand", "it is therefore difficult") is the giveaway: every connective is a way to avoid the argument. The paragraph reads as a survey of the topic, not as an argument about the question.

**Committed version (the rewrite).**

> The common law has always evolved by judge-made principle; the doctrines of precedent and ratio decidendi exist precisely because earlier courts made new law and later courts followed them. To forbid judges from making new law would be to freeze the common law at the date of any given statute. *Therefore* the question is not whether judges should make new law, but whether they should do so transparently.

The same content, committed to a position. The hedge ("there are arguments on both sides") is gone. The connective is plain ("therefore"). The paragraph ends with a redirection, not a refusal. The argument runs; it does not survey.

The difference between the two is not word count or vocabulary — the committed version is shorter, but the hedging version could be cut to the same length without gaining a spine. The difference is the willingness to commit. Section B is marked on that willingness, and the marker can see it in the first sentence.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Why a clear spine beats a longer hedging essay

The section's stated marking criterion is the ability to argue economically and to come to a conclusion. Two consequences follow.

First, length is not rewarded. The Consortium's recommended maximum is 750 words, ideally about 500 to 600. A piece that runs to 900 words without a clear position scores lower than a 550-word piece with a spine. The extra words either restate the body (waste) or hedge the position (worse than waste).

Second, balance is not rewarded. A balanced survey of considerations is the failure mode the section is designed to detect. The skill being tested is argument, which by definition takes a position. A piece that does not take a position is not arguing; it is surveying.

The deeper reason: arguing economically is the same skill a lawyer needs in a courtroom — state the case, support it, deal with the strongest objection, restate the position in light of the support. A student who can do that in 550 words on a generic topic can do it in a courtroom. A student who has only ever produced balanced surveys has not practised the skill the section is testing.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- **"A balanced essay is safer."** The section is marked on the ability to come to a conclusion. A balanced essay that never commits is the failure the section is designed to detect. Commit.
- **"I should give every objection equal weight."** Deal with the obvious objection in one paragraph. Grant it; state its limit; return to the position. Equal weight is a different argument, and a longer one.
- **"Longer is more impressive."** The recommended maximum is 750 words, ideally about 500 to 600. A shorter essay with a spine scores better than a longer one that hedges.
- **"I should avoid strong words because they sound biased."** Strong words are not bias; bias is unsupported strong words. A position supported by two reasons is argument, not bias.
- **"Plain connectives sound childish."** Plain connectives are what argument sounds like in plain English. The connective vocabulary (because, therefore, however, even so) is the connective vocabulary of reasoned prose. Avoiding it does not make the essay more sophisticated; it makes it harder to follow.
- **"The conclusion is where I introduce a final clever point."** The conclusion is where you answer the exact question. New arguments belong in the body. A conclusion that introduces a new argument is the same failure as a balanced essay — a refusal to commit.

#### Practice routine that builds the spine

The most efficient training is the rewrite. Take one past LNAT essay question. Write a hedging version first, in 20 minutes, then rewrite it as a committed version in 15 minutes. The rewrite is where the lesson lives. Cutting "it could be argued that" and replacing it with "the reason is" is the whole skill in microcosm.

Then, once a week, write one full committed essay from the two-line plan to the reread. Time it strictly. The constraint is part of the skill.
