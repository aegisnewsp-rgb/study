# Moving Charges

This is the unit where electric current becomes magnetism. Every coil, solenoid, galvanometer and mass spectrometer question in the syllabus starts from one experimental fact — Oersted found in 1820 that a current-carrying wire deflects a compass needle — and then asks either *how strong is the field at this point* or *what force does this field exert*. Learn Biot–Savart and Ampère for the first question and the Lorentz force plus the right-hand rules for the second, and the whole chapter reduces to four formulas and three rules.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

**The four formulas that do almost all the work**

| Situation | Formula | Variables and units |
|---|---|---|
| Field at distance r from a long straight wire | $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$ | I in A, r in m, B in T |
| Field at the centre of a circular coil | $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 N I}{2R}$ | N turns, R in m |
| Field on the axis, distance x from centre | $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 N I R^2}{2(R^2 + x^2)^{3/2}}$ | x in m |
| Field inside a long solenoid | $B = \mu_0 n I$ with $n = N/\ell$ | n in turns m⁻¹ |
| Field inside a toroid | $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 N I}{2\pi r}$ | r = distance from axis |
| Force on a moving charge | $F = qvB\sin\theta$ | q in C, v in m s⁻¹, F in N |
| Force on a current-carrying wire | $F = I\ell B \sin\theta$ | $\ell$ in m |
| Force per metre between parallel wires | $\dfrac{F}{\ell} = \dfrac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi d}$ | d = separation |
| Torque on a current loop | $\tau = NIAB\sin\theta$ | A = area in m² |
| Magnetic moment of a loop | $m = NIA$ | A·m² |
| Radius of circular path in B | $r = \dfrac{mv_\perp}{qB}$ | m in kg |
| Cyclotron frequency | $f = \dfrac{qB}{2\pi m}$ | Hz |
| Velocity selector | $v = E/B$ | — |

$\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}$ T·m·A⁻¹, and since 2019 it is an exactly defined SI constant. The tesla is defined by $1\ \text{T} = 1\ \text{N/(A·m)}$.

**The three direction rules, kept straight**

- **Right-hand thumb rule** — thumb along the current, curled fingers give the direction of $\vec{B}$ circling a wire. Current into the page gives clockwise field lines; current out of the page gives counter-clockwise.
- **Right-hand grip rule** — for a solenoid or coil, curl your fingers in the direction of current around the turns; your thumb points to the **north** pole. For a single circular loop, curl your fingers the way the current flows and your thumb gives the direction of the magnetic moment.
- **Fleming's left-hand rule** — thumb along current, first finger along $\vec{B}$, second finger along the force. This is the *force on a current-carrying conductor*, nothing else. Fleming's *right*-hand rule is the generator rule: it gives the direction of *induced emf* when a conductor moves in a field, and the two are mirror images of each other.

**The three properties of a magnetic field, stated as facts**

1. It never begins or ends at a point — field lines always form closed loops, because no magnetic monopole exists.
2. It is strongest where the field lines are densest.
3. Like poles repel, unlike poles attract, and the field outside a bar magnet is identical to that of an electric dipole: $B = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\dfrac{2m}{r^3}$ on the axial line, half that on the equatorial line.

**High-yield one-liners**

- A magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so a magnetic field does **zero work** and can never change a particle's speed.
- Inside a long solenoid the field is uniform and strong; outside it is nearly zero. That asymmetry is the whole point of a solenoid.
- Only the velocity component **perpendicular** to $\vec{B}$ produces circular motion. The parallel component carries the particle along the field lines at constant speed.
- The cyclotron frequency is independent of the particle's speed and radius — that is what lets one oscillator accelerate ions of many different energies.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

**From Oersted to Biot–Savart**

Oersted placed a straight wire above a compass needle and switched on a current; the needle turned. That single observation says a magnetic field circles a straight conductor. It also raises a question that Ampère answered much later: if the field of a current is circular around the wire, how strong is it at a given point? Biot and Savart answered it by analogy with Coulomb's law, treating the current as a sum of infinitesimal elements:

$$dB = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{I\,dl\,\sin\theta}{r^2}$$

where $r$ is the distance from the element $Idl$ to the field point and $\theta$ is the angle between the element and the line joining it to that point. The $\sin\theta$ factor is worth dwelling on: an element pointing directly at the field point contributes nothing, which is why the field along the extension of a straight wire is zero.

**Ampère's circuital law**

$$\oint \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 I_{enc}$$

The circulation of the field around any closed loop equals the current the loop encloses. It is to magnetism what Gauss's law is to electricity, and it is the tool of choice for the highly symmetric cases where the magnitude of $\vec{B}$ is constant over a convenient path.

- **Long straight wire.** Choose a circular path of radius r around the wire; $\oint B\,dl = B \cdot 2\pi r$, so $B = \mu_0 I/2\pi r$.
- **Long solenoid.** Choose a path that runs along the interior field and returns through the nearly field-free exterior. The exterior contribution is negligible, so $B\ell = \mu_0 n I \ell$ and $B = \mu_0 nI$. Uniform inside, zero outside.
- **Toroid.** The path is a circle of radius r inside the core, so $B \cdot 2\pi r = \mu_0 N I$ and $B = \mu_0 N I/2\pi r$, valid only inside the ring. Outside the toroid the enclosed current is zero, so the field is zero.

**Force on a moving charge and on a conductor**

A charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec{v}$ in a field $\vec{B}$ feels

$$\vec{F} = q(\vec{v}\times\vec{B}), \qquad F = qvB\sin\theta$$

Because $\vec{F}$ is perpendicular to $\vec{v}$ at every instant, the speed is constant. Only the direction changes. Where $\vec{v}$ is parallel to $\vec{B}$ the cross product is zero and the charge moves in a straight line. Where they are perpendicular, the path is a circle and the charge is a **positive or negative** charge — the sign of $q$ decides whether the rotation is clockwise or anticlockwise as seen from a fixed direction.

For a current-carrying wire of length $\ell$ in a field, summing $qvB\sin\theta$ over all the free electrons in it gives $F = I\ell B\sin\theta$. The straight wire is the special case where $I\ell$ is a single vector; the loop is handled by summing the forces on each side or, more usefully, by taking torques.

**Force between two parallel wires**

Each wire produces a field at the position of the other, and that field exerts a force on the second. Per unit length,

$$\frac{F}{\ell} = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi d}$$

Currents in the *same* direction attract; currents in *opposite* directions repel. The reason is one line long: in the same-direction case the field of wire 1 at wire 2 points into the page, and $I_2 d\vec{l}\times\vec{B}$ then pulls wire 2 towards wire 1 — work it out with the cross product rather than memorising it, and the direction survives for life.

**Torque on a coil and magnetic moment**

A single-turn loop of area $A$ carrying current $I$ has magnetic moment $m = IA$, directed along the normal by the right-hand rule; an $N$-turn coil has $m = NIA$. In an external field the coil experiences a torque

$$\tau = mB\sin\theta = NIAB\sin\theta$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the **area vector** (the normal) and $\vec{B}$. The torque always rotates $m$ towards alignment with $\vec{B}$, and at $\theta = 0$ the torque vanishes and the coil is in equilibrium — this is why an unmagnetised needle aligns with Earth's field, and why a current-carrying coil in a field is a mechanical device with an automatic read-out.

The same idea as potential energy gives the interaction energy: $U = -\vec{m}\cdot\vec{B} = -mB\cos\theta$. Negative when aligned, so that is the stable state; the system does work of magnitude $2mB$ in swinging from the anti-aligned to the aligned orientation.

**Galvanometer and the moving-coil principle**

A moving-coil galvanometer is a rectangular coil on a soft-iron core, suspended in the field of a permanent magnet, with a spiral hairspring restoring it. The current produces a torque $NABIA$, the hairspring produces a restoring torque $\kappa\theta$, and at equilibrium $\theta = NIABIA/\kappa$ is proportional to $I$. The same device becomes a motor when the current is large enough to turn it, and a generator when the coil is rotated instead — which is why the motor rule and the generator rule are the same physics with the energy flow reversed.

**What you should be able to compute on sight**

- Field on the axis of a circular coil, and the special case at the centre.
- Field inside a solenoid and a toroid.
- Force on a charge, on a straight wire, and between two parallel wires.
- Radius, time period and frequency of a charged particle in a uniform field.
- Torque and magnetic moment of a coil.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

**Deriving the field on the axis of a circular loop**

Take a loop of radius $R$, $N$ turns, current $I$, and consider a point $P$ on the axis at distance $x$ from the centre. The field from the near side element points partly *along* the axis and partly perpendicular to it; by symmetry the perpendicular components from opposite elements cancel, leaving only the axial component $dB\cos\theta$. Each element is at the same distance $r = \sqrt{R^2 + x^2}$ from $P$, and $\cos\theta = R/r$ where $\theta$ is the angle between the radius vector and the axis. Integrating round the loop, whose total length is $2\pi R$:

$$B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{I \cdot 2\pi R \cdot R}{(R^2 + x^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{\mu_0 N I R^2}{2(R^2 + x^2)^{3/2}}$$

Two limits are worth memorising because questions ask for them directly. At the centre, $x = 0$, so $B = \mu_0 N I/2R$. Far away, $x \gg R$, so $B \approx \mu_0 N I R^2/2x^3$ — the field of a magnetic dipole with moment $m = NIA$, falling off as the inverse cube rather than the inverse square. The same expression describes the near field of a bar magnet.

**Charged particle in a uniform field — the full picture**

Resolve the velocity into components: $v_\parallel$ along $\vec{B}$ and $v_\perp$ perpendicular to it. The force depends only on $v_\perp$, so there are two simultaneous motions — uniform circular motion of radius $r = mv_\perp/qB$ in the plane perpendicular to $\vec{B}$, and uniform translation of speed $v_\parallel$ along $\vec{B}$. The superposition is a helix, tightening into a circle when $v_\parallel = 0$ and into a straight line when $v_\perp = 0$. The pitch of the helix is $v_\parallel T$ with $T = 2\pi m/qB$.

The time period $T = 2\pi m/qB$ and the frequency $f = qB/2\pi m$ contain no reference to $v$ or to the radius. That is the deep point: a fixed field fixes a fixed rotation frequency for a given particle, regardless of how fast it is going.

**Cyclotron**

Two hollow electrodes ("dees") sit in a uniform field perpendicular to the plane of the dees. An ion starts near the centre, and the field bends it into a circle; each time it crosses the gap, the electric field between the dees does work on it and increases its energy, and its radius grows. The AC supply on the dees must reverse direction every half revolution, which means the driving frequency must equal the cyclotron frequency $f = qB/2\pi m$. Two consequences follow directly. First, because $f$ is independent of energy, a single oscillator accelerates ions of many different masses and charges — but only those with the *same* $q/m$, since $f$ depends on $q/m$ and nothing else. Second, a non-resonant oscillator makes the particles spiral inward or drift out instead of gaining energy, which is exactly the failure the resonance condition is designed to detect.

**Velocity selector and mass spectrometer**

Crossed uniform fields $\vec{E}$ and $\vec{B}$ at right angles, with $\vec{E} \perp \vec{B}$ and both perpendicular to the beam. The electric force $qE$ and the magnetic force $qvB$ are in opposite directions when $\vec{v}$ is perpendicular to both. Setting them equal gives $qE = qvB$, so

$$v = \frac{E}{B}$$

Charges at exactly that speed go straight through undeflected. Slower or faster ions are deflected and hit the wall. The selected beam then enters a region with magnetic field only, where the ions follow circles of radius $r = mv/qB = \frac{mv}{q}\cdot\frac{1}{B}$ and the radius measures $m/q$ — that is the working principle of a mass spectrometer. Because $r$ scales with $v$, the ions must all travel at the same speed on entering, which is precisely why the velocity selector comes first.

**Hall effect**

Take a conductor carrying current $I$ with cross-sectional area $A = wt$ and place it in a perpendicular magnetic field $B$. The magnetic force on the carriers pushes them to one side of the conductor until the resulting electric force balances it. For carrier charge density $ne$ and drift speed $v_d$, balance gives $eE = ev_dB$, and with $I = nev_dA$ this becomes $E = BI/(neA)$. The Hall voltage across the width $w$ is

$$V_H = Ew = \frac{BI}{net}$$

The sign of $V_H$ tells you whether the carriers are positive or negative, which is the decisive experiment for the semiconductor questions in this chapter. The magnitude also gives the carrier density $n$ directly, which is how Hall measurements are used in practice.

**Worked example — proton in a magnetic field**

A proton, $m = 1.67\times 10^{-27}$ kg, $q = 1.602\times 10^{-19}$ C, enters a field of 0.5 T perpendicular to its velocity of $2\times 10^6$ m s⁻¹.

Radius: $r = \dfrac{mv}{qB} = \dfrac{1.67\times 10^{-27} \times 2\times 10^6}{1.602\times 10^{-19}\times 0.5} = \dfrac{3.34\times 10^{-21}}{8.01\times 10^{-20}} = 4.17\times 10^{-2}$ m ≈ 4.2 cm.
Period: $T = \dfrac{2\pi m}{qB} = \dfrac{2\pi \times 1.67\times 10^{-27}}{8.01\times 10^{-20}} = 1.31\times 10^{-7}$ s.
Frequency: $f = 1/T = 7.63\times 10^6$ Hz, and $qB/2\pi m$ gives the same value directly.

Note what the period depends on: the field, the charge, the mass. Nothing about the speed. An electron in the same field has a 1836-times-larger frequency, because its mass is that much smaller.

**Worked example — a coil in a field, with the angle handled correctly**

A 50-turn coil of radius 8 cm carries 2 A in a uniform field of 0.4 T. The **plane** of the coil makes 30° with the field. Find the torque and the direction of rotation.

Area of one turn: $A = \pi R^2 = \pi (0.08)^2 = 2.011\times 10^{-2}$ m².
Magnetic moment: $m = NIA = 50 \times 2 \times 2.011\times 10^{-2} = 2.011$ A·m².
The area vector is *perpendicular* to the plane, so if the plane is at 30° to $\vec{B}$ the normal is at $90° - 30° = 60°$. Hence $\sin 60° = 0.866$.
Torque: $\tau = mB\sin 60° = 2.011 \times 0.4 \times 0.866 = 0.697$ N·m.

The coil rotates until its normal is parallel to $\vec{B}$, i.e. until the plane is perpendicular to the field, where the torque is zero. Reading sin 30° instead of sin 60° gives 0.40 N·m and is the single most common error in this question type — write the angle sentence, not just the number.

**Worked example — two parallel wires**

Two long parallel wires 5 cm apart carry 2 A each in the same direction.

$$\frac{F}{\ell} = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi d} = \frac{4\pi\times 10^{-7} \times 2 \times 2}{2\pi \times 0.05} = \frac{16\pi\times 10^{-7}}{0.1\pi} = 3.2\times 10^{-5}\ \text{N m}^{-1}$$

Attractive, because the currents run the same way. Over a 1 m length that is a total force of $3.2 \times 10^{-5}$ N, which is small but real — and the formula is the reason parallel high-current busbars must be mechanically supported.

**Where the classical picture stops**

The formula $r = mv/qB$ is non-relativistic. As $v$ approaches $c$, momentum becomes $p = \gamma mv$ with $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$, and the radius must be written $r = p/qB = \gamma mv/qB$. The period is correspondingly longer: a relativistic cyclotron does not produce a fixed frequency, which is why synchrotrons and cyclotrons are built differently. For the problems in this chapter the non-relativistic form is correct, and recognising that is the whole of the caveat.

- **"Motor left, generator right."** Fleming's *left*-hand rule gives the force on a current; his *right*-hand rule gives the direction of induced emf when the circuit moves. The two are mirror images and swapping them flips the sign of the answer.
- **"Thumb is the current, fingers are the field."** One rule, two applications: a straight wire gives $\vec{B}$ around the wire; a coil gives the north pole along the thumb.
- **"Inside strong, outside gone."** The field of a long solenoid is uniform inside and nearly zero outside — that asymmetry is what a solenoid is for.
- **"Magnetic does no work."** The force is perpendicular to the velocity, so kinetic energy is constant in any magnetic field. Any "the particle speeds up in the magnet" claim is wrong.
- **"Only the perpendicular part counts."** $v_\perp$ sets the radius, $v_\parallel$ sets the pitch. Using $v$ instead of $v_\perp$ in the radius formula is a frequent slip.
- **"Angle to the normal, not to the plane."** In $\tau = NIAB\sin\theta$, $\theta$ is between the area vector and $\vec{B}$. A plane at 30° to the field means $\theta = 60°$.
- **Flashcard Q&A:**
  - *Field at the centre of a circular loop?* → $\mu_0 N I/2R$.
  - *Field far from a small loop?* → dipole form, falls as $1/x^3$, not $1/x^2$.
  - *Two wires, currents same direction?* → attractive.
  - *Does $\vec{B}$ do work on a charge?* → no, force is always perpendicular to $\vec{v}$.
  - *Path when $\vec{v}$ makes an angle with $\vec{B}$?* → helix.
  - *Why must the cyclotron's AC frequency match $qB/2\pi m$?* → the particle arrives at the gap only every $T$; otherwise it never gets the kick at the right phase.
  - *Field outside a toroid?* → zero, because the enclosed current is zero.
  - *What does the sign of the Hall voltage tell you?* → whether the majority carriers are electrons or holes.

### 🎯 Exam Traps & Error Log

1. **Using the full speed in $r = mv/qB$ when $\vec{v}$ is not perpendicular to $\vec{B}$.** The correct radius is $r = mv_\perp/qB$; the parallel component produces a helix, not a circle.
2. **Forgetting the $\sin\theta$ on $qvB\sin\theta$** or on $I\ell B\sin\theta$. At $\theta = 90°$ the sine is 1 and the force is largest; at $\theta = 0$ the force is zero and the conductor is along the field.
3. **Putting $\theta = 30°$ in the torque formula when the problem says the *plane* makes 30° with the field.** The angle for $\tau = mB\sin\theta$ is between the area vector and the field, so it is 60°.
4. **Forgetting the factor of $N$ in $B = \mu_0 N I/2R$.** It is the most frequently dropped multiplier in the chapter.
5. **Using $B = \mu_0 I/2\pi r$ for the field inside a solenoid.** That is the straight-wire formula; a solenoid's field is $B = \mu_0 nI$ and depends on turns *per metre*, not on total current alone.
6. **Claiming a magnetic field does not affect an uncharged particle.** Correct — and a frequent assertion–reason option, because the Lorentz force is $q(\vec{E} + \vec{v}\times\vec{B})$ and vanishes when $q = 0$.
7. **Writing a solenoid's field as uniform everywhere.** It is uniform and strong inside and nearly zero outside, and a toroid's field exists only inside the ring.
8. **Getting the direction of force between parallel wires by memory.** Derive it once with the cross product: same-direction currents attract, opposite-direction currents repel, and the derivation takes a minute and never fails.
9. **Swapping Fleming's rules**, or using the right-hand thumb rule to get the direction of a *force* — the thumb rule gives field direction or pole, never force.
10. **Ignoring the sign of the charge in circular-motion questions.** An electron and a proton with the same speed in the same field have the same radius ($|q|$ enters) but rotate in opposite senses.
11. **Treating the $1/r$ or $1/r^3$ fall-off as the same for a wire and a loop.** A straight wire's field falls as $1/r$; a small loop's far field falls as $1/r^3$.

### 🧪 Self-Test — 8 Questions with Worked Answers

**1. A circular coil of 100 turns and radius 20 cm carries 4 A. Find the field at the centre and at 20 cm along the axis.**
Centre: $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 N I}{2R} = \dfrac{4\pi\times 10^{-7}\times 100 \times 4}{2 \times 0.2} = \dfrac{5.03\times 10^{-4}}{0.4} = 1.257\times 10^{-3}$ T.
On axis at $x = 0.2$ m: $R = x = 0.2$ m, so $R^2 + x^2 = 0.08$ and $(0.08)^{3/2} = 0.02263$.
$B = \dfrac{4\pi\times 10^{-7}\times 100\times 4\times 0.04}{2 \times 0.02263} = \dfrac{2.01\times 10^{-5}}{0.04525} = 4.45\times 10^{-4}$ T.
Note the field has fallen to about 35 % of its central value at a distance equal to the radius. If a candidate's answer is larger than the central value, they have divided instead of multiplied somewhere.

**2. A long solenoid of 500 turns, 25 cm long, carries 3 A. Find the field inside and the number of turns per metre.**
$n = N/\ell = 500/0.25 = 2000$ turns m⁻¹.
$B = \mu_0 n I = 4\pi\times 10^{-7}\times 2000\times 3 = 7.54\times 10^{-3}$ T.
The field outside is negligible for a long solenoid. If the solenoid were 500 turns only 2.5 cm long, $n$ would be 200 per cm and the internal field would be ten times smaller — turn density, not total turns, is what sets the field.

**3. A proton enters a uniform magnetic field of 0.2 T with a velocity of $3\times 10^6$ m s⁻¹ at 30° to the field. Find the radius of the circular component and the speed along the field.**
Perpendicular component: $v_\perp = v\sin 30° = 1.5\times 10^6$ m s⁻¹. Parallel: $v_\parallel = v\cos 30° = 2.6\times 10^6$ m s⁻¹.
$r = \dfrac{mv_\perp}{qB} = \dfrac{1.67\times 10^{-27}\times 1.5\times 10^6}{1.602\times 10^{-19}\times 0.2} = \dfrac{2.505\times 10^{-21}}{3.204\times 10^{-20}} = 7.82\times 10^{-2}$ m ≈ 7.8 cm.
The path is a helix of radius 7.8 cm advancing at $2.6\times 10^6$ m s⁻¹ along $\vec{B}$. Using the full $v$ instead of $v_\perp$ would give 1.56 cm — a factor of two out.

**4. Two long parallel wires 10 cm apart carry 3 A and 5 A in opposite directions. Find the force per unit length and state whether it is attractive or repulsive.**
$\dfrac{F}{\ell} = \dfrac{\mu_0 I_1I_2}{2\pi d} = \dfrac{4\pi\times 10^{-7}\times 15}{2\pi \times 0.1} = \dfrac{6\pi\times 10^{-6}}{0.2\pi} = 3.0\times 10^{-5}$ N m⁻¹.
Repulsive, because the currents are opposite. The magnitude is the same as it would be for same-direction currents — the direction of the currents changes the sign of the force, not its size.

**5. An electron of mass $9.11\times 10^{-31}$ kg and charge $1.602\times 10^{-19}$ C moves at $2\times 10^6$ m s⁻¹ perpendicular to a field of 2 T. Find the radius and the time period, and comment on the direction of rotation.**
$r = \dfrac{mv}{qB} = \dfrac{9.11\times 10^{-31}\times 2\times 10^6}{1.602\times 10^{-19}\times 2} = \dfrac{1.822\times 10^{-24}}{3.204\times 10^{-19}} = 5.69\times 10^{-6}$ m.
$T = \dfrac{2\pi m}{qB} = \dfrac{2\pi\times 9.11\times 10^{-31}}{3.204\times 10^{-19}} = 1.79\times 10^{-11}$ s, so $f \approx 5.6\times 10^{10}$ Hz.
The radius is about 6 micrometres — nanometre-scale confinement, which is how a mass spectrometer resolves isotopes. The electron rotates in the opposite sense to a proton of the same speed in the same field, because the charge is negative; the radius is identical because only $|q|$ enters.

**6. A velocity selector has $E = 3\times 10^4$ V/m and $B = 0.6$ T. What speed passes undeflected, and what happens to ions of half that speed?**
$v = E/B = 3\times 10^4/0.6 = 5\times 10^4$ m s⁻¹.
For an ion at $2.5\times 10^4$ m s⁻¹ the magnetic force $qvB$ is only half the electric force $qE$, so the electric force dominates and the ion curves along the field lines toward the negative plate. Faster ions curve the other way. Only the selected speed survives the gap — that is the point of the device.

**7. A rectangular coil of 50 turns, area 0.1 m², carries 1 A in a field of 0.05 T. The normal to the coil makes 30° with the field. Find the torque and the interaction energy.**
$m = NIA = 50 \times 1 \times 0.1 = 5$ A·m².
$\tau = mB\sin 30° = 5 \times 0.05 \times 0.5 = 0.125$ N·m.
$U = -mB\cos 30° = -5 \times 0.05 \times 0.866 = -0.217$ J.
The energy is negative because the coil is not yet aligned and the field is doing positive work on it as it rotates. When $\theta = 0$ the torque is zero and $U = -0.25$ J, the minimum.

**8. A copper strip of width 2 mm and thickness 5 × 10⁻⁴ m carries 3 A in a field of 0.5 T perpendicular to its face. The carrier density is $8.5\times 10^{28}$ m⁻³. Find the Hall voltage.**
$A = wt = 2\times 10^{-3} \times 5\times 10^{-4} = 1.0\times 10^{-6}$ m².
$V_H = \dfrac{BI}{net} = \dfrac{0.5 \times 3}{8.5\times 10^{28}\times 1.602\times 10^{-19}\times 5\times 10^{-4}}$.
Denominator: $8.5\times 1.602\times 5 = 68.1$, so $6.81\times 10^{6}$.
$V_H = \dfrac{1.5}{6.81\times 10^{6}} = 2.2\times 10^{-7}$ V ≈ 0.22 μV.
The negative sign of the carriers in a metal is what makes this voltage negative with respect to the stated convention; the magnitude is the number that matters. Halving the thickness halves $V_H$, which is why thin foils are used as Hall probes.

### 💡 Pro Tips

1. **Write "θ is the angle between the area vector and B" on your working before substituting.** It is thirty seconds that prevents the most common wrong answer in torque questions.
2. **Use the right-hand rule to set up geometry before touching numbers.** A wrong diagram produces a wrong answer that still looks plausible; a right diagram makes the formula application routine.
3. **Breathe out $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}$.** Every formula in the chapter carries the $4\pi$ in the numerator and a $\pi$ in the denominator, so π cancels and the arithmetic is clean.
4. **Convert everything to SI first.** Radius in cm, field in gauss, or current in mA will ruin an otherwise correct solution; a missing $10^{-2}$ is the usual culprit.
5. **In velocity-selector and mass-spectrometer questions, do the balance of forces explicitly.** Writing $qE = qvB$ and then solving is faster and safer than trying to recall the final form.
6. **Check the radius formula for dimensionality.** $[mv/qB] = [kg\cdot m/s]/[C\cdot T] = m$, since $\text{T} = \text{kg}/(\text{C}\cdot\text{s})$. If your result is not in metres, something is misplaced.
7. **Learn only three direction rules and use them all.** Right-hand thumb for the field of a wire, right-hand grip for the pole or moment, Fleming left for the force. Three rules beat nine remembered special cases.
8. **Do the axis-of-a-loop and parallel-wires problems last in a paper, not first.** They are single-formula questions that will still be there when the harder items are done; there is no gain in spending early minutes on them.
