# General Biology and Physiology

Biology is 40 of the 100 items in the Survey of the Natural Sciences. The ADA's DAT Candidate Guide splits those 100 items into Biology (40 items), General Chemistry (30 items) and Organic Chemistry (30 items), and the section runs for 90 minutes inside a five-hour fifteen-minute administration of 280 items in total. The ADA publishes no per-topic weightage, so how those 40 items divide between cell biology, genetics, microbiology, immunology and the three organ systems is unknown. Treat the areas below as a coverage checklist, not a forecast.

What biology items on this section reward is mechanism rather than labels. When the stem asks why a structure is built a certain way, the answer is a reason, and a reason needs the structure, the job and the consequence of failure in that order. Candidates who memorise a list of organelles lose marks on those stems; candidates who can say what each structure is for and what breaks without it answer the same stems in a sentence.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

**Every biology item is one of four verbs, and they come in order.** Name it, track it, explain it, predict it.

| The move | The question it produces | What you build before answering |
|---|---|---|
| Name | What is this structure, cell, pathogen or hormone? | the vocabulary for the structure |
| Track | Where does this substance go, and what happens at each step? | the pathway or circuit |
| Explain | Why is it built this way, and what fails without it? | structure → function → consequence |
| Predict | If this changes, what happens downstream? | the feedback loop |

**The eight areas, and the one thing that earns marks in each.**

| Area | The fact that carries the items |
|---|---|
| Cell biology | Structure follows function; every organelle exists because a job has to get done |
| Genetics and inheritance | A phenotype follows from a genotype through a stated rule, and a pedigree pattern names the inheritance type |
| Microbiology | Bacteria are prokaryotes with a peptidoglycan wall; viruses have no metabolism of any kind |
| Immunology | The innate response is fast and non-specific; the adaptive response is slow, specific and remembers |
| Anatomy and physiology | Homeostasis is negative feedback — the response opposes the change that triggered it |
| Digestive system | Enzymes are substrate-specific, and absorption happens where surface area and blood flow are greatest |
| Nervous system | An action potential is all-or-none, and myelin is what makes conduction fast |
| Endocrine system | Hormones act through receptors, and release is controlled by feedback rather than by nerve signals alone |

**Ten facts worth a mark each.**

- Mitochondria carry their own DNA and replicate independently of the cell cycle.
- Ribosomes come in two sizes: 70S in prokaryotes, 80S in eukaryotes.
- The electron transport chain and ATP synthase sit in the inner mitochondrial membrane; the Krebs cycle runs in the matrix.
- Both DNA and RNA are synthesised 5' to 3', and the ribosome reads mRNA 5' to 3'.
- Mitosis keeps the chromosome number; meiosis halves it.
- Replication is semiconservative — every new DNA molecule keeps one old strand and gains one new one.
- Bacteria and archaea are both prokaryotes, but only bacteria have peptidoglycan in the wall.
- Red blood cells have no nucleus and no mitochondria, and survive for roughly 120 days.
- Insulin comes from the beta cells of the islets of Langerhans; glucagon comes from the alpha cells.
- ADH increases water reabsorption in the collecting duct; aldosterone increases sodium reabsorption and potassium excretion.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Cell biology and membranes

| Organelle | Its job | What breaks without it |
|---|---|---|
| Nucleus | Stores DNA and runs transcription | No new ribosomes; the cell cannot replace lost proteins |
| Ribosome | Builds polypeptide from mRNA | No protein synthesis |
| Rough endoplasmic reticulum | Folds and ships secreted proteins | Antibodies and digestive enzymes cannot leave the cell |
| Golgi apparatus | Modifies, sorts and packages | Secreted material reaches the membrane unprocessed |
| Lysosome | Hydrolytic enzymes, acidic pH | Engulfed material accumulates; the cell swells |
| Mitochondrion | ATP production by oxidative phosphorylation | No usable ATP from glucose |
| Peroxisome | Breaks down fatty acids and hydrogen peroxide | Very-long-chain fatty acids accumulate |

| Transport | Direction | Energy needed | Carrier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple diffusion | Down the gradient | No | None |
| Facilitated diffusion | Down the gradient | No | Protein channel or carrier |
| Osmosis | Water down its own gradient | No | Aquaporin channels |
| Active transport | Against the gradient | ATP | Pump protein |
| Endocytosis and exocytosis | Bulk materials in and out | ATP | Vesicle |

The two facts that carry the most items: the electron transport chain pumps protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane, and ATP synthase uses that gradient to make ATP — which is why uncouplers such as dinitrophenol are toxic; and the Krebs cycle feeds NADH and FADH₂ into that chain, so the cycle and the chain are one pathway split across two membranes.

Mitosis runs prophase (chromatin condenses, spindle forms, nuclear envelope breaks down), metaphase (chromosomes align at the metaphase plate), anaphase (sister chromatids separate towards opposite poles) and telophase, followed by cytokinesis.

#### Genetics and inheritance

| Pattern | Clue in the pedigree | Transmission |
|---|---|---|
| Autosomal dominant | Appears in every generation; affected parent and unaffected partner give about half affected | Both sexes, father to son possible |
| Autosomal recessive | Skips generations; affected child from unaffected parents | Both sexes |
| X-linked recessive | Mostly males; no father-to-son transmission; affected maternal grandfather to all daughters | Mother carries, sons affected |
| X-linked dominant | Affected father passes to all daughters and no sons | Both sexes |

Hardy–Weinberg gives p² + 2pq + q² = 1 for the homozygous dominant, heterozygous and homozygous recessive proportions, and it holds only in a large, randomly mating population with no selection, mutation or migration.

The central dogma is DNA → RNA → protein. Transcription copies a gene into messenger RNA; translation reads that message on a ribosome, three bases at a time. Mutations divide into point mutations — silent, missense and nonsense — and frameshift mutations, where an insertion or deletion shifts every codon after it, which is why a single-base deletion is far more damaging than a point substitution.

#### Microbiology

Bacterial identification rests on shape and wall. Gram-positive bacteria have a thick peptidoglycan layer, retain the crystal violet stain and appear purple; Gram-negative bacteria have a thin layer, an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharide and take up the counterstain, appearing pink or red. Shapes are cocci (spherical), bacilli (rod-shaped) and spirilla (spiral).

Viruses differ from bacteria in every way an item can test: no metabolism, no ribosomes, no independent replication, and they multiply only inside a host cell. A bacteriophage with a capsid and no envelope is a naked virus; the enveloped ones carry glycoproteins on the surface that decide which cells they can enter.

Sterilisation destroys every microbe including spores, and autoclaving or incineration do it. Disinfection kills most organisms on a surface, and bleach, alcohol and quaternary ammonium compounds do that job.

Antibiotics act on the machinery bacteria have and we do not: the cell wall (penicillins and cephalosporins), protein synthesis at the ribosome (macrolides, aminoglycosides, tetracyclines), the cell membrane (polymyxins) and nucleic acid synthesis (quinolones, rifampin). Resistance comes from enzymes that destroy the drug, from altered targets, and from pumps that eject it.

#### Immunology

Innate immunity is the wall: skin and mucosa, phagocytes, complement and inflammation. It acts within minutes and does not improve with exposure. Adaptive immunity takes days, is specific to an epitope, and leaves memory cells behind.

B cells mature into plasma cells that secrete antibody and into memory cells that respond faster on a second exposure. Cytotoxic T cells kill infected cells. Helper T cells coordinate everything else, activating B cells and cytotoxic T cells. That is why an item describing a patient with no antibody response at all usually points at helper T-cell loss rather than at a B-cell defect: without helper T cells, B cells are never switched on.

| Antibody | Where it is found | Its job |
|---|---|---|
| IgM | First antibody made in a primary response | Activates complement; a marker of recent infection |
| IgG | Most of the serum | Neutralises, opsonises, and crosses the placenta |
| IgA | Secretions, breast milk | Protects mucosal surfaces and the infant gut |
| IgE | Mast cell surface | Triggers allergy and anaphylaxis |
| IgD | Surface of a naive B cell | Acts as the antigen receptor |

Vaccination works through memory cells, which is why a booster matters and why the response to a second exposure is faster and stronger.

#### Anatomy, physiology and homeostasis

The four tissue types are epithelial (linings and barriers, avascular, resting on connective tissue), connective (support and matrix), muscle (contractile) and nervous (conducting).

Homeostasis is negative feedback. Body temperature, blood glucose and blood pressure all run on the same loop: a receptor detects a deviation, a control centre compares it against a set point, an effector acts, and the change reverses the deviation. Positive feedback amplifies instead — uterine contractions in labour, platelet aggregation in clotting, and the opening of potassium channels in an action potential.

Blood is plasma plus formed elements. Red cells carry haemoglobin and therefore oxygen and part of the carbon dioxide; they have no nucleus and no mitochondria and live about 120 days. Platelets are cell fragments that initiate clotting. White cells divide into neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils and basophils, and the first three are the ones that appear in most items.

In the urinary system, filtration happens in the glomerulus, most reabsorption happens in the proximal tubule, and the loop of Henle builds the medullary osmotic gradient that allows urine to be concentrated. ADH acts on the collecting duct to insert water channels.

#### Digestive system

Mechanical digestion is chewing, churning and peristalsis. Chemical digestion is enzymes, stomach acid and bile.

| Enzyme | Breaks | Where it works |
|---|---|---|
| Salivary amylase | Starch to maltose | Mouth, then small intestine |
| Pepsin | Protein to peptides | Stomach, at acidic pH |
| Trypsin and chymotrypsin | Protein to peptides | Small intestine, from the pancreas |
| Pancreatic lipase | Fat to fatty acids and monoglycerides | Small intestine |
| Maltase, peptidases | Disaccharides and peptides to monomers | Brush border of the small intestine |

Absorption is built around surface area and blood flow. The villi and microvilli multiply the surface of the small intestine, and the two routes out differ: sugars and amino acids enter the blood capillaries and travel to the liver through the hepatic portal vein, while fats are packaged into chylomicrons and enter the lacteals and the lymphatic system. An item that asks why liver enzymes rise after a meal is testing the portal route.

The liver makes bile and albumin, stores glycogen, and converts ammonia to urea. The pancreas supplies bicarbonate and digestive enzymes and houses the islets of Langerhans. The large intestine absorbs water and electrolytes and hosts bacteria that ferment residue and make vitamin K.

#### Nervous system

A neuron carries signals from dendrites and the cell body down an axon to its terminals, and myelin from oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system or Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system insulates the axon so that the action potential jumps between nodes of Ranvier.

The resting membrane potential sits near −70 mV, maintained by the sodium–potassium ATPase and by potassium leak channels. An action potential is threshold-dependent and all-or-none: sodium influx depolarises, potassium efflux repolarises, and a refractory period follows so the signal cannot run backwards. Neurotransmitters cross the synapse and are cleared by enzymes such as acetylcholinesterase or by reuptake.

The reflex arc is the item that pays best: receptor → sensory neuron → spinal cord → interneuron → motor neuron → effector, with the brain informed but not initiating the response. The central nervous system is the brain and spinal cord; the peripheral system is everything else, split into somatic (skeletal muscle) and autonomic, and the autonomic split runs sympathetic (fight or flight) against parasympathetic (rest and digest).

#### Endocrine system

| Hormone | Source | Main effect |
|---|---|---|
| Insulin | Beta cells of the islets | Lowers blood glucose: GLUT4 uptake in muscle and fat, glycogen synthesis in liver and muscle |
| Glucagon | Alpha cells of the islets | Raises blood glucose: glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis in the liver |
| ADH | Hypothalamus, released by the posterior pituitary | Water reabsorption in the collecting duct |
| Oxytocin | Hypothalamus, released by the posterior pituitary | Uterine contraction and milk ejection |
| Thyroxine (T3, T4) | Thyroid | Raises metabolic rate; iodine is required to make it |
| Parathyroid hormone | Parathyroid | Raises blood calcium from bone, kidney and vitamin D activation |
| Calcitonin | C cells of the thyroid | Lowers blood calcium |
| Cortisol | Adrenal cortex | Gluconeogenesis, protein breakdown, immunosuppression |
| Aldosterone | Adrenal cortex | Sodium reabsorption and potassium excretion in the distal nephron |
| Adrenaline | Adrenal medulla | Fight-or-flight: heart rate, bronchodilation, glycogenolysis |

Hormones fall into two chemical classes, and the class predicts the mechanism. Peptide hormones are water-soluble, cannot cross the membrane, and act through a membrane receptor with a second messenger inside. Steroid hormones are lipid-soluble, enter the cell, and act through intracellular receptors that change gene expression. Catecholamines sit between the two: they are amino acid derivatives but act at membrane receptors.

Release is controlled by feedback. Cortisol feeds back to suppress ACTH, thyroxine feeds back to suppress TSH, and insulin and glucagon feed back on blood glucose. Oxytocin in labour is the rare positive feedback loop.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Worked item 1 — a pedigree that names the inheritance type

**Stem.** A mother and a father, both clinically normal, have four children: two sons, both affected by a bleeding disorder; two daughters, both clinically normal. The mother's father was affected by the same disorder; the mother's mother was not.

**Question.** What is the inheritance pattern, and what is the probability that a son born to one of the daughters will be affected?

**Step 1 — recessive or dominant?** Two clinically normal parents have affected children, so the trait is recessive: affected individuals have no working copy, so both parents must carry one.

**Step 2 — autosomal or X-linked?** Every affected individual in this family is male, and the affected grandfather is on the maternal side. That pattern fits an X-linked recessive trait: the affected grandfather passed his X chromosome to every daughter, making the mother a carrier, and she passed the affected X to her sons.

**Step 3 — why the daughters are unaffected.** The father contributes a normal X to every daughter, so no daughter can have two affected copies. Each daughter therefore has a 50 per cent chance of being a carrier.

**Step 4 — the probability asked for.** For any given daughter: 50 per cent chance she is a carrier, and if she is, 50 per cent chance she passes the affected X to a son. So the probability that a son of a daughter is affected is one half times one half, or one in four.

**Step 5 — the transmission rule that explains the pedigree.** A father passes his Y chromosome to his sons, never his X, so an X-linked trait cannot pass from father to son.

> ⚡ **Examiner Trap.** Answering one half for the daughter's son and forgetting the daughter's own one-in-two chance of being a carrier. The two halves multiply. The second trap is concluding "autosomal recessive" because both parents are unaffected, which ignores the sex pattern and the affected grandfather.

#### Worked item 2 — two sensory pathways, two crossing points

**Question.** A patient has a spinal cord lesion on the left side at the lumbar level. Below the lesion, pain and temperature sensation is lost on the right side of the body, while vibration and position sense are lost on the left side of the body below the lesion.

**Step 1 — trace the pain and temperature pathway.** Fibres enter the dorsal horn, synapse within one or two segments, cross in the anterior white commissure and then ascend in the contralateral spinothalamic tract. A left-sided lesion therefore produces loss of pain and temperature on the **right** below the level, because the fibres have already crossed.

**Step 2 — trace the vibration pathway.** Fine touch, vibration and proprioception ascend ipsilaterally in the dorsal columns and do not cross until they reach the medulla, where they decussate into the medial lemniscus. A left-sided spinal lesion therefore produces loss of vibration on the **left** below the lesion.

**Step 3 — name the pattern.** Loss of one modality on the same side and another on the opposite side, below the lesion, is the pattern of a hemicord injury.

> ⚡ **Examiner Trap.** Assuming the two modalities travel together because both are "sensation". Their crossing points differ, and that is exactly why the losses fall on different sides. The second trap is forgetting the short crossing segment for pain and temperature: a lesion within one or two segments of the entry level affects the crossing fibres before they have ascended.

#### Worked item 3 — an endocrine defect read from two hormones

**Question.** A patient has fasting hyperglycaemia, weight loss, polyuria and polyphagia. Insulin is low and C-peptide is low. Glucagon is high. Where is the defect?

**Step 1 — what C-peptide tells you.** Beta cells synthesise proinsulin and cleave it into insulin and C-peptide, then secrete both together in equal amounts. C-peptide is therefore a marker of the body's own insulin production.

**Step 2 — read the pair.** Low insulin with low C-peptide means the gland is making too little of both. That is a failure of insulin secretion from the beta cells, not insulin resistance and not an injected insulin. With injected insulin the pattern is the opposite: the C-peptide would be low because the injected molecule carries none with it, but the insulin level would be high.

**Step 3 — check the counter-regulatory hormone.** Glucagon should fall when blood glucose is high, because the alpha cells read the same feedback signal the beta cells do. That it stays high shows the alpha cells are no longer responding to it. The raised glucagon is an inappropriate response to the hyperglycaemia, not the cause of it.

**Step 4 — name the consequence.** Without insulin, muscle and adipose tissue cannot take up glucose through GLUT4, the liver keeps producing glucose by gluconeogenesis, and the excess spills into the urine — which is the polyuria and the weight loss.

> ⚡ **Examiner Trap.** Diagnosing insulin resistance, which is the commonest cause of hyperglycaemia and would come with a normal or high C-peptide. The second trap is reading the raised glucagon as the primary defect. Third trap: assuming the injected-insulin case also produces a low C-peptide, and using that to argue the two are indistinguishable.

#### Mitosis and meiosis, side by side

| | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Occurs in | Somatic cells | Germ cells in the gonads |
| Divisions | One | Two (meiosis I and II) |
| Daughter cells | Two | Four |
| Chromosome number | Same as the parent | Half the parent |
| Genetic identity | Identical, barring mutation | Varied by crossing over and independent assortment |
| Where the variation comes from | Mutation only | Crossing over in prophase I and random assortment in metaphase I |

| Cells involved | Neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells, complement | B cells, T cells, antibodies |

#### ⚡ Quick Exam Checklist

- Move identified (name, track, explain, predict) before any answer is chosen
- Structure, function and consequence given together when a stem asks why
- Transport questions answered with the gradient and the energy source, not just the name
- Mitosis and meiosis kept apart on chromosome number and on where variation comes from
- Pedigree patterns checked against sex distribution and against father-to-son transmission
- C-peptide read as the marker of endogenous insulin secretion, never as an afterthought
- Crossing points of the spinothalamic and dorsal column pathways recalled before any lesion question
- Antibody class matched to its location and its function
- Enzyme matched to its substrate and its site of action
- Negative feedback distinguished from positive feedback before predicting a hormonal change
- Gram-positive and Gram-negative matched to their wall structure and their stain colour
