# Issue Essay — GRE Analytical Writing Notes

The Issue task asks you to take a position on a general statement about society, education, technology or research, and to defend it. You get **30 minutes** and a choice between two prompts. The task is not a test of your opinions — it is a test of whether you can state a position, support it with reasons and examples, and acknowledge the strongest objection to it. An essay that argues one side relentlessly without ever meeting the counter-argument scores lower than one that concedes a real limitation and then explains why the position still holds.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

**Format facts worth memorising**

- **Analytical Writing is one section with two separately timed tasks**: the **Analyze an Issue** task and the **Analyze an Argument** task, **30 minutes each**.
- The Issue task gives you **two prompts and you choose one**. Choose within the first minute — deliberating longer costs writing time.
- The score runs **0–6 in half-point increments**. It is reported separately from the Verbal and Quantitative scores and does **not** affect your 130–170 section scores.
- Scoring combines a trained human reader with an automated scoring engine. Both respond to structure, relevance and control of language — not to length alone.

**The one structural rule**

Every Issue essay needs four things, in order: a **clear position** stated in the first paragraph, **two or three body paragraphs each built on one reason**, at least one paragraph that **engages the strongest counter-argument**, and a **conclusion that restates the position in light of what you conceded**. An essay missing the concession paragraph reads as unaware rather than confident.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

**A 30-minute clock that works**

- **Minutes 0–3 — choose and plan.** Pick the prompt you can support with concrete examples. Write a three-line outline: position, two reasons, one counter-argument.
- **Minutes 3–7 — introduction.** State the position directly. Do not open with "Since the dawn of time". Name the tension the prompt raises, then say where you stand.
- **Minutes 7–22 — body.** Two or three paragraphs, each with a topic sentence, a specific example, and one sentence explaining why the example supports the position.
- **Minutes 22–27 — concession.** Present the strongest objection fairly, then answer it. This paragraph is what separates a 4 from a 5.
- **Minutes 27–30 — conclusion and proofread.** Restate the position; fix obvious errors. A rushed conclusion costs less than a missing one.

**How to generate examples under pressure**

Prepare four or five **versatile case studies** in advance — one from science or medicine, one from technology, one from history or politics, one from education, one from business. Almost any prompt can be answered with one of them. For each case study, memorise four facts: what happened, when, who was affected, and what the outcome was. Specificity is what the readers reward; an essay full of abstractions scores in the middle regardless of its logic.

**The concession paragraph, written properly**

A weak concession says "some people disagree". A strong one names the actual objection: "The clearest objection is that this reasoning proves too much — the same argument would justify restricting any activity that carries risk." Then answer it: "But the comparison fails because the risk here is involuntary and borne by third parties, which is not true of the cases the objection cites." Name the objection, then show why it does not defeat you.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

**Where the marks actually leak**

- **Agreeing with the prompt without argument.** A restatement of the prompt is not a position. The readers look for a claim you would defend against someone who disagrees.
- **Straw-manning the counter-argument.** Presenting a weak version of the objection and knocking it down scores worse than not addressing it, because it shows you could not find the real difficulty.
- **All-or-nothing framing.** Prompts often contain words like *only*, *always*, *never* or *the best*. A strong essay usually **qualifies**: the claim holds under these conditions, fails under those. Qualified positions are easier to defend and read as more sophisticated.
- **Example recycling without analysis.** Naming a case is not supporting a position. Every example needs one sentence stating *why it counts as evidence for this particular claim*.
- **Running out of time mid-paragraph.** A complete short essay outscores an incomplete long one. If time is short, cut a body paragraph rather than the concession or the conclusion.

**Language control that the readers actually reward**

Vary sentence length deliberately — a short sentence after two long ones lands. Avoid the essay's two commonest crutches: *very* as an intensifier and *In today's society* as an opener. Use precise verbs (*undermines*, *sustains*, *overstates*, *underwrites*) rather than *is* plus an adjective. Do not pad for length; a tightly argued 500-word essay outscores a repetitive 800-word one, because the readers score the reasoning they can find.

**A four-week drill schedule**

- **Week 1 — case studies.** Build five versatile case studies, four memorised facts each. This is preparation, not writing.
- **Week 2 — outlines only.** Take ten prompts and produce a position, two reasons and one counter-argument for each, in five minutes. No full essays.
- **Week 3 — full essays under time.** Two 30-minute essays per sitting, one from each content area. Read them back and mark the sentence where the position is stated — if you cannot find it, neither could a reader.
- **Week 4 — revision pass.** Rewrite your weakest essay from Week 3, tightening the concession paragraph. Improvement here moves the score most.
