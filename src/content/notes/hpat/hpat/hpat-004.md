# Written English

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute HPAT-Ireland revision before the ACER sitting.

**Written English** — Quick Facts for UL MSc Occupational Therapy and MSc Speech and Language Therapy applicants only

**What is Section 4?**
Section 4 is the fourth and final HPAT-Ireland section, but it is **required only for applicants to the University of Limerick MSc Occupational Therapy and MSc Speech and Language Therapy programmes**. Standard medicine applicants at RCSI, UCD, TCD and UL complete only Sections 1-3 (and do not sit Section 4). Section 4 consists of two short written pieces assessed automatically against linguistic criteria. It is administered by ACER on behalf of the participating universities.

**At a glance:**
- **Two short written tasks** — both submitted in the same HPAT sitting window
- **UL applicants only** — required only for MSc OT and MSc SLT (UL); NOT required for medicine applicants at any participating university
- **Marked automatically** against linguistic criteria (idea development, structure, expression, mechanics)
- **No curriculum content** — preparation is short timed written pieces, not subject-matter research
- **Smaller weight** in the HPAT-Ireland composite (weight 1; Sections 1-3 carry weights 5/4/4)

**Section applicability reference (verify on hpat-ireland.acer.org before each cycle):**
| Section | Skill | Required for | Medicine / OT / SLT |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Logical Reasoning & Problem Solving | All applicants | Medicine + UL OT + UL SLT |
| 2 | Interpersonal Understanding | All applicants | Medicine + UL OT + UL SLT |
| 3 | Non-Verbal Reasoning | All applicants | Medicine + UL OT + UL SLT |
| 4 | Written English | UL OT / SLT only | UL OT + UL SLT only |

**Time budget rule:** Section 4 must be planned carefully. Most candidates applying to medicine do NOT sit Section 4. If a candidate applies to both medicine AND UL OT/SLT, the HPAT-Ireland composite includes Section 4 weighted only for the OT/SLT consideration. Plan 30-45 minutes of Section 4 practice for any UL OT/SLT applicant in the final two weeks.

### 🟡 Standard — Detailed Review (1d–2w)
> A working understanding for steady preparation across a few weeks.

**Who sits Section 4, and who does not**
HPAT-Ireland Section 4 is taken only by applicants to specific UL programmes:
- **MSc Occupational Therapy at UL** — Section 4 is required as part of the HPAT composite
- **MSc Speech and Language Therapy at UL** — Section 4 is required as part of the HPAT composite
- **Undergraduate medicine at RCSI, UCD, TCD or UL** — Section 4 is NOT taken; the HPAT composite for medicine applicants covers Sections 1-3 only

If a candidate applies to medicine AND to UL OT or UL SLT, they sit Section 4 as part of the same test window, but only the OT/SLT programme considers the Section 4 score. The medicine application at RCSI/UCD/TCD/UL does not weight Section 4.

**Why Section 4 preparation looks different from Sections 1-3**
Sections 1-3 reward repeated timed practice on diagram and passage-style questions. Section 4 is structurally different — it is two short written pieces marked automatically against linguistic criteria. The productive pattern:

1. **Two short written pieces** — Section 4 contains two short written tasks, each completed in the same HPAT sitting window after Sections 1-3. Both are assessed automatically against linguistic criteria (idea development, structure, expression, mechanics) — not by a panel.
2. **Marked against linguistic criteria** — the automatic marking rubric covers, in order: idea development (does the response show a clear line of reasoning or argument?), structure (paragraphing, linking, cohesion), expression (vocabulary, sentence variety, register), and mechanics (spelling, grammar, syntax). Each criterion carries a comparable weight.
4. **Practise timed short written pieces** — the preparation pattern is 30-45 minutes of focused written-piece practice per week for two weeks before the sitting. Short written pieces (300-500 words each) under simulated timing give the best Section 4 score leverage.
5. **Read widely and across registers** — Section 4 marks down expression that is repetitive. Reading widely across training formats (editorials, review articles, op-eds, professional blogs) primes vocabulary and sentence variety.

**The key difference between Sections 1-3 and Section 4 preparation**
Sections 1-3 are psychometric — they measure pattern recognition, spatial reasoning and interpersonal inference. Section 4 measures a writing ability. Preparation that improves Sections 1-3 (timed diagram practice, error-log review) does not necessarily improve Section 4, and vice versa. Candidates applying to both medicine and UL OT/SLT must budget separate time for Section 4 in the final two weeks.

**Linguistic criteria covered:**
- Idea development (a clear line of reasoning or argument throughout the response)
- Structure (paragraphing, linking, cohesion, intro/conclusion)
- Expression (vocabulary range, sentence variety, register, audience-fit)
- Mechanics (spelling, grammar, syntax, punctuation)

**Common pitfalls:**
- Repeating the same sentence pattern (marks down expression)
- Weak paragraph structure (marks down ideas and structure)
- Verbatim repetition of words from the prompt (marks down expression)
- Long, run-on sentences rather than varied sentence lengths
- Inconsistent register (sliding from formal to casual)
- Misspelled words or wrong grammar (marks down mechanics)

### High-Yield Topics

**Topics to drill down in detail, in priority order:**

1. **Idea development** - the rubric's largest and most signal-bearing criterion. A response with a clear, sustained line of reasoning or argument scores well; one that lists facts without a sustained line scores lower. The skill is to commit to one central idea per response and develop it across paragraphs.

2. **Structure and cohesion** - paragraphs follow the central idea, with linking words and a clear intro/conclusion. The skill is to plan a three-paragraph response (claim → support → implication) before writing, then revise for cohesion.

3. **Expression and sentence variety** - varied sentence lengths and vocabulary; appropriate register for a professional healthcare application. The skill is to draft, then revise for variety.

5. **Mechanics and conventions** - spelling, grammar, punctuation, syntax. Section 4 is marked automatically against the linguistic-criteria rubric; mechanics errors are flagged reliably and reduce scores.

7. **Timed written-piece practice** - 30-45 minute weekly blocks of timed short written pieces in the two weeks before the sitting. Section 4 rewards volume of timed practice more than any other preparation pattern.

9. **Audience-fit and register** - responses are read by an automatic marker scoring against linguistic criteria; the productive register is professional healthcare without being overly academic. The skill is to read widely and adapt register.

10. **Common error patterns and how to spot them** - sentence-pattern repetition, paragraph cohesion failure, register slip. Build an error-log of one-line notes from practice pieces.

### Previous Year Patterns

**Recurring Section 4 patterns in ACER materials (qualitative, not quantitative):**

1. **Two short written tasks** - both written tasks are short (300-500 words each). Each task tests a different dimension of the rubric (one tests argument development; one tests expression and register).

2. **Linguistic criteria are the only marking axis** - automatic marking against the rubric is the only scoring signal. Subject-matter knowledge does not contribute to the score.

3. **Re-marking rules** - re-marking is not available for Section 4. The automatic-marking process is the only score; if a candidate believes their response was mis-marked, there is no human re-mark.

4. **Section 4 does not contribute to medicine composite** - the medicine composite scores for RCSI, UCD, TCD, UL medicine only Sections 1-3. Section 4 is invisible to medicine selection committees at those universities. Candidates applying only to medicine should not sit Section 4 (confirm on hpat-ireland.acer.org before each cycle).

**Pattern to watch:** Section 4 is structurally different from Sections 1-3. Candidates often underprepare it because Sections 1-3 take most of the practice time, especially for medicine applicants. The productive pattern is to budget 30-45 minutes of Section 4 practice per week for the two weeks before the sitting, alongside the Section 1-3 programme.

### Pro Tips

1. **Use the official ACER Practice Tests first.** They are the only published real past questions and the only authoritative source of section style. Work through them after the practice-question and practice-placement stages described on the ACER preparation page.

2. **Section 4 is for UL OT/SLT only.** Confirm on hpat-ireland.acer.org before each cycle whether your target programme requires Section 4. Medicine applicants at RCSI, UCD, TCD or UL do NOT sit Section 4.

3. **Section 4 is marked automatically against linguistic criteria.** The automatic rubric covers idea development, structure, expression and mechanics. Plan each response around these four criteria in that order of weight.

4. **Re-marking is not available.** If you believe your response was mis-marked, there is no re-mark process. The automatic mark is final.

5. **Practise timed short written pieces.** Section 4 preparation is 30-45 minute weekly blocks of timed short written pieces (300-500 words each) for the two weeks before the sitting. Volume of timed practice is the largest improvement lever.

6. **Plan before writing.** For each task, spend 3-5 minutes outlining the three-paragraph structure (claim → support → implication) before writing. A 3-paragraph response with a clear central idea scores higher than a stream-of-consciousness response.

7. **Vary sentence length and vocabulary.** A response with one long sentence pattern or one narrow vocabulary range marks down on expression. After drafting, revise for variety.

8. **Read widely across registers.** Editorials, review articles, op-eds and professional blogs all help build vocabulary and sentence variety. Read at least 5 minutes per day across these formats for the two-week run-up.

9. **Don't bring outside knowledge to Section 4.** Section 4 is marked on linguistic criteria, not subject-matter content. Don't fill your response with factual claims that don't develop a line of reasoning.

10. **Confirm Section 4 in the live Information Booklet before each cycle.** ACER updates the booklet annually. Verify the live specification on hpat-ireland.acer.org before committing a revision plan to a student.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Dive (5/10 — how the automatic marker actually reads Section 4)

> Section 4 is the only HPAT-Ireland section with no ground truth you can grind against.
> Sections 1–3 reward volume; Section 4 rewards four separable habits. Read this tier
> when the yellow tier is no longer enough to explain *why* a piece scored what it did.

#### The four criteria are four independent detectors, not one impression

The automatic marker scores idea development, structure, expression and mechanics
**separately**. That matters, because it means a piece can be strong on one axis and
still lose the band on another — and it means you can attack them one at a time.

| Criterion | What it rewards | What it punishes | Cheapest fix in a 5-minute edit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Idea development | One central claim carried forward through every paragraph | A list of facts with no line of reasoning | Delete your weakest paragraph and add a second supporting sentence to your strongest |
| Structure | Paragraphs that each do one job, joined by explicit links | Four paragraphs that all restart from scratch | Write your topic sentence above each paragraph before drafting; revise so consecutive paragraphs start with *However / This matters because / By contrast* |
| Expression | Varied sentence length, vocabulary beyond the prompt's, a stable professional register | One repeated sentence pattern; words lifted verbatim from the prompt | Read the draft aloud and underline every sentence over 35 words; break one in half |
| Mechanics | Correct spelling, agreement, tense, punctuation | Any consistent error pattern, which the marker flags reliably | Read once only for errors, not for meaning |

#### Examiner Trap — the paraphrase trap costs both idea development and expression

The single most common Section 4 error is answering the *topic* instead of the *question*.
A piece that defines what community transport is, then explains why it matters generally,
has shown the marker that you can write — and has not developed an idea. Idea development
asks whether **your** line of reasoning holds together. Anchor every paragraph to the exact
wording of the task, and if a paragraph could be pasted into an answer to a different task,
it is not doing work.

#### Worked walkthrough — one response, marked on all four axes

Take the claim: *the real barrier to cycling in a mid-sized town is not the absence of
infrastructure but the absence of continuity.*

1. **Claim** — the idea is a real argument with a counter-intuitive "not X but Y" shape.
   Idea development is already ahead of an average response before the second paragraph.
2. **Support** — describe what a discontinuous network actually costs a rider: a usable
   route that dead-ends at a junction forces an experienced cyclist off-road, while a
   novice simply stops riding. This is *reasoning*, not description.
3. **Implication** — for a health profession, continuity is a population-health problem
   rather than an engineering one: the same gap that stops a rider also delays a
   rehabilitation appointment. This is what lifts the piece into the top band.

**The mark-up that matters:** compare that with a piece that opens by defining what a
cycle lane is, then lists benefits. Same vocabulary range, similar length, but three
paragraphs doing the same job and none carrying an argument. That is the gap the marker
reads on idea development, and no amount of vocabulary polish closes it.

> **Tip:** write the topic sentence of every paragraph on a separate line before you draft
> the paragraph. If two topic sentences could be swapped between paragraphs without damage,
> your structure has not been planned — it has only been ordered.

#### The sentence-variety ladder (the cheapest expression gain available)

| Length | Job it does | Where to place it |
|---|---|---|
| Under 8 words | Assertion or transition | Opening a paragraph, or bridging two ideas |
| 12–20 words | Statement of fact or reasoning | The body of every paragraph |
| 25–35 words | A claim with a qualifier — the load-bearing sentence | Once per paragraph, at the end of the argument |
| Over 40 words | Almost always a drafting failure | Break it into two sentences |

A response where every sentence sits in the 12–20 band reads as monotonous to the marker
even when the content is correct. Deliberately place one short sentence and one long one
per paragraph.

#### Timed practice protocol for the final 14 days

1. **Days 14–8** — one 20-minute block. Read an editorial or op-ed for 10 minutes, then
   write 300–400 words answering a task of your own construction, borrowing its argument
   but not its wording.
2. **Days 7–4** — one 30-minute block. Work through the ACER practice material in full
   simulation: no dictionary, no pausing, then apply the 3–5 minute outline step from the
   yellow tier before each piece.
3. **Days 3–1** — one 30-minute block. Two pieces back to back, timed, with a hard stop.
   The second piece is where planning discipline actually gets tested.
4. **Every session** — self-mark against all four criteria before reading the model answer.
   Mark yourself on each axis separately, because that is how the automatic marker reads.

#### Self-marking when no human marker exists

Since re-marking is not available, the only feedback loop you control is a structured
self-mark. Use three passes, in this order, and never mix them:

- **Pass 1 — mechanics only.** Correct every spelling, agreement, tense and full-stop error.
  Do not comment on argument. Do not fix structure.
- **Pass 2 — structure only.** Check that each paragraph has exactly one job and that the
  ordering earns its place. Mark the transitions with `→`.
- **Pass 3 — expression only.** Read aloud. Underline repeated sentence patterns. Circle
  any word lifted verbatim from the prompt. Cut one sentence from every paragraph that
  repeats an idea already made.

Running the passes in this order prevents the common failure of rewriting the argument
when the actual defect was a missing comma — which costs mechanics marks while leaving the
real, invisible structure problem in place for the next sitting.
