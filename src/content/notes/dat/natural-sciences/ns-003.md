# Biochemistry and Metabolism

Biochemistry is not a separate section of the DAT. It sits inside the Survey of the Natural Sciences, which the ADA's 2026 Candidate Guide splits into 100 items: Biology (40 items), General Chemistry (30 items) and Organic Chemistry (30 items). Inside the Biology block, the guide's first content area, Cell and Molecular Biology, is the one that names "cell metabolism (including photosynthesis, enzymology)" alongside "biomolecules, and integrated relationships". So this topic is the biology half of your largest section, and it is best attacked as chemistry you already know, plus the biology that hangs off it.

Two mechanics from the same guide shape how the items are written. The section runs for 90 minutes inside a total administration of five hours and 15 minutes. The ADA also states that both the US customary system and the metric system of measurement are used on the examination. Biochemistry is where that bites: an item can hand you a concentration in millimoles and a free energy in kilocalories, and expect you to work in either system without warning you which.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

**Every biochemistry item is one of five moves.** Structure, mechanism, regulation, defect, consequence. Decide which move the stem is making before you read the four options, because the same fact appears as the answer in one move and as a distractor in another.

**Memory hook — S-M-R-D-C.** S-structure (what is it built from), M-mechanism (which enzyme, which cofactor), R-regulation (which hormone switches it on or off), D-defect (which step is missing), C-consequence (what the patient shows).

| The stem asks | The answer lives in |
|---|---|
| What is this molecule made of? | Monomer identity, bond type, level of structure |
| Which enzyme or cofactor does this step need? | Cofactor table; reaction class |
| Why does the rate change with temperature or pH? | Active-site ionisation, denaturation, Km against Vmax |
| Which step is blocked in this patient? | The accumulated substrate and the missing enzyme |
| What does this hormone or drug do? | Competitive inhibition; insulin against glucagon |

**Ten facts worth a mark each.**
- ATP hydrolysis is commonly quoted at about −30.5 kJ/mol, roughly −7.3 kcal/mol.
- The peptide bond is covalent, and it is made by the ribosome.
- A–T pairs with two hydrogen bonds, G–C with three.
- NADH feeds the electron transport chain; NADPH funds reductive biosynthesis.
- Vitamin C is a cofactor for collagen hydroxylation, vitamin K for clotting factor carboxylation.
- Glycine is the only achiral amino acid; proline's nitrogen sits in a ring.
- Tryptophan and phenylalanine absorb ultraviolet light near 280 nm.
- Cysteine is the only amino acid carrying a thiol.
- PFK-1 is the committed step of glycolysis.
- Starch and glycogen are both glucose polymers built from α-1,4 linkages with α-1,6 branches, and glycogen branches far more often.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Bonds, buffers and the pKa you actually use

Three bond types carry the biology. Covalent bonds give proteins their primary structure and cannot be broken by heat. Ionic bonds hold the folded tertiary structure together at physiological pH, and hydrogen bonds give the secondary structure. That distinction explains why an enzyme denatures in heat but only a strong acid or base will actually hydrolyse the chain.

Buffers follow from one equation: pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]). A buffer works best when the pH sits near the pKa of its conjugate pair, and this is why the pH optimum of an enzyme is not an arbitrary number but the pH at which the active-site residues are correctly ionised. For a free amino acid the carboxyl group has a pKa near 2 and the amino group near 9 to 10, so at neutral pH the molecule is a zwitterion with no net charge. The isoelectric point of a simple amino acid sits at the average of the two pKa values. Histidine is the one to remember separately, because its imidazole side chain has a pKa near 6, which is why histidine sits at enzyme active sites at physiological pH.

#### Amino acids and the shape of a protein

Primary structure is the sequence, joined by peptide bonds. Secondary structure is the α-helix and the β-pleated sheet, held by backbone hydrogen bonds; in the helix the hydrogen bond runs from the carbonyl of residue i to the N–H of residue i+4. Tertiary structure is the folded three-dimensional shape, driven mainly by the hydrophobic effect with ionic, hydrogen-bond and disulfide interactions added on. Quaternary structure is the assembly of subunits, as in haemoglobin with its α and β pairs.

Two structural facts get tested directly. Proline breaks an α-helix because its ring nitrogen has no hydrogen to donate. Collagen is a triple helix of Gly-X-Y repeats, and the hydroxyproline that stabilises it is made by prolyl hydroxylase, which needs vitamin C. And the peptidyl transferase that forms peptide bonds is catalytic RNA, which is why ribosomes are described as ribozymes.

| Level | Held together by | What breaks it |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Peptide bonds (covalent) | Acid or base hydrolysis, not denaturation |
| Secondary | Backbone hydrogen bonds | Heat, extremes of pH |
| Tertiary | Side-chain packing, ionic bonds, disulfides | Heat, extremes of pH, reducing agents |
| Quaternary | Contacts between subunits | Same agents as tertiary |

#### Enzymes: classes, kinetics and what moves the rate

The six classes are worth having cold, because the class tells you the mechanism. Oxidoreductases move electrons and hydrogen, and their names end in "-dehydrogenase". Transferases move functional groups, and "-kinase" means phosphate is the group. Hydrolases use water to split a bond, so they include peptidases and lipases. Lyases remove groups to form or break a double bond without hydrolysis or oxidation. Isomerases rearrange a molecule in place. Ligases join two molecules together using ATP, and DNA ligase is the example.

Kinetics is the other half. The Michaelis–Menten equation, v = Vmax[S] / (Km + [S]), says two things: Km is the substrate concentration at which the rate is half of Vmax, and Vmax is the rate when the enzyme is saturated with substrate, which is why the curve flattens. The double-reciprocal Lineweaver–Burk plot converts that to a straight line, 1/v = (Km/Vmax)(1/[S]) + 1/Vmax, where the y-intercept is 1/Vmax and the x-intercept is −1/Km. Read the intercepts, not the slopes, when you identify an inhibitor.

Temperature and pH change rate for different reasons, and the shape of the curve tells you which. Temperature gives a rising limb as collision frequency rises, then a sudden fall once the protein denatures. pH gives a bell-shaped curve because ionisation of active-site residues matters on both sides of the optimum. Allosteric enzymes such as haemoglobin and PFK-1 do not follow Michaelis–Menten at all; they show a sigmoidal curve because of cooperativity.

#### Carbohydrate metabolism

Glycolysis is ten steps that take one glucose to two pyruvate, with a net gain of 2 ATP and 2 NADH. Three steps are irreversible and therefore the control points: hexokinase or glucokinase, phosphofructokinase-1 and pyruvate kinase. PFK-1 is the committed step, and it is the enzyme to name when an item asks what happens to glycolysis when ATP or citrate rises.

Hormones set the direction. Insulin, released by pancreatic beta cells, promotes glycolysis and glycogen synthesis. Glucagon from alpha cells and epinephrine from the adrenal medulla inhibit glycolysis and switch on gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis. In the liver, that switch runs through PFK-2 and fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, which are isoforms of the same bifunctional protein; phosphorylation turns one into the other. In muscle the same hormone response exists but muscle cannot export glucose, because it lacks glucose-6-phosphatase.

Pyruvate becomes acetyl-CoA through the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, which sits in the mitochondrion, is irreversible, and needs five cofactors: thiamine pyrophosphate, lipoate, coenzyme A, FAD and NAD⁺. From there the citric acid cycle produces NADH, FADH₂ and GTP per acetyl-CoA. Textbooks disagree about the final ATP tally per glucose once you add in the shuttle and the proton accounting, so learn the accounting itself rather than a single number.

| Glycogen storage disease | Missing enzyme | Hallmark |
|---|---|---|
| Type I, von Gierke | Glucose-6-phosphatase | Severe fasting hypoglycaemia, lactic acidosis |
| Type II, Pompe | Lysosomal α-glucosidase | Cardiomegaly, hypotonia |
| Type III, Cori | Glycogen debranching enzyme | Hepatomegaly, milder hypoglycaemia |
| Type V, McArdle | Muscle glycogen phosphorylase | Exercise cramps, myoglobinuria |

#### Lipid metabolism

Fatty acid breakdown happens in mitochondria as beta-oxidation, and each round of the cycle removes two carbons and yields one acetyl-CoA, one FADH₂ and one NADH. Long-chain fatty acids cannot cross the inner mitochondrial membrane unaided, so they are handed over by the carnitine shuttle, and the mitochondrial step of that shuttle is the control point. Malonyl-CoA, the committed precursor of fatty acid synthesis, inhibits it, which is the elegant way of preventing synthesis and oxidation from running at once.

Ketone bodies are acetoacetate, beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetone. The liver makes them from excess acetyl-CoA and extrahepatic tissues use them, but the liver cannot use them because it lacks the enzyme that transfers CoA from succinyl-CoA to acetoacetate. That asymmetry is why a diabetic patient in ketoacidosis has acidic blood.

Cholesterol is made in the liver from acetyl-CoA, with HMG-CoA reductase as the rate-limiting enzyme and the target of the statins. Lipoproteins carry it: chylomicrons deliver dietary fat from the intestine, VLDL carries liver-made triglyceride outward, LDL delivers cholesterol to tissues via receptor-mediated endocytosis, and HDL carries it back. Cholesterol also feeds steroid hormones, bile acids and, through 7-dehydrocholesterol in the skin, vitamin D₃.

#### Nucleic acids

A nucleotide is a base, a pentose and phosphate. DNA carries deoxyribose and uses thymine; RNA carries ribose and uses uracil. Adenine and guanine are purines, cytosine, thymine and uracil are pyrimidines. Chargaff's rules follow from base pairing: in double-stranded DNA, adenine equals thymine and guanine equals cytosine, so total purines equal total pyrimidines. Because G–C pairs form three hydrogen bonds against two for A–T, GC-rich DNA has a higher melting temperature and shows a larger hyperchromic shift on denaturation.

Strands are antiparallel, with a 5′ phosphate end and a 3′ hydroxyl end, joined by phosphodiester bonds. Replication is semiconservative: helicase opens the duplex, primase lays an RNA primer, DNA polymerase extends only from a 3′ end in the 5′ to 3′ direction, so the leading strand is continuous while the lagging strand is made as Okazaki fragments joined by ligase. Topoisomerase relieves the supercoiling ahead of the fork, and telomerase extends the ends of linear chromosomes.

Mutation classification is a favourite source of items. A transition swaps a purine for a purine or a pyrimidine for a pyrimidine; a transversion swaps a purine for a pyrimidine. A substitution may be silent, missense or nonsense, and an insertion or deletion that is not a multiple of three causes a frameshift. Sickle cell anaemia is the standard example: a single base change in the beta-globin gene turns a glutamate codon into a valine codon, giving one wrong amino acid in the beta chain.

#### Vitamins and cofactors

Vitamins are the dietary source of coenzymes. The water-soluble group is the B vitamins and vitamin C; the fat-soluble group is A, D, E and K. Learn the pairing in one direction only, from deficiency symptom to coenzyme, because the item usually gives the patient and asks for the vitamin.

- Thiamine (B1) gives thiamine pyrophosphate, needed by pyruvate dehydrogenase, alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and transketolase. Deficiency is beriberi and Wernicke-Korsakoff, with lactic acidosis because pyruvate cannot be converted to acetyl-CoA efficiently.
- Riboflavin (B2) gives FAD, needed by succinate dehydrogenase and glutathione reductase. Deficiency causes cheilosis, glossitis and corneal vascularisation.
- Niacin (B3) gives NAD⁺ and NADP⁺. Deficiency is pellagra: dermatitis, diarrhoea, dementia.
- Pantothenic acid (B5) gives coenzyme A, the carrier of the acyl group.
- Pyridoxine (B6) gives pyridoxal phosphate, the coenzyme for transamination, decarboxylation and the first step of heme synthesis. Deficiency causes peripheral neuropathy, cheilosis and sideroblastic anaemia.
- Biotin (B7) is the coenzyme for carboxylases, and raw egg white avidin binds it and can cause deficiency.
- Folate (B9) gives tetrahydrofolate, the one-carbon carrier for purine and thymidylate synthesis. Deficiency gives megaloblastic anaemia and neural tube defects.
- Cobalamin (B12) is used by methionine synthase and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase. Deficiency gives megaloblastic anaemia, neuropathy and raised methylmalonic acid, and it occurs in pernicious anaemia through loss of intrinsic factor.
- Vitamin C is the reducing cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylase in collagen. Deficiency is scurvy, with bleeding gums, corkscrew hairs and poor wound healing.
- Vitamin A maintains vision and epithelium; its deficiency brings night blindness.
- Vitamin D increases calcium absorption; its deficiency brings rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults.
- Vitamin E protects membranes from oxidation; its deficiency causes hemolytic anaemia.
- Vitamin K carboxylates the glutamate residues of clotting factors II, VII, IX and X, so its deficiency prolongs the prothrombin time.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Worked item 1 — reading a double-reciprocal plot

**Question.** A Lineweaver–Burk plot shows the uninhibited reaction and the inhibited reaction as two straight lines that cross on the y-axis. The inhibited line has a steeper slope and its x-intercept is closer to the origin.

**Step 1 — take the intercepts first.** The two lines share the same y-intercept, and the y-intercept equals 1/Vmax. Same y-intercept means Vmax is unchanged.

**Step 2 — take the x-intercept.** The x-intercept equals −1/Km. The inhibited line's x-intercept has moved toward zero, so −1/Km has become less negative, so Km has increased.

**Step 3 — name it.** Higher apparent Km with unchanged Vmax is competitive inhibition. The inhibitor competes with substrate for the same active site, so enough substrate can still saturate the enzyme; you just need more of it.

> ⚡ **Examiner Trap.** Reading the slope first and calling it competitive inhibition because the lines cross on the y-axis is only right if you also check the x-intercept, and the trap is that noncompetitive inhibition can look similar in a hand-drawn sketch. Noncompetitive inhibition lowers Vmax, so its y-intercept rises. If both intercepts move, you are looking at uncompetitive inhibition. The second trap is treating Km as an affinity constant: Km is the substrate concentration at half Vmax, and a larger Km means more substrate is needed to reach half-maximal rate, not that the enzyme binds more tightly.

| Inhibition type | Vmax | Apparent Km | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Competitive | Unchanged | Increased | Lines cross on the y-axis |
| Noncompetitive (pure) | Decreased | Unchanged | Lines cross on the x-axis |
| Uncompetitive | Decreased | Decreased | Parallel lines |
| Irreversible | Decreased | Not meaningful | Enzyme is inactivated, so the inhibitor dose sets the activity |

#### Worked item 2 — hyperammonaemia with orotic acid in the urine

**Question.** A neonate develops vomiting, lethargy and hyperammonaemia after being fed protein. Urinary orotic acid is raised. Which step of the urea cycle is blocked?

**Step 1 — where the ammonia comes from.** Two sources feed the cycle. Glutamate dehydrogenase releases free ammonia from glutamate, and glutamine synthetase traps it into glutamine. Both are mitochondrial, and the ammonia must be converted to carbamoyl phosphate by carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I, which is activated by N-acetylglutamate.

**Step 2 — locate the leak.** The two mitochondrial steps are carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I and ornithine transcarbamylase. Carbamoyl phosphate cannot cross the mitochondrial membrane, so if it accumulates because the ornithine transcarbamylase step is blocked, it spills into the cytosol, where it enters pyrimidine synthesis and produces orotic acid. The excess orotic acid is therefore a marker of the mitochondrial block.

**Step 3 — answer.** Ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency. The remaining three steps of the cycle, argininosuccinate synthetase, argininosuccinate lyase and arginase, all sit in the cytosol, so a block in any of them gives hyperammonaemia with a normal urine orotic acid.

> ⚡ **Examiner Trap.** Treating raised orotic acid as proof of a pyrimidine synthesis defect. Orotic acid is raised in OTC deficiency because carbamoyl phosphate is diverted into pyrimidine synthesis; the defect is in the urea cycle, not in pyrimidine synthesis. The second trap is assuming any hyperammonaemia is liver failure. Third trap: forgetting that carbamoyl phosphate synthetase II, the cytosolic enzyme for pyrimidine synthesis, is a different enzyme from synthetase I, and confusing the two is what makes this item look harder than it is.

#### Worked item 3 — a deficiency, a coenzyme and a symptom

**Question.** A patient has a macrocytic anaemia, numbness in the hands and feet, and a raised serum methylmalonic acid. Which vitamin is deficient, and which reaction is blocked?

**Step 1 — the discriminator.** Raised methylmalonic acid points at the methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA step, catalysed by methylmalonyl-CoA mutase. That enzyme needs adenosylcobalamin, the active form of vitamin B12.

**Step 2 — the chain.** Without B12, methylmalonyl-CoA accumulates and methylmalonic acid rises. The second B12-dependent reaction, methionine synthase, also stalls, so homocysteine rises and methionine falls.

**Step 3 — the cause of the deficiency.** Intrinsic factor is made by gastric parietal cells and is needed for B12 absorption in the terminal ileum. Loss of intrinsic factor gives pernicious anaemia, and ileal resection or a vegan diet gives other routes to the same block. Folate deficiency also causes megaloblastic anaemia but does not raise methylmalonic acid and does not cause the neuropathy, because the folate-dependent reactions are different.

> ⚡ **Examiner Trap.** Answering folate for a macrocytic anaemia because folate and B12 look interchangeable on a blood film. The separators are methylmalonic acid, which rises only in B12 deficiency, and the neurological signs, which do not occur in folate deficiency. The second trap is assuming a vegan diet is the only route; intrinsic factor loss is the classic exam cause.

Two further discriminations earn marks on their own. NADH is the electron donor to the electron transport chain and therefore the source of oxidative phosphorylation, while NADPH is spent on reductive biosynthesis and on regenerating reduced glutathione; an item that swaps them is testing exactly this. And a pyruvate that cannot enter the mitochondria because pyruvate dehydrogenase is blocked accumulates, is converted to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase, and consumes the bicarbonate that buffers blood, producing a lactic acidosis.

#### ⚡ Quick Exam Checklist

- Structure of the molecule identified before any enzyme is considered
- Cofactor named alongside the enzyme, not instead of it
- Km read as the substrate concentration at half Vmax, and the intercepts used to identify an inhibitor
- Competitive inhibition flagged by unchanged Vmax with raised apparent Km
- Committed step of glycolysis identified as PFK-1, and irreversible steps named as three
- Insulin against glucagon direction correct for every pathway asked about
- Muscle recognised as unable to release free glucose because it lacks glucose-6-phosphatase
- Two H bonds for A–T and three for G–C, with the GC effect on melting temperature stated correctly
- Transition distinguished from transversion, and missense, nonsense and frameshift kept apart
- Coenzyme matched to the deficiency symptom, especially B12 against folate
- Both unit systems kept comfortable, because the ADA states that both are used on the examination
