# Textual Conversations — HSC English Advanced (NESA 2024) Notes

Textual Conversations is the Year 12 Module A of the HSC English Advanced syllabus. It is examined in HSC Paper 2 Section I. Students explore how composers engage with other texts through imitation, appropriation, parody or subversion. The module requires sustained comparative composition across two prescribed texts.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Module focus.** The ways composers engage with other texts through imitation, appropriation, parody or subversion. Comparison is built into the assessment design — there is no single-text option.
- **Texts.** Schools select at least one pair of texts in conversation. The NESA-prescribed pairings are listed in the English Stage 6 Prescriptions document.
- **Outcomes.** Students analyse how composers draw on other texts to construct meaning; compose comparative responses integrating both texts.
- **Examination.** HSC Paper 2, Section I. Time and weighting per the annual Examination Specifications.

#### Examiner traps

- Treating the two texts separately — comparison requires integration across both texts in each paragraph.
- Failing to identify the type of engagement (imitation, appropriation, parody, subversion) — the type shapes the analysis.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Types of textual engagement

Imitation: the new text follows the conventions of the source text in form and content. Example: a poet imitating the sonnet form of Shakespeare.

Appropriation: the new text takes a story, character or idea from the source and reworks it for a new context. Example: a contemporary novelist rewriting Jane Eyre.

Parody: the new text exaggerates the conventions of the source for comic or critical effect. Example: a satirical film sending up the conventions of melodrama.

Subversion: the new text deliberately reverses or undermines the source's values. Example: Wide Sargasso Sea (Jean Rhys) rewriting Jane Eyre from Bertha's perspective.

#### Comparative framework

For each paragraph of a comparative response, structure as follows:

1. **Topic sentence.** A claim about the relationship between the two texts (e.g., "Both composers critique the patriarchal values of their contexts, but with different effects").
2. **Text 1 evidence.** Quote, image or passage from the first text.
3. **Text 2 evidence.** Quote, image or passage from the second text.
4. **Comparison.** An explanation of how the two texts engage with each other — similarity, difference, or both.
5. **Link back to thesis.**

#### Language features to know

Allusion, motif, echo, palimpsest, refraction, metatext, parody, satire, irony, intertextuality. Use these precisely — "allusion" is not interchangeable with "reference".

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Why Textual Conversations rewards deep reading

- **Comparative reading is the hardest skill.** Students must hold two texts in mind and integrate evidence across them in every paragraph. This is harder than single-text analysis.
- **The module rewards deep knowledge of both texts.** Skim-reading the second text on the day of the examination is fatal — students need quotes, language features and contextual knowledge ready.
- **The marking scheme scores integration.** A response that discusses Text 1 for three paragraphs and Text 2 for three paragraphs scores lower than a response that integrates every paragraph. Aim for integration in every body paragraph.

#### Year 12 planning pattern

- **Term 1.** Read both prescribed texts in full. Build a quote bank for each.
- **Term 2.** Identify the type of engagement (imitation, appropriation, parody, subversion) for each pairing. Practice timed comparative responses.
- **Term 3.** Full Paper 2 practice with both Section I (Textual Conversations) and Section II (Critical Study of Literature). Mark against the published HSC marking guidelines.
- **Term 4 (HSC).** Final revision of the quote bank and the engagement types.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Comparative means 'compare Text 1 then Text 2'." No — comparison requires integration across both texts in every body paragraph.
- "One text must be older than the other." The pairing can be two contemporary texts in dialogue with each other, or a contemporary text engaging with a classical one.
- "Personal response is wrong." Personal response is acceptable in a conclusion but the bulk of the response must be textual evidence.

#### Specification reference

This module is examined in HSC Paper 2 Section I. The prescribed text pairings are published annually by NESA. Confirm the live prescribed pairings and any NESA updates on curriculum.nsw.edu.au for the current specification.
