# Thermochemistry and Energetics

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Thermochemistry measures the **heat change** that accompanies chemical reactions and physical changes; energetics adds **work** to that picture using ΔU = q + w. A reaction is **exothermic** when ΔH is negative (heat released) and **endothermic** when ΔH is positive (heat absorbed). The single most-tested formula is **q = mcΔT**, where q is heat in joules, m is mass in kg, c is specific heat capacity in J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, and ΔT is temperature change in K. At constant pressure, the heat absorbed equals ΔH, so any q = mcΔT measurement is a direct route to a reaction's enthalpy change. Hess's law lets you sum intermediate ΔH values to reach a target reaction.

- **Energy conservation:** ΔU = q + w, sign by chemist convention (q into system is +, w on system is +).
- **Heat sign rule:** exothermic ΔH < 0, endothermic ΔH > 0.
- **Standard state:** 1 bar pressure, element in reference form set to ΔH_f° = 0.
- **Bond rule:** breaking a bond costs energy; forming a bond pays it back.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Energy, System, and the First Law
Energy is the capacity to do work or supply heat. A **system** is the matter under study; everything else is the **surroundings**; together they form the **universe**. **Open systems** exchange both matter and energy; **closed systems** exchange only energy; **isolated systems** exchange neither. The first law of thermodynamics states that the total energy of an isolated system is constant, written ΔU = q + w, where q is heat absorbed by the system and w is work done on the system.

#### Enthalpy and Reaction Sign Convention
**Enthalpy (H)** is the heat content at constant pressure. For any reaction, ΔH = H(products) − H(reactants). Because ΔH is measured at constant pressure, q_p = ΔH for processes that do no non-expansion work. NECO questions often show **energy-level diagrams** with reactants on one side and products on the other: a downward arrow to products indicates an exothermic reaction with negative ΔH, while an upward arrow indicates an endothermic reaction with positive ΔH.

#### Calorimetry and Heat Capacity
The heat transferred to a substance is calculated using **q = mcΔT**, where m is the mass in kilograms and c is the specific heat capacity (water = 4 200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹; aluminium ≈ 900 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹). Mixing grams with J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ is a frequent source of error, as is forgetting that ΔT in K and °C are numerically identical only when both are temperature differences.

#### Hess's Law and Bond Enthalpies
Hess's law: total enthalpy change is independent of the route, depending only on the initial and final states. This allows you to combine equations by reversing steps (and flipping the sign of ΔH) and by multiplying equations by a stoichiometric coefficient (and multiplying ΔH by the same factor).

| Concept | Key point |
| --- | --- |
| Exothermic reaction | Heat released, ΔH < 0, products lower on energy diagram |
| Endothermic reaction | Heat absorbed, ΔH > 0, products higher on energy diagram |
| Hess's law | ΔH_total = Σ ΔH(intermediate steps) along chosen path |
| Bond enthalpy estimate | ΔH_rxn ≈ Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed), energies in kJ mol⁻¹ |

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Bond Breaking versus Bond Formation
Reaction enthalpy has a clear molecular picture. **Bond breaking requires energy** and **bond formation releases energy**. Approximate a reaction's enthalpy from mean bond enthalpies using ΔH_rxn ≈ ΣD(bonds broken) − ΣD(bonds formed), where D is the mean bond dissociation energy in kJ mol⁻¹. A positive result flags an endothermic step; a negative result flags an exothermic step. Real values from calorimetry often differ slightly because mean bond enthalpies are averaged over many molecules.

#### Worked Example with Numbers
A 0.150 kg block of aluminium cools from 90 °C to 30 °C. Using c = 900 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹: q = 0.150 × 900 × (30 − 90) = 0.150 × 900 × (−60) = −8 100 J. The negative sign means the aluminium loses 8 100 J of heat to the surroundings. Convert the answer to kilojoules (−8.10 kJ) before writing it next to a molar quantity. Expressing enthalpy per mole requires an additional step of dividing by the number of moles of aluminium involved.

#### Relations to Adjacent Topics
Thermochemistry links directly to **chemical equilibrium** (Gibbs energy, ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, decides spontaneity), **electrochemistry** (electrode potentials translate to Gibbs energy via ΔG = −nFE), and **rates of reaction** (activation energy on a reaction profile). NECO candidates should remember that a negative ΔH does **not** automatically mean the reaction will occur at a useful rate or even proceed at all; spontaneity requires ΔG < 0, while ΔH alone only describes heat flow.

#### Common Exam Traps
- Mixing units so c is J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ while m stays in kilograms, inflating or shrinking q by 1 000.
- Forgetting to flip the sign of ΔH when a Hess's-law equation is reversed.
- Treating "elements in standard states" as having non-zero ΔH_f°; they are defined as 0.

1. List the steps to determine ΔH for a target reaction from formation enthalpies, including sign handling.
2. Explain why bond enthalpies give only an estimate of ΔH_rxn, citing at least one physical reason.
