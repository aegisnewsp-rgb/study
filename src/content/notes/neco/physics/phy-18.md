# Nuclear Physics: Radioactivity and Fission/Fusion

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Radioactivity** is the spontaneous emission of particles or radiation from unstable atomic nuclei. Three radiation types exist: **alpha (⁴He)** has highest ionization but lowest penetration (paper stops it); **beta (⁻¹e/⁺¹e)** penetrates farther (aluminum stops it); **gamma (γ)** penetrates most (lead stops it).

Key formulas to memorize: **N = N₀e^(−λt)**, **T½ = 0.693/λ**, **A = λN**. Alpha decay reduces nucleon number by 4 and atomic number by 2 (²³⁸U → ²³⁴Th + ⁴He). Beta decay keeps nucleon number constant while changing atomic number. **Fission** splits heavy nuclei (²³⁵U + n → fragments + 2–3n + ~200 MeV); **fusion** combines light nuclei (²H + ³H → ⁴He + n + 17.6 MeV). Chain reactions need critical mass with neutron moderators. Binding energy per nucleon peaks at iron-56.

NECO SSCE exam pointers: expect MCQs on half-life calculations, the decay law, and distinguishing alpha/beta/gamma properties. Numericals involving T½ = 0.693/λ appear frequently. Carbon dating questions sometimes ask for sample age.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### The Nature of Radioactive Decay

Radioactive decay is a first-order kinetic process governed by the equation **N = N₀e^(−λt)**, where N₀ is the initial number of undecayed nuclei, N is the number remaining after time t, and λ is the decay constant (unique to each isotope). The decay constant has units of s⁻¹ and cannot be altered by temperature, pressure, or chemical state.

**Half-life (T½)** is the time required for half the nuclei in a sample to decay. The relationship **T½ = ln2/λ = 0.693/λ** shows that half-life is independent of the initial sample size — a critical point for NECO questions. An isotope with λ = 0.0693 s⁻¹ has a half-life of exactly 10 seconds. You can also calculate remaining nuclei using **N = N₀(½)^(t/T½)**.

#### Types of Emissions

| Radiation | Composition | Penetration | Ionizing Power |
|-----------|------------|-------------|----------------|
| Alpha (α) | ⁴He nucleus (2p + 2n) | Lowest — stopped by paper | Highest |
| Beta (β) | Electron or positron | Moderate — stopped by aluminum | Moderate |
| Gamma (γ) | High-energy photon | Highest — stopped by lead (cm) | Lowest |

**Alpha decay** equation: ²³⁸U₉₂ → ²³⁴Th₉₀ + ⁴He₂. The parent loses 4 nucleons and 2 protons. **Beta-minus decay**: n → p + ⁻¹e (Z increases by 1). **Beta-plus decay**: p → n + ⁺¹e (Z decreases by 1). **Gamma decay** does not change Z or A — it simply releases excess nuclear energy.

#### Nuclear Fission

In fission, a heavy nucleus (typically ²³⁵U) absorbs a neutron, becomes unstable, and splits into two lighter nuclei while releasing 2–3 fast neutrons and approximately 200 MeV of energy. The energy comes from the mass defect — the sum of fission product masses is less than the original uranium nucleus mass. Using **E = mc²** and **Q-value = (mass_initial − mass_final) × 931.5 MeV** lets you calculate the energy released per fission event.

A sustained **chain reaction** requires the neutron multiplication factor to equal or exceed 1. Below critical mass, too many neutrons escape the sample. **Neutron moderators** (graphite, water, heavy water) slow fast neutrons to thermal neutrons, increasing the probability of ²³⁵U fission. Control rods (cadmium or boron) absorb excess neutrons to regulate the reaction.

#### Nuclear Fusion

Fusion combines light nuclei to form heavier nuclei with a net release of energy. The deuterium-tritium reaction: ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n + 17.6 MeV. Because both nuclei carry positive charge, they repel each other — overcoming the Coulomb barrier requires temperatures exceeding **10⁷ K**, achievable only in stars or thermonuclear weapons. The energy release per unit mass in fusion exceeds that of fission because the mass defect per nucleon is larger for light nuclei fusing toward iron-56.

#### NECO SSCE Pattern

Questions typically test: (1) calculating remaining nuclei or activity after n half-lives, (2) identifying radiation type from penetration properties, (3) writing balanced decay equations, and (4) explaining chain reaction requirements.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### The Statistical Nature of Decay

Individual radioactive nuclei decay randomly — you cannot predict which specific atom will decay next. However, the bulk behavior of a large population follows deterministic laws with remarkable precision. The decay constant λ represents the probability per unit time that any given nucleus will decay. For a sample of 10¹² atoms with λ = 0.001 s⁻¹, the expected activity is 10⁹ decays per second. Quantum mechanics governs each decay event; the exponential law emerges from millions of independent random processes.

#### Activity Units and Measurement

**Activity (A)** = λN, measured in becquerels (Bq = 1 decay/s). The older unit, **curie (Ci)**, equals 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq (approximately the activity of 1 gram of radium-226). For NECO calculations, convert between units carefully: 1 mCi = 3.7 × 10⁷ Bq.

#### Carbon-14 Dating

Living organisms maintain a constant ¹⁴C/¹²C ratio through metabolic exchange with the environment. Upon death, no new carbon enters, and the ¹⁴C fraction decreases as it decays (T½ = 5,730 years). The formula **t = T½ × log₂(N₀/N)** calculates the age, where N₀ is the expected initial activity and N is the measured activity. This technique reliably dates organic samples up to approximately 50,000 years; beyond about 10 half-lives, residual activity becomes indistinguishable from background radiation.

#### Binding Energy and Nuclear Stability

The **mass defect** is the difference between the sum of constituent nucleon masses and the actual nuclear mass. This defect, converted via **E = mc²**, represents the **binding energy** that holds the nucleus together. The binding energy per nucleon curve shows that iron-56 (Fe-56) has the maximum binding energy per nucleon (~8.8 MeV/nucleon). Nuclei heavier than iron (like uranium) release energy when split (fission) because their binding energy per nucleon is lower. Nuclei lighter than iron (like hydrogen) release energy when fused (fusion) because they move toward the peak at iron. This explains why both fission and fusion are exothermic processes.

#### Common Mistakes in NECO Questions

1. **Confusing ionization with penetration**: Alpha particles ionize most strongly because they are doubly charged and slow-moving, but they travel only a few centimeters in air and cannot penetrate paper. Students often mix this up.
2. **Forgetting atomic number changes**: Only gamma decay leaves Z and A unchanged. In alpha decay, Z decreases by 2; in beta decay, Z changes by ±1. Always verify your decay equation conserves nucleon number and charge.
3. **Neglecting the temperature requirement for fusion**: Fusion does not occur at room temperature regardless of pressure — Coulomb repulsion prevents nuclei from approaching close enough to fuse. Stars achieve this through gravitational compression raising core temperatures to 10⁷–10⁸ K.

#### Practice Prompts

1. A ²³⁸U sample has a half-life of 4.5 × 10⁹ years. Calculate the decay constant in s⁻¹. If the sample contains 6.02 × 10²³ atoms, what is its activity in curies?

2. In a fission reaction, ²³⁵U absorbs a neutron and splits into ¹⁴¹Ba and ⁹²Kr plus three neutrons. Write the balanced equation and calculate the energy released (given mass defect = 0.186 u; use 931.5 MeV per u).
