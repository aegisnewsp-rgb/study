# Percentages

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **percentage** expresses a number as a fraction of 100; the symbol **%** literally means *per hundred*. Every percentage problem reduces to three variables: the **rate** (%), the **base** (the whole you compare against), and the **value** (the part). The governing relationship is **Value = (Rate / 100) × Base**, which rearranges to Base = (Value / Rate) × 100 when you need to recover the original quantity. Two shortcuts save the most time on NAT-I: (1) **x% of y = y% of x**, which lets you pick whichever side is easier to compute, and (2) **successive percentage change** combined as *a + b + (ab/100)* for two back-to-back changes. Memorise these and the profit/loss base rule — profit % uses **cost price** as base, discount % uses **marked price** as base.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Conversion Triangle

Three forms describe the same number, and converting fluently between them is the first skill NAT-I tests:

| Form | Example | Relationship to 25% |
|------|---------|---------------------|
| Fraction | 1/4 | 25 / 100 |
| Decimal | 0.25 | 25 / 100 |
| Percentage | 25% | 25 / 100 |

To find a percentage of a quantity, convert the % to a decimal (divide by 100) and multiply. To express one quantity *A* as a percentage of another *B*, compute **(A / B) × 100** — here *B* is always the base.

#### Percentage Change

**Percentage change = ((New − Old) / Old) × 100.** The base is always the **original (old)** value, regardless of whether the change is an increase or decrease. A common trap reverses this for decreases; never let the new value become the base.

#### Successive Percentage Change

When a quantity first changes by *a%* and then by *b%*, the **net** change is:

> **Net % = a + b + (ab / 100)**

This is **not** simply *a + b*. A 10% rise followed by a 10% fall gives *10 − 10 + (10·−10)/100 = −1%*, not 0%. The (ab/100) term captures the compounding effect of applying the second percentage to an already-altered base.

#### Profit, Loss, and Discount Bases

- **Profit %** and **Loss %** are calculated on **cost price (CP)**.
- **Discount %** and **Markup %** are calculated on **marked price (MP)** or **list price**.
- **Selling Price (SP) = CP × (1 + profit%/100)** and **SP = MP × (1 − discount%/100)**.

Mixing these bases — e.g., computing discount on cost price — is the most frequently marked error in NAT-I word problems.

#### Worked Example

A shirt marked at Rs 2,500 is sold at a 20% discount, then a sales tax of 8% is added. Final price = 2500 × 0.80 × 1.08 = **Rs 2,160**. Notice how each percentage acts as a **multiplier**, not a number to be added or subtracted.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Mechanism

Percentage points vs percent change: if an interest rate moves from 4% to 6%, the **percentage point** change is +2, but the **percent change** is (2/4)×100 = 50%. NAT-I occasionally conflates these in word problems to trap careless readers.

Negative and fractional percentages: a −15% change means multiplying by 0.85. Successive formula still holds — plugging *a = 20* and *b = −10* yields *20 − 10 + (20·−10)/100 = 8%* net change.

Population and growth problems: a population growing at *r%* per year becomes *P × (1 + r/100)^n* after *n* years (compound). NAT-I sometimes disguises this as a "successive percentage" chain — recognising the pattern saves minutes.

#### Common Mistakes

- Using **new value as base** for percentage decrease (e.g., dividing drop by the lower figure inflates the %).
- Adding % values across years without compounding: 10% growth for 3 years is **not** 30% but *1.1³ − 1 = 33.1%*.
- Treating "%" as additive: increasing 200 by 10% gives 220, not 210.
- In ratio problems, failing to convert both quantities to a common base before comparing.

#### Connections

Percentages underpin **simple interest, compound interest, profit/loss, discount, ratios, data interpretation, and growth rates**. Mastering the multiplier view (×1.10, ×0.85, ×1.0725) cascades directly into compound interest and CI–SI difference problems.

#### Practice Prompts

1. A product's price rose 20% in January, fell 10% in March, then rose 25% in June. What is the net percentage change from the original price? *(Answer: +35%)*
2. After a 15% discount, an item is sold for Rs 1,700. What was the marked price, and what would the discount % be if the seller instead offered Rs 1,530? *(Answer: Rs 2,000; 23.5%)*
