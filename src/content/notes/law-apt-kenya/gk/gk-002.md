# Sources of Kenyan Law (2)

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> A four-line scannable summary for last-minute revision before the exam.

Kenyan law flows from eight ranked sources fixed by Article 2 of the Constitution of Kenya 2010, with the Constitution sitting at the apex. Below it stand treaties (once domesticated), Acts of Parliament, delegated legislation, common law, African customary law, doctrines of equity, and received English statutes still suitable to Kenya after 12 December 1963. Anything inconsistent with the Constitution is void.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** "**C-T-A-D-C-C-E-R**" — **C**onstitution, **T**reaties, **A**cts, **D**elegated legislation, **C**ommon law, **C**ustomary law, **E**quity, **R**eceived English statutes. Walk down the list in order and stop at whichever source still answers the question.

- **Supreme text:** Constitution of Kenya 2010, Article 2(1)–(4).
- **Treaties:** become binding after parliamentary enactment under Article 2(6).
- **Customary law:** valid only if not repugnant to justice, morality, or written law.
- **Received English law:** frozen at 12 December 1963 (Kenya's Independence Day).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### The Eight Sources in Order
Article 2 of the Constitution of Kenya 2010 ranks legal authority from highest to lowest, and any rule inconsistent with the Constitution has no force. Treaties ratified by Kenya form part of domestic law only after Parliament passes enabling legislation (Article 2(6)). Acts of Parliament come next, followed by delegated legislation made by bodies Parliament has authorised — county assemblies, ministers, the Chief Justice, and professional regulators.

The remaining four sit as residual sources: the common law, African customary law, the doctrines of equity, and the received English statutes that applied immediately before 12 December 1963. The Judicature Act (Cap 8) reinforces Article 2 by directing superior courts to apply these sources in the same sequence whenever a statute does not cover the dispute.

| Source | Legal basis | When it applies | Key condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Constitution | Article 2(1) | Always | Supreme; overrides any conflicting rule |
| Treaties | Article 2(6) | After domestication | Requires an Act of Parliament |
| Acts of Parliament | Article 2(3) | When enacted | Must pass constitutional muster |
| Delegated legislation | Article 2(3), parent statute | Within delegated scope | Ultra vires if it exceeds parent Act |
| Common law | Judicature Act, Cap 8 §3 | Gap-filler only | Must suit local conditions |
| Customary law | Cap 8 §3(2) | Personal-law matters | Not repugnant to justice/morality |
| Equity | Cap 8 §3 | Common law remedy inadequate | Maxim "equity follows the law" |
| Received English statutes | Kenya (Independence) Order in Council 1963 | Pre-1963 English law | Applicable on 12 December 1963 |

#### How Customary Law Is Tested
The repugnancy clause in Section 3(2) of the Judicature Act invalidates any customary rule that offends justice and morality or clashes with a written law. The same clause limits the customary law to communities to whom it relates — it does not apply uniformly to every Kenyan. Cases on intestate succession and bride price frequently hinge on this filter.

#### Judicial Precedent as Part of the Common Law
Stare decisis is the engine of judicial precedent: a binding ratio decidendi from a superior court must be followed by lower courts on the same point. Article 163(7) makes Supreme Court decisions binding on every other court in Kenya. Precedent is therefore not a separate ninth source; it is the mechanism by which common law grows.

> 💡 **High-Yield Revision Hook:** Treat stare decisis as a *gear* inside common law, not a separate source — examiners deduct marks for listing it alongside the eight.

#### Worked Problem
A candidate argues in a mock answer that the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights automatically binds Kenyan courts because Kenya ratified it in 1992. Apply Article 2(6) to test the claim.

Article 2(6) reads: "Any treaty or convention ratified by Kenya shall form part of the law of Kenya under this Constitution." Kenya operates a dualist system for treaty implementation. Therefore ratification alone does not make the Charter enforceable in Kenyan courts; Parliament must domesticate it through an Act. Until that happens, the treaty is binding at international level only and cannot, on its own, ground a claim before the High Court. The candidate's argument collapses at the domestication step.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students write "ratification = binding" and lose marks. Dualism demands a domesticating statute; cite Article 2(6) explicitly, not just the treaty name.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Where the Common Law Picks Up Its Anglo-Kenyan Accent
Common law in Kenya is not a copy of today's England. It is the body of English principles that were applicable in Kenya on 12 December 1963, as Schedule 2 to the Kenya (Independence) Order in Council 1963 confirms. Later English decisions are persuasive, not binding. Kenyan judges frequently depart from English precedent where local conditions, the 2010 Constitution, or African customary law point a different way — for instance in inheritance cases where a daughter was historically excluded under English primogeniture.

#### Equity's Two Operative Limits
Equity supplements the common law only when the common law remedy is inadequate; it never overrides an Act of Parliament. The maxim "equity follows the law" governs the relationship, and equitable relief is discretionary — granted on clean hands, good faith, and no undue delay. Courts will refuse equitable relief that contradicts a statutory scheme, even if doing so produces a hard outcome for the applicant.

#### Constitutional Supremacy and the Inconsistency Test
Article 2(4) declares any law, act, or omission inconsistent with the Constitution void to the extent of that inconsistency. Practically, this means a delegated instrument can be struck down for ultra vires, and a customary rule can be struck down for repugnancy or written-law conflict. The hierarchy in Article 2 is therefore not academic: it is the test examiners expect you to apply.

| Edge case | Mechanism | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Treaty ratified but not enacted | Dualism under Article 2(6) | International obligation only, no domestic force |
| Customary rule conflicting with a statute | Repugnancy + written-law test | Customary rule yields |
| Delegated legislation exceeding parent Act | Ultra vires | Provision struck down |
| Pre-1963 English statute now unsuitable | Local-conditions filter | Disapplied by courts |
| Supreme Court ruling on a constitutional point | Article 163(7) | Binding on all other courts |

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

2. A county assembly passes a by-law on solid-waste management that conflicts with a national regulation on the same subject. Walk through the hierarchy in Article 2 and the parent-Act test to decide which rule prevails, and state which body would hear the dispute.
