# Photosynthesis and Respiration — Leaving Certificate (Established) Biology Notes

Unit 2.2 is the largest single question bank in Strand 2. It asks you to do four things: outline photosynthesis, aerobic respiration and anaerobic respiration; **model the two-stage processes with reference to transfer molecules**; investigate factors affecting the rate of photosynthesis; and recognise how the internal structures of mitochondria and chloroplasts make these processes possible. The word *model* is deliberate — the paper wants diagrams and equations, not paragraphs. Practise drawing the four locations (light-dependent in the thylakoid, light-independent in the stroma, glycolysis in the cytoplasm, the Krebs cycle and ETC in the mitochondrial matrix and inner membrane) until you can do it without hesitating.

The single idea that unifies the unit is **energy transfer**. Light energy becomes chemical energy in ATP and NADPH; that energy is spent fixing carbon; the sugar is then broken down to release that energy as ATP. ATP, NAD⁺ and NADP⁺ are the currency, and the specification names all three explicitly.

---

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Photosynthesis is anabolic** (building larger molecules from smaller ones) and acts as a **carbon sink**, removing CO₂ from the atmosphere. **Respiration is catabolic** (breaking down molecules to release energy) and returns CO₂ to the atmosphere. The two are close to reverse equations, but they are not simply each other reversed — they happen in different organelles, at different times, with different mechanisms.
- **Balanced equation, photosynthesis:** 6CO₂ + 6H₂O →(chlorophyll, light) C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂.
- **Balanced equation, aerobic respiration:** C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy (ATP).
- **Anaerobic respiration in yeast (alcoholic fermentation):** C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ + energy. **In muscle (lactic acid fermentation):** C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2CH₃CHOHCOOH + energy.
- **Conditions necessary for fermentation**, named by the specification: **presence of sugars and microorganisms (yeast or bacteria), an anaerobic environment, and a suitable temperature**. All three are testable and all three appear in mark schemes.
- **First stage (glycolysis).** In the **cytoplasm**, one six-carbon carbohydrate is split into two molecules of **pyruvate**, generating ATP and NADH. This stage does not require oxygen.
- **Second stage (aerobic only).** Pyruvate is broken down to **acetyl-CoA** plus one CO₂. Acetyl-CoA enters the **citric acid (Krebs) cycle** in the mitochondrial matrix, producing CO₂ and transferring electrons to NAD⁺ and FAD. Those electrons pass to an **electron transport chain** in the inner membrane; the energy released drives **ATP synthase**, which makes ATP.
- **Chemiosmosis.** Transfer of electrons along the chain causes a charge difference that attracts protons; protons accumulate outside the inner mitochondrial membrane and then flow back down their gradient through ATP synthase, which harnesses that flow to make ATP. Exactly the same mechanism runs in the thylakoid membrane, in the opposite direction of proton accumulation.
- **Light-dependent reactions (non-cyclic), in the thylakoid membranes.** A photon is absorbed by a pigment and passed between pigment molecules until it reaches a reaction-centre chlorophyll. Because chlorophyll becomes positively charged, it attracts electrons from the splitting of water (**photolysis**), releasing oxygen. Energy from the electron transfer produces ATP and **NADPH** from NADP⁺.
- **Cyclic pathway.** Some electrons return to chlorophyll and transfer surplus energy to ATP formation through an alternative route. ATP is made; NADPH and oxygen are not.
- **Light-independent reactions, in the stroma.** Protons and electrons are transferred from NADPH to CO₂, and ATP supplies the energy, producing glucose. ADP and NADP⁺ return to the light-dependent reactions to be recharged.
- **Limiting factors for photosynthesis** (specification): **temperature, light intensity and CO₂ concentration**. Rate rises with each until another factor becomes limiting, then plateaus.
- **Structure-function:** chloroplasts have an outer and inner membrane, a stroma, and stacks of thylakoids called grana; mitochondria have an outer membrane, a matrix, and an inner membrane folded into **cristae**. Both inner membranes carry the electron transport chains and ATP synthase.

#### Examiner traps

- **Putting glycolysis in the mitochondria.** It happens in the cytoplasm. This error costs the location mark every time.
- **Saying the light-independent reactions need light directly.** They do not — they need ATP and NADPH, which is why they are called independent: they do not use light *directly*.
- **Writing O₂ as a product of the light-independent reactions.** Oxygen comes from splitting water in the light-dependent stage.
- **Reversing photosynthesis and respiration as if they were mirror images.** They are complementary, not inverse processes.
- **Confusing NAD⁺ and NADP⁺.** NADP⁺ is reduced to NADPH in photosynthesis; NAD⁺ is reduced to NADH in respiration.
- **Omitting ATP yield when asked for it.** Glycolysis yields a small amount of ATP directly; the citric acid cycle yields very little itself — almost all the aerobic ATP comes from the electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation.

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### The two-stage model, stage by stage

| Stage | Where | What happens | Transfer molecules |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Photosynthesis: light-dependent (non-cyclic) | Thylakoid membrane | Photon absorbed, water split (photolysis), O₂ released, ATP and NADPH made | NADP⁺ → NADPH; ADP → ATP |
| Photosynthesis: light-independent | Stroma | CO₂ fixed using ATP and NADPH to build carbohydrate | NADPH → NADP⁺; ATP → ADP |
| Respiration: glycolysis | Cytoplasm | Six-carbon carbohydrate split into 2 pyruvate | NAD⁺ → NADH; ADP → ATP |
| Respiration: link reaction | Mitochondrial matrix | Pyruvate → acetyl-CoA + CO₂ | NAD⁺ → NADH |
| Respiration: citric acid cycle | Mitochondrial matrix | Acetyl-CoA oxidised, CO₂ released, electrons removed | NAD⁺ → NADH; FAD → FADH₂ |
| Respiration: electron transport chain | Inner mitochondrial membrane | Electrons pass down the chain, pumping protons; protons flow back through ATP synthase | NADH/FADH₂ oxidised; ADP + Pi → ATP |

Reading that table left to right is enough to answer most of the modelling questions. Note that the *only* stage in respiration that produces oxygen is none of them — oxygen is consumed in the chain — and the *only* stage in photosynthesis that produces oxygen is the light-dependent one.

#### Investigating the rate of photosynthesis

The specification asks you to investigate factors affecting the rate, using primary and secondary data. The standard practical uses pondweed (*Elodea*) and measures **the volume of oxygen bubbles per minute** in a beaker of water.

**Method.** Place a known mass of pondweed in the beaker with a known volume of sodium hydrogencarbonate solution (which supplies CO₂), position a capillary tube over the cut stem, and place the whole setup in a water bath at a controlled temperature. Count bubbles or measure the gas volume displaced per minute.

**Variables.** Independent: temperature, light intensity (distance from the lamp), or CO₂ concentration. Dependent: volume of gas produced per unit time. Controlled: length of the stem cut, volume of solution, starting temperature, duration, and the lamp's wattage.

**Improvements that earn the evaluation mark.** Use a lamp at a fixed distance rather than changing bulb power, since heat radiation confounds light intensity with temperature. Use a water bath so temperature is uniform and does not vary with the lamp. Cut the stem at an angle under water to prevent air entering the cut and blocking the gas path. Count bubbles for a fixed time rather than timing an arbitrary number of bubbles. Repeat and take the mean. Use a control of dead pondweed to show the recorded gas is from photosynthesis and not from thermal expansion.

**The limitation to state.** Counting bubbles measures oxygen production by volume but does not give a mass of O₂, and the volume changes with temperature, so the water bath is not optional. The setup also measures gross photosynthesis only; respiration continues in the leaf, so a more accurate rate would be net photosynthetic rate.

#### Limiting factors

*Worked question.* Pondweed in a beaker produces oxygen at 2 cm³ per minute. The lamp is moved closer and the rate rises to 9 cm³ per minute. Moving it closer again changes nothing.
*A.* Between the two positions light intensity was limiting, so increasing it increased the rate of the light-dependent reactions. At the third position it is no longer limiting — CO₂ concentration or temperature is now the constraint, so raising light further has no effect. To identify which, change CO₂ concentration while holding light constant: if the rate rises, CO₂ was limiting; if it does not, temperature is limiting. This is the correct experimental design and stating it is worth more than any single graph.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Worked Higher-level questions with answers

**Q1.** Explain why the rate of photosynthesis falls when the temperature is raised above about 40 °C.
*A.* Two effects compete. Raising temperature increases molecular kinetic energy, which would increase the rate of the light-independent reactions and the rate of enzyme-catalysed steps. Above about 40 °C the enzymes catalysing the light-independent reactions begin to denature: their tertiary structure changes, so the active sites no longer complement their substrates, in particular the large enzyme RuBisCO that fixes CO₂. Active enzyme concentration falls faster than the temperature benefit rises, so the rate drops. The drop is steep rather than gradual, and is not reversible on cooling — both of which are diagnostic of denaturation rather than of a reversible equilibrium effect.

**Q2.** Compare the electron transport chain in a mitochondrion with one in a chloroplast.
*A.* Both are chains of protein complexes and mobile electron carriers embedded in a membrane, and in both the transfer of electrons down the chain causes a charge difference that attracts protons and pumps them to one side, creating a gradient. In the mitochondrion the chain is in the inner mitochondrial membrane and protons accumulate in the intermembrane space; in the chloroplast the chain is in the thylakoid membrane and protons accumulate inside the thylakoid lumen. In both, protons return down the gradient through ATP synthase, which harnesses the flow to make ATP. The differences are in what supplies the electrons and what accepts them: in respiration the electrons come from NADH and FADH₂ and end up on oxygen, giving water; in photosynthesis they come from water split by photolysis and end up on NADP⁺, giving NADPH, with oxygen released as a by-product. The ATP made in respiration is used for cellular work; the ATP made in photosynthesis is used to fix CO₂.

**Q3.** Explain why oxygen is released in photosynthesis but not in respiration.
*A.* In photosynthesis, water is split in the light-dependent reactions — photolysis — to replace the electrons that leave chlorophyll. The released hydrogen ions and electrons combine to give molecular oxygen as a by-product. Respiration does not split water at any stage. Oxygen is instead the final electron acceptor in the mitochondrial electron transport chain, where it combines with electrons and protons to form water. So the two gases appear at opposite ends of the two processes: O₂ is produced where electrons are taken *from* water, and consumed where electrons are given *to* it.

**Q4.** A student adds yeast and glucose to warm water and seals the container with a rubber tube. Explain what happens, and how it demonstrates that fermentation is anaerobic.
*A.* The yeast respire anaerobically because the sealed container excludes atmospheric oxygen. With glucose present and the temperature in the enzyme's usable range, fermentation proceeds: glucose is converted to ethanol and carbon dioxide, with a small amount of ATP. Carbon dioxide collects and is driven out through the tube, which is the observable evidence that the pathway is running. Had oxygen been available, the yeast would instead carry out aerobic respiration, which yields far more ATP per glucose and produces no ethanol or CO₂. This is the practical basis of bread making (CO₂ raises the dough) and of brewing.

#### How this unit pays elsewhere

- **Unit 2.3** reuses the citric acid cycle's products as the starting point for protein synthesis — amino acids are made from intermediates of the cycle.
- **Unit 2.6** covers how the products of photosynthesis (glucose, then sucrose) are transported around the plant in the phloem, and how O₂ and CO₂ diffuse across the stomata.
- **Unit 3.1** treats photosynthesis as the entry point for energy flow in a food web, and as a carbon sink.
- **Unit 3.2** returns to fermentation as an industrial application.
- **Biology in Practice Investigation** (40% of the grade) — the pondweed investigation is a ready-made project that produces primary data, a hypothesis and a justified conclusion.

#### Mistakes students actually make

1. **Location errors.** Glycolysis is cytoplasmic; the Krebs cycle is in the matrix; the chain is in the inner membrane; the light-dependent reactions are in the thylakoid membranes; light-independent are in the stroma. Mark these on a diagram before you memorise them.
2. **Treating the two processes as exact mirrors.** They share an overall equation shape but not a mechanism.
3. **Missing the transfer molecules.** Every stage question should name NAD⁺/NADH, NADP⁺/NADPH, ADP/ATP and where they change.
4. **Confusing ATP production location.** In aerobic respiration, the citric acid cycle itself makes very little ATP; nearly all of it comes from oxidative phosphorylation at ATP synthase.
5. **Losing the control or repeats in an investigation plan**, which is where most of the marks in a practical question actually sit.
6. **Writing "the rate increases with temperature" with no upper bound.** The curve has an optimum and then denaturation takes over.
7. **Ignoring respiration in a photosynthesis investigation.** A leaf respires continuously, so the measured rate is net photosynthesis; say so and it demonstrates real understanding.
