# Consumer Behaviour

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Consumer behaviour studies **how individuals select, buy, use, and dispose** of goods, services, and ideas to satisfy needs and wants. It rests on the distinction between **need** (biological requirement), **want** (culturally shaped desire), and **demand** (want backed by purchasing power).

- **Total Utility (TU)** is the sum of all marginal utilities: TU = ΣMU.
- **Marginal Utility (MU)** is the addition to TU from one more unit consumed: MUₙ = TUₙ − TUₙ₋₁.
- **Consumer equilibrium** (one good case) requires MUₓ/Pₓ = MUᵧ/Pᵧ = MUₘ (marginal utility of money).
- **Price Effect = Substitution Effect + Income Effect** (Hicks–Slutsky decomposition).
- **Engel's Law**: as income rises, the *proportion* spent on food falls.

**High-yield exam pointers:** (1) Indifference curves are convex to the origin and never intersect. (2) Consumer surplus = willingness to pay minus price actually paid. (3) Giffen goods are a rare subset of inferior goods where demand rises with price.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Definitions and the Cardinal Approach
Consumer behaviour analyses the **decision process** of buyers — need recognition, information search, evaluation of alternatives, purchase, and post-purchase feedback (Kotler's five-stage model). The **economic view** assumes a rational consumer maximising utility subject to a budget constraint.

In the **cardinal utility** framework, satisfaction is measurable in *utils*. Total Utility rises with consumption, but at a decreasing rate due to the **Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility**. Beyond a saturation point, MU turns negative and TU begins to fall — a frequently tested trap in RAS short notes.

#### Ordinal Approach and Indifference Curves
The **ordinal approach** ranks preferences without measuring them. An **indifference curve** joins all bundles yielding equal satisfaction. Its key properties are:

| Property | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Downward sloping | More of X requires less of Y to keep utility constant |
| Convex to origin | Diminishing Marginal Rate of Substitution (MRSₓᵧ) |
| Non-intersecting | Intersection violates transitivity of preferences |
| Higher curve = higher utility | Ordinal ranking, not cardinal measurement |

The **budget line** Pₓ·x + Pᵧ·y = M has slope −Pₓ/Pᵧ. Equilibrium occurs where the highest attainable indifference curve is **tangent** to the budget line, so MRSₓᵧ = Pₓ/Pᵧ.

#### Key Decompositions and Goods
A price change produces a **price effect**, split via Hicks or Slutsky into a **substitution effect** (relative price change, real income constant) and an **income effect** (real income change). Goods are classified as normal (positive income effect), inferior (negative income effect), Giffen (negatively sloped demand despite being inferior), and Veblen (demand rises with price because of status signalling).

**Exam pattern in RAS:** Questions typically arrive as 10–15 mark short notes asking for utility maximisation conditions, indifference curve diagrams, or the difference between cardinal and ordinal approaches.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Consumer Surplus and Engel's Law
**Consumer surplus**, introduced by Jules Dupuit and popularised by Alfred Marshall, equals **willingness to pay minus actual price paid** (₹). Graphically it is the triangular area between the demand curve and the price line. Surplus shrinks when prices rise or when substitutes proliferate.

The **Engel curve** plots quantity demanded of a good against income M: x = f(M). For normal goods it slopes upward; for inferior goods it eventually slopes **backward**. Engel's Law — that the *food share* of expenditure falls as income rises — is empirically verified across Indian NSS consumer expenditure rounds and is a recurring RAS application point for Indian rural-urban consumption patterns.

#### Edge Cases and Common Mistakes
- **Giffen paradox:** requires the negative income effect to *dominate* the negative substitution effect — true for very inferior staple goods like coarse cereals in poor households.
- **Veblen goods** break the law of demand: conspicuous consumption raises demand when price rises.
- **Consumer sovereignty** assumes the buyer is king, but advertising-induced preference shifts, asymmetric information, and externalities (pollution, addiction) limit this doctrine — a frequent General Studies Economics question.
- Confusing MU = TU (incorrect) with MU = ΔTU/Δx (correct) is a standard marker of weak answers.

#### Connections and Practice Prompts
This topic links directly to **Theory of Demand**, **Elasticity of Demand**, **Market Structures** (consumer choice under oligopoly), and **Welfare Economics** (consumer surplus as a measure of welfare gain).

#### Practice prompts:
1. Draw an indifference map and budget line, and derive the consumer equilibrium condition. Show how equilibrium shifts when price of X falls, decomposing into substitution and income effects.
2. "Engel's Law is a special case of consumer behaviour theory." Discuss using Indian NSS data on food expenditure share.
