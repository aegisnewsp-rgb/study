# Ranking and Ordering

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Ranking and Ordering** questions in NAT-I (NTS) Analytical Reasoning present a set of persons, objects, or events whose relative standing must be deduced from comparative clues. The core operation is **transitive inference**: if A > B and B > C, then A > C, and the chain A > B > C is fully determined. Position is counted either from the top (Rank 1 = topmost) or the bottom, so a single rank statement can be inverted: if 5 people are ahead of X in a row of 9, X occupies position 6 from the top and position 4 from the bottom. Three high-yield pointers: (1) always identify the **extreme positions** first, because "tallest", "shortest", "first", or "last" clues fix one end of the chain; (2) treat "between" conditions as yielding **two mirror possibilities** (A–B–C or C–B–A) unless directional words like "to the left of" lock the order; (3) eliminate options using **negative information** ("X is not adjacent to Y") before committing to a placement.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Construction Rules

Every Ranking and Ordering set in NAT-I follows a deterministic construction. The student receives comparative statements of three forms: direct inequalities (A > B), betweenness (B is between A and C), and positional anchors (D is at one extreme). These statements are converted into a **directed chain**, and the chain is then placed against the total count to label each slot.

**Transitive inference** is the workhorse. A statement pair like "A is older than B" and "B is older than C" produces the inequality A > B > C, which immediately fixes A's relative standing against C even though no direct comparison was given. NAT-I questions frequently test this exact step by asking for a comparison that appears nowhere in the clues but is derivable from them.

#### The Rank-to-Position Formula

When a question specifies how many persons are ahead of or behind a target, the **rank formula** applies:

$$\text{Total} = (\text{ahead of X}) + 1 + (\text{behind X})$$

If 7 students are ahead of Sara in a class of 25, Sara's rank from the top is 8, and her rank from the bottom is 25 − 7 = 18. Both numbers must be carried forward because subsequent clues often pivot on the *other* direction.

#### Worked Mini-Example

Clues: (i) P > Q, (ii) R > P, (iii) S is between R and Q, (iv) T is last. From (i) and (ii), R > P > Q. Inserting S between R and Q gives R > S > P > Q. T is last, so T < Q, giving the full chain R > S > P > Q > T. The position of S is now fixed as second from the top, and any option contradicting this is eliminated.

#### Common NAT-I Traps

- Treating "B is between A and C" as a single order rather than two mirror orders.
- Forgetting that **rank from bottom = (total + 1) − rank from top**, a substitution that flips many wrong answers to correct ones.
- Counting ordered pairs (A,B) and (B,A) as distinct when the question asks for unordered combinations.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Branch Resolution

A single NAT-I set can carry **multiple valid configurations** when the clues do not uniquely anchor every variable. In such cases, every branch must be exhausted before selecting the answer, because NAT-I options often list positions that are true in *one* branch but not the other. A reliable method is to build a **branch table**: list all constraint-consistent orders, then test each option against every branch.

#### Adjacency and Extreme-Position Logic

Adjacency clues such as "A sits next to B" produce **two micro-arrangements** (A-B or B-A) unless an additional clue locks the direction. Extreme-position clues (tallest, shortest, oldest, first, last) collapse the branch count dramatically — always extract them first because they reduce the search space from n! to (n−1)!.

#### Connection to Floor and Row Arrangements

NAT-I occasionally fuses vertical rank (floor number in a building) with horizontal position (left-to-right seat). Treat floor as the primary ordering axis and seat as the secondary one, applying the same transitive rules independently on each axis before intersecting the two orders.

#### Common Mistakes in Extended Sets

- **Rank vs. position confusion**: Rank 1 from the top equals position n from the bottom, not position 1.
- **Inconsistent-branch acceptance**: choosing an answer that fits only one branch without verifying the others.
- **Transitive gap**: writing A > B from the clues but failing to extend it to C through the intermediate B > C relation.
- **Tie assumptions**: assuming equal standing when the clues only fail to specify an order — treat ties as a separate branch, not as a default.

#### Practice Prompts

1. In a race of 8 runners, M finishes 3 places ahead of N, and N finishes 5 places ahead of O. If P is last, what is P's position from the top?
2. Among five friends A, B, C, D, E arranged by height, B is taller than D but shorter than A, C is not the shortest, and E is taller than A. Rank them from tallest to shortest and identify every valid configuration.
