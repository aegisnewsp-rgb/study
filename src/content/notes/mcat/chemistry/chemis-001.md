# Atomic Structure — MCAT Pakistan

Atomic Structure is the foundation of every other Chemistry unit: orbitals, quantum numbers, electron configuration, and periodic trends. Under the PMDC 2026 Chemistry allotment of 45 MCQs (≈25 % of the 180-MCQ MDCAT paper, PMDC Public Notice, 2026), this unit contributes roughly 5–7 MCQs per paper — every question rests on it, even when the stem is about bonding, kinetics, or the periodic table. PMDC confirms the 2026 syllabus is identical to the 2025 Uniform Curriculum (PMDC MDCAT Curriculum 2025), so the student learning outcomes below are unchanged for this cycle.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

**What to lock in within the first hour.**
- **Atom basics.** Protons (charge +1, mass ≈ 1 u) and neutrons (charge 0, mass ≈ 1 u) sit in a tiny nucleus; electrons (charge −1, mass ≈ 1/1836 u) occupy the surrounding space. Atomic number Z = proton count; mass number A = protons + neutrons; isotopes share Z but differ in A.
- **Quantum numbers.** Four numbers define every electron: **n** (principal, 1, 2, 3, …; size and energy), **l** (azimuthal, 0 → n−1; orbital shape), **mₗ** (magnetic, −l to +l; orientation), **mₛ** (spin, +½ or −½). No two electrons in the same atom share all four (Pauli exclusion).
- **Orbitals and shapes.** s = spherical (l = 0), p = dumbbell (l = 1, three orientations pₓ, p_y, p_z), d = cloverleaf (l = 2, five orientations), f = complex (l = 3, seven orientations). Each orbital holds 2 electrons.
- **Electron configuration rules.** Aufbau (fill lowest energy first), Pauli (one pair max per orbital, opposite spins), Hund (fill degenerate orbitals singly before pairing). Order of filling: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p.
- **Periodic trends (across a period, left → right).** Atomic radius decreases, ionisation energy increases, electron affinity becomes more negative, electronegativity increases. Down a group, atomic radius increases and ionisation energy decreases.

**One-line recap before walking in.** Electrons live in orbitals defined by four quantum numbers; their arrangement drives size, ionisation energy, electronegativity, and the chemical behaviour of every element.

### 🟡 Standard — Exam Prep (3d-3w)

#### Quantum numbers and orbitals
The principal quantum number **n** sets the shell and the energy scale: n = 1 is the ground state, n = 2 is the first excited shell, and so on. Each shell has n sub-shells; each sub-shell has 2l+1 orbitals; each orbital holds 2 electrons. The total electron capacity of a shell is 2n².

The azimuthal quantum number **l** runs from 0 to n−1 and determines orbital shape. The magnetic quantum number **mₗ** runs from −l to +l, naming individual orbitals (e.g., l = 1 → mₗ = −1, 0, +1 → p₋₁, p₀, p₊₁, which chemists relabel pₓ, p_y, p_z). The spin quantum number **mₛ** = ±½ distinguishes the two electrons in a single orbital; opposite spins are required (Pauli).

**Worked example.** List the four quantum numbers for the last electron of sodium (Z = 11): electron configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹. The 3s electron has n = 3, l = 0, mₗ = 0, mₛ = +½ (or −½, by convention). The 2p sub-shell fills as 2p₊₁, 2p₀, 2p₋₁ singly first, then pairs; Hund's rule is the trap.

#### Electron configuration, ions, and exceptions
Write the configuration of an atom by filling orbitals in Aufbau order. For an **anion**, add electrons to the next available orbital (Cl⁻ is [Ne] 3s² 3p⁶). For a **cation**, remove from the outermost shell first, then from the highest-n orbital (Fe is [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶; Fe³⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁵, not [Ar] 4s² 3d³).

Exceptions to the strict Aufbau order: **chromium** (Cr, Z = 24) is [Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵ and **copper** (Cu, Z = 29) is [Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰. The half-filled and fully-filled d sub-shells have extra exchange stabilisation. Mo, Ag, and Au show similar behaviour; know Cr and Cu by name.

#### Periodic trends explained
- **Atomic radius.** Across a period, the increasing nuclear charge pulls the same shell closer, so radius decreases. Down a group, each new shell sits outside the previous, so radius increases. A cation is smaller than its parent atom (lost shell or reduced shielding); an anion is larger (added electron, more repulsion).
- **Ionisation energy (IE).** The energy to remove an electron. IE₁ < IE₂ < IE₃ … because removing an electron from a positive ion takes more energy. A large jump between successive IEs signals a new shell (e.g., Na IE₁ ≈ 496 kJ/mol vs IE₂ ≈ 4560). Group 2 has higher IE than Group 1; N has higher IE than O (the half-filled 2p³ stability resists removal).
- **Electron affinity (EA).** Energy released when an electron is added. More negative EA = greater attraction for the electron. Cl has the most negative EA of the halogens; noble gases have positive (unfavourable) EA.
- **Electronegativity (χ, Pauling scale).** F is the most electronegative (χ ≈ 4.0), Cs the least (χ ≈ 0.7). χ governs bond polarity: Δχ > 1.7 is ionic, 0.4–1.7 is polar covalent, < 0.4 is essentially nonpolar covalent.

**Worked example.** Rank Na, Mg, Al, Si in order of first ionisation energy. — **Na < Al < Mg < Si**. Na is the lowest (Group 1, one outer electron). Mg is higher than Al because removing an electron from 3s² (Mg) is harder than removing from 3s² 3p¹ (Al). Si is highest because nuclear charge keeps increasing across the period.

#### Atomic models and spectral evidence
The **Bohr model** (1913) places electrons in fixed circular orbits around the nucleus, with quantised angular momentum; it explains the line spectrum of atomic hydrogen (Lyman, Balmer, Paschen series) but fails for multi-electron atoms. The **quantum mechanical (wave) model** treats electrons as standing-wave orbitals; Schrödinger solved for hydrogen, getting discrete energy levels and orbital shapes. The Heisenberg uncertainty principle (Δx·Δp ≥ ℏ/2) tells us we cannot know both an electron's position and momentum precisely — a key reason orbitals are *probability maps*, not orbits.

**Worked example.** Calculate the energy of a photon emitted when an electron in a hydrogen atom drops from n = 3 to n = 2 (Balmer series, Hα line). ΔE = −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J × (1/n_f² − 1/n_i²) = −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ × (1/4 − 1/9) ≈ −3.03 × 10⁻¹⁹ J; wavelength λ = hc/E ≈ **656 nm** (red, the Hα line).

#### Isotopes and atomic mass
**Isotopes** share Z but differ in neutron count: ¹H, ²H (deuterium), ³H (tritium); ¹²C, ¹³C, ¹⁴C. The periodic-table atomic mass is a weighted average of isotopic masses: Aᵣ(X) = Σ (fractional abundance × isotopic mass). ¹²C defines the atomic mass unit (1 u = 1/12 the mass of ¹²C ≈ 1.6605 × 10⁻²⁷ kg).

**Worked example.** Chlorine is 75.77 % ³⁵Cl and 24.23 % ³⁷Cl. Aᵣ(Cl) = 0.7577 × 35 + 0.2423 × 37 ≈ **35.45**. A question asking for the atomic mass of an element with two isotopes always wants a weighted mean, not the mass of the major isotope.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (1mo+)

#### Wave–particle duality and de Broglie
Light and matter both show wave and particle behaviour. The **de Broglie wavelength** is λ = h/mv; an electron accelerated through 100 V has λ ≈ 0.123 nm, on the order of atomic spacings — which is why electron diffraction probes crystal structure. The Davisson–Germer experiment (1927) confirmed electron diffraction on a nickel crystal.

#### Schrödinger equation in plain language
The time-independent Schrödinger equation $\hat{H}\psi = E\psi$ has solutions $\psi_{n,l,m_l}$ — the atomic orbitals. The square of the wave function, |ψ|², is the probability density; the radial distribution function (4πr²|R|²) gives the most probable radius. Nodes: radial nodes = n − l − 1; angular nodes = l. A 3p orbital has one radial and one angular node; 4d has two radial and two angular.

#### Spin–orbit coupling and the Zeeman effect
In multi-electron atoms, the electron's spin interacts with its orbital angular momentum (spin–orbit coupling). In an external magnetic field, energy levels split (Zeeman effect), producing fine structure in spectra. For MCAT, the takeaway is that **magnetic quantum number mₗ** distinguishes orbitals that are degenerate in the absence of a field but become distinct in a field — the basis of NMR spectroscopy.

#### Modern periodic table nuances
- **Relativistic effects** for heavy elements (Au, Pb) contract s orbitals and expand d and f orbitals, explaining why Au is yellow (relativistic shift of absorption edge) and Hg is liquid at room temperature.
- **Superheavy elements** (Z > 103) are placed in their predicted groups but their chemistry is uncertain because relativistic effects dominate and few atoms exist at a time.
- **Isoelectronic series** (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺ all [Ne]) share configuration; ionic radius decreases with increasing nuclear charge.

#### Connections to other units
Atomic Structure underpins **Chemical Bonding** (configurations decide whether atoms gain, lose, or share electrons), **Periodic Table** (trends derive from nuclear charge and shielding), **Acids and Bases** (high χ = strong oxyacid; HF is weak because F⁻ is small and tightly held), **Electrochemistry** (ionisation energies relate to standard reduction potentials for metals), and **Modern Physics** (the Bohr model and atomic spectra are the bridge).

## Common traps

1. **Calling the d sub-shell "five orbitals of two electrons each = 10" without naming the rule.** d orbitals hold 10 electrons; s holds 2; p holds 6; f holds 14. A question asking "how many electrons can the n = 3 shell hold?" wants 2n² = **18**, not 8.
2. **Writing [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶ for Fe³⁺.** Fe³⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁵. Electrons leave from 4s *first* (the highest-n orbital), then from 3d. Many students write 3d⁴ or 3d⁶ for Fe³⁺; the correct answer is 3d⁵ because the half-filled d sub-shell is extra-stable.
3. **Saying N has lower ionisation energy than O.** It does not. N (2p³) is half-filled and stable; O (2p⁴) has paired electrons in one p-orbital that repel each other, making the first electron easier to remove. The order across period 2: Li < B < Be < C < O < N < F < Ne (with Be > B and N > O as the two kinks).
4. **Confusing electronegativity with electron affinity.** χ is a relative *bond*-pulling scale (Pauling); EA is the energy *released* when a free atom gains an electron. They trend together but are not the same quantity. A question asking "which has the most negative electron affinity?" (Cl) and "which is the most electronegative?" (F) expect different answers.
5. **Treating the Bohr model as the final word.** Bohr explains the *hydrogen* line spectrum but cannot handle multi-electron atoms, the Zeeman effect, or the fine structure. The quantum mechanical model — orbitals, not orbits — is the working model. Any modern PMDC question that asks about orbital *shape* (spherical, dumbbell) expects the wave model, not Bohr's circular orbits.
