# Verbal Ability

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your CAT slot.

VARC mixes Reading Comprehension with Verbal Ability items in one 40-minute slot. **24 questions**, no sectional cutoff, but a high overall cutoff at IIM A/B/C/L means every paragraph-level decision matters.

| Component | Question count (typical) | Time per item |
|---|---|---|
| Reading Comprehension (3–4 passages) | 16–18 | 1.5–2 min each |
| Para Jumbles | 3 | 1.5 min |
| Para Summary | 3–4 | 1.5 min |
| Odd One Out | 2–3 | 1 min |
| Grammar / Verbal Logic | 0–2 | 1 min |

- **RC** tests main idea, tone, inference, purpose, and vocabulary-in-context.
- **VA** tests logical flow: jumbles (TQN — Theme, Quote, Number), summary (central theme vs detail), OOO (function divergence), grammar (SV agreement, modifiers, parallelism).
- Master **elimination, not selection**: four options, only one survives.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** "SIFT-E" — **S**can for opener, **I**dentify mandatory pairs, **F**ilter by tone match, **T**heme first in summary, **E**liminate grammatically. Run this five-step ladder on every VA item before you commit.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

> Working understanding for a student 2 days to 2 months out from CAT.

#### RC: The Mark Mine

CAT RC passages come in four shapes: factual, opinion-based, literary, and abstract. Question stems cluster into five families — main idea, tone/attitude, inference, purpose, and vocabulary-in-context. Inference items are the highest-trap zone: a 100% supported conclusion, not a plausible leap. Purpose items ask *why the author included X*; the answer is always a function (example, contrast, concession), never a restatement.

Read the first and last paragraphs before the middle ones. Most main ideas live there, and you will answer 4–6 passage questions without re-reading.

#### Para Jumbles: Opening Heuristics + Mandatory Pairs

Two engines drive jumbles. **Opening heuristics** flag sentence 1: a definition, a proper noun introduction, or a pronoun whose antecedent has not yet appeared. **Mandatory pairs** lock two sentences together — pronoun-antecedent, cause-effect, contrast ("however/yet/but"), or a quoted term that must be defined before it is used.

Apply **TQN flow**: Theme first (general statement), then Quote or specific data, then Number or final example. Four permutations survive the filter; pick the one whose sentence 1 matches your opener heuristic.

#### Para Summary vs Odd One Out

Summary asks for the option that **captures the central theme** of all four sentences. Detail-heavy options — names, dates, percentages — are usually wrong; they describe *what* the passage says rather than *what it is about*. OOO asks the opposite: the sentence whose **function or theme diverges** from the other three, even if its vocabulary looks similar.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Feature | Para Summary | Odd One Out |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Pick the option that fits all four sentences | Pick the sentence that breaks the pattern |
| Right answer is… | A generalisation of the passage | A function or theme outlier |
| Wrong-answer trap | Contains keywords from the passage | Shares keywords but a different role |
| Time budget | 1.5 min | 1 min |

#### Grammar: The Five Killer Rules

CAT grammar items are short (one sentence, four replacements). Five errors account for ~90% of options:

1. Subject-verb agreement across intervening phrases
2. Dangling or misplaced modifiers
3. Parallelism (A is to B as C is to D)
4. Tense consistency across clauses
5. Pronoun case and clear antecedent

#### Worked VA Item

**Passage (truncated):** Four sentences about urban rewilding projects.

A. Cities from Singapore to Stockholm are reintroducing native species into disused lots.
B. Supporters argue this softens the hard edge of concrete habitats.
C. Critics counter that novelty outweighs ecological function.
D. Singapore's punggol park now hosts hornbills breeding on rooftops.

**Q.** Which sentence is the **Odd One Out**?

**Resolution.** A, B, C share a general argumentative frame: claim, support, opposition, all about the *idea* of urban rewilding. D pivots to a single concrete example — a specific city, a specific species, a specific behaviour. Function diverges. Eliminate A (general claim), B (pro-argument), C (counter-argument). D is the odd sentence.

#### Options ranked:
- D ✓ (function outlier — example, not argument)
- A, B, C (rhetorical chain — claim → support → counter)

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students choose A because "cities, species, lots" sounds technical and D sounds conversational. Trap. Tone is not the criterion here; **logical function** is. Match on role, not on register.

> 📌 **Formula Check:** OOO rule of thumb — *if three sentences do the same job and one does something else, the one that does something else is the answer.* Apply this even when the odd sentence *feels* similar in vocabulary.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

> For students 3 months out, aiming at 99+ percentile in VARC.

#### Time-Allocation Geometry

VARC is a **throughput** section, not an accuracy section. The 40-minute slot, 24 questions, no sectional cutoff — three geometric facts dictate strategy. First, RC dominates mark share (~70%) and time share (~70%). Second, the gap between a 95th and 99th percentile scorer is rarely grammar; it is RC inference plus a clean Para Summary sweep. Third, leaving questions unattempted is **cheaper** than guessing wrong, because there is no sectional cutoff to meet — only overall percentile.

| Slot minute | Block | Target accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| 0–25 | First 2 RC passages | 90%+ |
| 25–40 | Last RC + all VA | 80%+ |
| Reserve | Last 2 min for unattempted | — |

#### Edge Cases and Boundary Conditions

- **Inference vs Assumption.** Inference must be *logically compelled* by the passage; assumption is unstated but required for the argument to stand. CAT occasionally tests the difference by offering a plausible assumption as a tempting inference.
- **Para Jumbles with no clean opener.** When all four sentences look equally "general", apply TQN in reverse: find the **closing** sentence (often contains a number, a future projection, or a final consequence) and work backward.
- **Summary where two options feel right.** Pick the one with the **broader scope** and the **less specific vocabulary**. CAT rewards abstraction in summary items.
- **Grammar where no option seems wrong.** Re-read the original stem for the rule it violates; the error is usually a modifier or an SV-agreement across a prepositional phrase. If still blank, leave it — grammar items yield the lowest ROI per minute.

#### Advanced Traps and Exceptions

1. **Tone drift.** A passage may shift tone mid-way (e.g., from descriptive to argumentative). Question stems about tone will refer to a *specific paragraph*, not the whole passage.
2. **"All of the following EXCEPT" stems.** These invert the work — you must verify three options true and one false; budget 30 extra seconds.
3. **Vocabulary-in-context.** The word's dictionary meaning is irrelevant; only the passage-level sense matters. Substitute your own word and test fit.
4. **Para Jumbles with 5 sentences.** The opening-heuristic shortcut weakens; lean harder on mandatory pairs and pronoun chains.
5. **RC passages on abstract topics (philosophy, art theory).** Main idea lives in the *logical structure* (X argues Y because Z), not in topic sentences alone.

#### Two Practice Prompts

1. Take a 4-sentence paragraph from an editorial. Write a one-line **central theme** statement, then list three **detail statements** that would be wrong summary options. Check: does your theme survive if you swap a synonym into each detail?
2. Pull an RC passage you have already solved. Re-answer the **inference** items only, then mark each as *compelled*, *plausible*, or *leap*. CAT-correct inferences are always *compelled*.
