# Pharmacokinetics Describes What the Body Does to the

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**General Pharmacology** underpins every clinical drug question in INI CET. It binds the time course of a drug (**ADME**: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion) to its action at the receptor (**pharmacodynamics**).

- **Volume of distribution (Vd)** = Dose ÷ Plasma concentration — high Vd means extensive tissue binding.
- **Clearance (CL)** = Rate of elimination ÷ Plasma concentration — hepatic + renal contribution.
- **Half-life (t½)** = 0.693 × Vd / CL — **steady state** is reached after **4–5 half-lives**.
- **Loading dose** depends on Vd; **maintenance dose** depends on CL — students frequently invert these.
- **Therapeutic Index (TI)** = TD50 / ED50 — low-TI drugs (digoxin, lithium, warfarin) need **therapeutic drug monitoring**.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Pharmacokinetics — ADME and core equations

Pharmacokinetics describes what the body does to the drug. Bioavailability (F) for oral dosing follows **F = Fa × Fg × Fh**, where Fa is the fraction absorbed, Fg is the fraction escaping gut-wall metabolism, and Fh is the fraction escaping hepatic first-pass metabolism. IV administration gives F = 1.

| Parameter | Formula | Clinical meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Volume of distribution | Vd = Dose / Cp | Apparent volume the drug distributes into |
| Clearance | CL = Rate of elimination / Cp | Plasma volume cleared per unit time |
| Half-life | t½ = 0.693 × Vd / CL | Time for Cp to fall by 50% |
| Loading dose | LD = Target Cp × Vd | Rapid attainment of therapeutic level |
| Maintenance dose | MD = Target Cp × CL × τ | Sustains steady-state concentration |

#### Pharmacodynamics — receptors, agonists, dose–response

Pharmacodynamics describes what the drug does to the body. A **full agonist** has intrinsic activity = 1 and produces Emax; a **partial agonist** elicits a submaximal response even at full receptor occupancy and can antagonise a full agonist in its presence. **Competitive antagonists** shift the EC50 rightward without altering Emax, whereas **non-competitive antagonists** reduce Emax.

#### Kinetics and metabolism

Most drugs follow **first-order kinetics** (constant fraction eliminated per unit time). **Zero-order kinetics** applies when elimination pathways are saturated — examples are ethanol, phenytoin (at toxic doses), high-dose theophylline, and aspirin overdose. **Phase I** metabolism (CYP450-mediated oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis) usually inactivates the drug; **Phase II** reactions (glucuronidation, sulfation, acetylation) increase water solubility for excretion.

#### Common traps in INI CET MCQs

- Confusing loading-dose and maintenance-dose formulae.
- Assuming all drugs obey first-order kinetics.
- Marking grapefruit juice as a CYP3A4 **inducer** — it is an **inhibitor**.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Enzyme induction vs inhibition — a clinical pearl

Enzyme **inducers** (phenytoin, rifampicin, carbamazepine, phenobarbitone, St John's wort) up-regulate CYP450 transcription, producing new enzyme protein over **1–2 weeks**. The clinical consequence is reduced plasma levels of victim drugs — a classic INI CET scenario asks why warfarin INR falls after starting rifampicin. In contrast, enzyme **inhibitors** (ketoconazole, erythromycin, clarithromycin, cimetidine, grapefruit juice, fluconazole) act within hours by blocking the active site, raising victim-drug levels and risking toxicity.

#### Pharmacogenomics and adverse drug reactions

| Genetic trait | Drugs affected | Clinical consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slow acetylator | Isoniazid, hydralazine, procainamide | Peripheral neuropathy, drug-induced lupus |
| G6PD deficiency | Primaquine, sulfonamides, dapsone, nitrofurantoin | Haemolytic anaemia |
| Pseudocholinesterase deficiency | Succinylcholine | Prolonged apnoea |

#### Low therapeutic index — why monitoring matters

A low-TI drug such as **digoxin** (TI ≈ 2–3), **lithium**, or **warfarin** has a narrow window between therapeutic and toxic concentrations. The question bank tests two ideas: (1) why these agents are monitored using serum levels, and (2) how dosing interval changes alter time-above-MEC without changing steady-state Cp (which depends only on dose-per-unit-time and CL).

#### Practice prompts

1. A patient on a theophylline infusion has Cp = 20 mg/L at a rate of 40 mg/h. If the infusion is stopped, what is the predicted Cp after one half-life, and how many hours until steady state had been reached?
2. Warfarin is started concurrently with rifampicin. Predict the INR trend over three weeks and identify which drug's plasma level is primarily altered.
