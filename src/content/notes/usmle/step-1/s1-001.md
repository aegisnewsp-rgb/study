# Physiology of Core Systems

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

Step 1 "assesses your understanding of basic sciences crucial for practicing medicine" — that is the wording NBME and the FSMB use, and the four systems on this page are where most of the first-year medical curriculum lives. The topic split below is StudyRoadmap's own study organisation, not an NBME blueprint.

Each of these systems runs on one organising idea. Learn the idea and most of the facts reorganise themselves around it.

| System | The idea that organises it | The equation that does most of the work |
|---|---|---|
| Cardiovascular | Flow follows a pressure gradient | CO = HR × SV; MAP ≈ DBP + ⅓(SBP − DBP) |
| Respiratory | Gas moves down a partial-pressure gradient | Fick's law: rate ∝ surface area × ΔP ÷ √molecular weight |
| Renal | Filtration is a balance of four pressures | GFR = Kf × [(P<sub>GC</sub> − P<sub>BS</sub>) − (π<sub>GC</sub> − π<sub>BS</sub>)] |
| Gastrointestinal | Solutes need a gradient and a carrier | Na⁺-coupled secondary active transport |

**Cardiovascular, the four lines that matter.** Cardiac output is heart rate multiplied by stroke volume, and stroke volume is end-diastolic volume minus end-systolic volume. Preload raises stroke volume through the Frank-Starling mechanism: more filling stretches the fibre, the fibre contracts harder. Afterload opposes it, so a rise in arterial resistance drops stroke volume even when filling is unchanged. Mean arterial pressure is diastolic pressure plus about one third of the pulse pressure at a heart rate near 60; as the heart speeds up, the share shifts toward one half.

**Respiratory, the four lines that matter.** Boyle's law (P₁V₁ = P₂V₂) explains how ventilation changes alveolar pressure, and Fick's law explains how gas crosses the alveolar-capillary membrane. Carbon dioxide diffuses far more readily than oxygen because it is much more soluble in plasma, so CO₂ leaves the alveolus at partial-pressure gradients where oxygen could not. Oxygenation therefore depends on matching ventilation to perfusion, whereas CO₂ elimination depends mostly on alveolar ventilation.

**Renal, the four lines that matter.** Glomerular filtration depends on filtration coefficient Kf and on net filtration pressure: glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid out, plasma oncotic pressure and capsular hydrostatic pressure push it back in. About one fifth of renal plasma flow becomes filtrate, and the tubule then reclaims nearly all the filtered sodium, water and glucose. Filtration is not the same as reabsorption — the tubule has transport maximums, so a substance can be filtered faster than it can be reabsorbed and appear in urine despite a normal plasma level.

**Gastrointestinal, the four lines that matter.** The gut moves chyme by peristalsis, which is controlled by the enteric nervous system rather than by the brain stem. Absorption is done by specific carriers: sodium and glucose move together through SGLT1 in the duodenum, fats are packaged into micelles and taken up as chylomicrons into lymph, vitamin B12 needs intrinsic factor and the terminal ileum, and iron is absorbed mainly as Fe²⁺ in the duodenum.

**Memorise for the Lite tier:**
- CO = HR × SV, and MAP ≈ DBP + ⅓ pulse pressure at a normal heart rate.
- Frank-Starling relates preload to stroke volume; afterload and contractility set the other limits.
- Fick's law for gas exchange, Boyle's law for ventilation.
- Dead space is ventilated but unperfused; shunt is perfused but unventilated.
- GFR depends on Kf and net filtration pressure, not on tubular reabsorption.
- Creatinine clearance estimates GFR; inulin is the reference standard.
- Sodium-coupled glucose absorption is secondary active transport; fat enters lymph as chylomicrons.
- B12 absorption requires intrinsic factor and the terminal ileum.

### 🟡 Standard — Full Coverage (1–2d)

#### Cardiovascular: which variable moved

Clinical physiology questions rarely ask "what is the cardiac output". They hand you a change and ask you which link of the chain moved. Work the chain in order: heart rate → stroke volume → arterial pressure → tissue perfusion.

A patient with a blood pressure of 85/50 mmHg, a heart rate of 128/min and warm extremities has lost volume. Heart rate has risen to compensate, stroke volume has fallen because preload is down, and the pulse pressure is narrow because diastolic pressure tracks the arterial volume. Now change one fact: if the extremities are cold and the jugular veins are distended, the same numbers mean something completely different — a failing heart that cannot pump, so fluid backs up while the body thinks it needs more volume.

Three variables set stroke volume, and they are worth separating because exam stems mix them freely. Preload is the end-diastolic volume, set by venous return and by the heart's filling compliance. Contractility is the intrinsic contractile strength at a fixed preload, controlled by sympathetic stimulation, circulating catecholamines and calcium handling. Afterload is the resistance the ventricle must eject against. The Frank-Starling mechanism operates on preload and contractility together: a bigger EDV means more stretched sarcomeres and more force, up to a point.

For pressure work, systemic vascular resistance is the resistance in the arterial circuit and the determinant of MAP for a given cardiac output: MAP ≈ CO × SVR. If a drug drops SVR but the heart cannot increase CO, the blood pressure falls. If a patient has a high blood pressure with a low heart rate and a wide pulse pressure, the arterioles are stiff and SVR is high.

Autoregulation protects flow in the brain, heart and kidney across a range of arterial pressure by local arteriolar smooth muscle, and it is why a stenotic kidney keeps its filtration until the stenosis becomes severe.

#### Respiratory: reading the numbers

The alveolar gas equation is the one calculation worth owning. At sea level the inspired oxygen partial pressure is about 760 mmHg; humidified air reaching the alveolus is about 613 mmHg once water vapour is subtracted. Subtract the alveolar carbon dioxide divided by the respiratory quotient:

P<sub>A</sub>O₂ = P<sub>I</sub>O₂ − (P<sub>A</sub>CO₂ ÷ R)

With a P<sub>A</sub>CO₂ of 40 mmHg and R of 0.8, the alveolar oxygen works out near 100 mmHg, and arterial oxygen sits a little lower at about 95 mmHg because of the physiological shunt through the bronchial circulation and the veins of the lungs. When you see an arterial oxygen tension well below what the equation predicts, that difference is the shunt.

Dead space and shunt are opposites. Dead space is ventilated alveoli with no blood flow, which wastes ventilation and raises PaCO₂; it is increased by pulmonary embolism and by mechanical ventilation with too few breaths for the perfused lung. Shunt is perfused alveoli with no ventilation, which shunts blood past the exchange surface and lowers PaO₂; it is increased by atelectasis and by pneumonia. Both raise the alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient, and only shunt lowers PaO₂ that oxygen therapy cannot fully correct.

Two shifters of the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve show up repeatedly. The Bohr effect is the rightward shift produced by falling pH and rising CO₂ — exactly the conditions of exercising muscle — and it increases oxygen unloading. The Haldane effect is the reverse: oxygenated blood carries less CO₂, so oxygen loading in the lung promotes CO₂ unloading there.

#### Renal: filtration, reabsorption and what the urine tells you

The three-nephron model divides the kidney into functional units: the proximal tubule reabsorbs roughly two thirds of filtered sodium and water and almost all glucose, the loop of Henle creates the medullary osmotic gradient that concentrates urine, and the distal tubule and collecting duct fine-tune volume and potassium under hormonal control.

Glucose is the example of a transport maximum. The SGLT transporters saturate, so once filtered glucose exceeds the transport maximum, glucose appears in the urine even though plasma glucose may still be normal. The same saturable behaviour applies to phosphate and to some amino acids.

For clinical reasoning, fractional excretion of sodium separates pre-renal from intrinsic renal causes of kidney injury:

FENa = (U<sub>Na</sub> × P<sub>Cr</sub>) ÷ (P<sub>Na</sub> × U<sub>Cr</sub>) × 100

A FENa below 1% means the kidney is holding on to sodium because perfusion or volume is short; a FENa above 2% means tubular injury has broken the ability to reabsorb sodium. A urine sediment showing muddy brown granular casts points the same way as the high FENa.

#### Gastrointestinal: carriers, enzymes and the lymph route

Fat is the outlier in the absorptive story. Bile salts emulsify large fat droplets and form micelles, which carry fatty acids and monoglycerides to the enterocyte brush border. Inside the cell they are re-esterified into triglycerides, packaged with apolipoproteins into chylomicrons, and dumped into lacteals, so absorbed fat enters the lymphatic system and then the systemic circulation, bypassing the portal vein first.

Sugars and amino acids behave differently. Glucose and galactose use SGLT1, which couples sodium entry to glucose entry against the glucose gradient — secondary active transport, because the sodium gradient itself was built by the basolateral Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. Fructose uses GLUT5 and is not sodium-coupled, which is why it behaves differently in the small bowel.

Protein digestion is staged: pepsin works in the stomach at low pH, then trypsin and chymotrypsin from the pancreas take over in the duodenum, and brush-border peptidases finish the job. Lactase and sucrase-isomaltase sit on the enterocyte membrane, so lactase deficiency produces symptoms at the point of contact rather than in the pancreatic juice.

#### Worked example: the breathless climber

A climber at altitude has a headache, is confused, and has a respiratory rate of 28. Arterial blood gas: pH 7.46, PaCO₂ 28 mmHg, PaO₂ 48 mmHg. Work it in three steps. First the pH is alkalotic. Second, the PaCO₂ is low, so the alkalosis is respiratory in origin — hyperventilation, which is what the body does at altitude. Third, the PaO₂ of 48 mmHg against an alveolar oxygen of roughly (613 − 28/0.8) ≈ 578 mmHg leaves an enormous alveolar-arterial gradient, which is far too wide for altitude alone and points to a shunt, here from high-altitude pulmonary oedema flooding alveoli with fluid. The confusion is then partly the alkalosis and partly the hypoxia.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (3–5d)

#### How each system fails when stressed

Cardiovascular stress is a demand problem: when output cannot rise to match demand, the body recruits sympathetic tone and redistributes blood to the heart and brain, sacrificing renal and gut flow. The first measurable sign is often reduced urine output, which is why an oliguric patient needs a fluid challenge rather than a diuretic.

Respiratory stress is a matching problem. The lung can be stiff (low compliance, work of breathing up, gas exchange preserved until late) or floppy (high compliance but alveolar collapse, shunt up and hypoxaemia early). Distinguishing these two is a common exam fork: emphysema is high compliance with a destroyed surface area, while fibrosis is low compliance with a preserved surface area.

Renal stress is a perfusion problem with two escape routes. The kidney can autoregulate by dilating the afferent arteriole, and it can protect glomerular filtration by constricting the efferent arteriole under angiotensin II — which raises filtration pressure but also raises capillary oncotic pressure, and if it goes too far causes acute tubular injury. Over-aggressive efferent constriction is a recognised cause of AKI in heart failure.

Gastrointestinal stress is an ischaemia problem. The splanchnic circulation is one of the first to be sacrificed in shock, and mucosal ischaemia causes erosions and stress ulcers, which is why acid-suppressing medication appears in the management of critically ill patients.

#### Common mistakes

Writing MAP as the average of systolic and diastolic pressure. The average of systolic and diastolic is the mean arterial pressure only when systolic is twice diastolic; the working formula is DBP + ⅓ of the pulse pressure.

Calling the pulmonary artery an artery in the wrong sense. It carries deoxygenated blood; naming follows the direction of flow away from the heart, not oxygen content.

Assuming a low GFR means the tubule is damaged. Filtration and reabsorption are separate processes, and a low GFR is most often a perfusion problem with an intact tubule.

Confusing clearance with excretion. Clearance is the volume of plasma from which a substance is removed per unit time; for a substance freely filtered and neither reabsorbed nor secreted, such as inulin, clearance equals GFR. Creatinine is filtered and slightly secreted, so its clearance slightly overestimates true GFR.

Reading alveolar ventilation as equal to minute ventilation. Minute ventilation is tidal volume times respiratory rate, and it includes dead space. Alveolar ventilation is (tidal volume − dead space) × rate.

#### Exam traps

The pressure in the pulmonary artery is generated by the right ventricle, so a right ventricular infarct drops pulmonary artery pressure and raises central venous pressure at the same time. Both rise together in pulmonary hypertension or right heart failure.

A patient with a low PaO₂ and a normal PaCO₂ is most likely shunting, not hypoventilating, because hypoventilation raises CO₂.

Cushing reflex: a raised intracranial pressure causes bradycardia and hypertension, whereas a haemorrhage causes tachycardia and hypotension. Both are "high pressure, low output" versus "low pressure, high output" responses to the same threat.

In the proximal tubule, glucose reabsorption is linked to sodium reabsorption through SGLT1, so a drug that blocks the sodium gradient reduces glucose reabsorption as well — which is how SGLT2 inhibitors lower blood glucose and how they can cause genital mycotic infection by increasing urinary glucose.
