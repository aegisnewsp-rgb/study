# IV. Acids and Bases — HKDSE Chemistry Notes

This topic covers Topic IV of the Compulsory Part of HKDSE Chemistry. It addresses common acids and alkalis; indicators and the pH scale; the behaviour of acids and bases; acid-base reactions (neutralisation, salt preparation); concentration of solutions (mol/dm³, molarity); strong and weak acids and bases; and salt hydrolysis.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Common acids**: hydrochloric acid (HCl), sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), nitric acid (HNO₃), ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH), citric acid (C₆H₈O₇). Strong acids fully ionise in water; weak acids partially ionise.
- **Common alkalis**: sodium hydroxide (NaOH), potassium hydroxide (KOH), ammonia (NH₃ in water), calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂). Strong bases fully dissociate; weak bases partially.
- **pH scale**: 0 (strongly acidic) to 14 (strongly alkaline); 7 is neutral. Each unit change represents a 10× change in [H⁺].
- **Indicators**: litmus (red in acid, blue in alkali); methyl orange (red in acid, yellow in alkali); phenolphthalein (colourless in acid, pink in alkali); universal indicator (full pH range).
- **Neutralisation**: acid + base → salt + water. Example: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O.
- **Salt preparation**: acid + metal, acid + base, acid + carbonate, acid + oxide, or direct combination. Choose the method based on the salt's solubility and the available starting materials.
- **Concentration**: c = n/V (mol/dm³), where n is moles and V is volume in dm³.
- **Strong vs weak**: HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄ are strong acids (fully ionised); CH₃COOH is a weak acid (partially ionised).
- **Salt hydrolysis**: the reaction of a salt with water to produce an acidic or alkaline solution. Salts of strong acids and weak bases (e.g. NH₄Cl) are acidic; salts of weak acids and strong bases (e.g. CH₃COONa) are alkaline; salts of strong acids and strong bases (e.g. NaCl) are neutral.

#### Examiner traps

- Treating "concentrated" and "strong" as synonymous — concentrated refers to the amount of solute per volume; strong refers to the degree of ionisation.
- Confusing acid-base neutralisation with acid-metal reactions (the latter produces hydrogen gas).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Acids and bases in water

**Arrhenius definition**: an acid produces H⁺ in water; a base produces OH⁻ in water.

**Brønsted-Lowry definition**: an acid is a proton (H⁺) donor; a base is a proton acceptor. This extends to non-aqueous systems.

**Lewis definition**: an acid is an electron pair acceptor; a base is an electron pair donor.

#### Strong vs weak acids and bases

**Strong acids**: HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄ (first proton only). Fully ionised in water. ~1 mol/dm³ HCl has [H⁺] ≈ 1 mol/dm³.

**Weak acids**: CH₃COOH, H₂CO₃, citric acid. Partially ionised. The acid dissociation constant Kₐ measures the strength: Kₐ = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA]. Smaller Kₐ = weaker acid.

**Strong bases**: NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂. Fully dissociated.

**Weak bases**: NH₃ (in water), amines. Partially protonated.

#### pH calculations

For a strong acid (fully ionised), [H⁺] = initial concentration of acid.

For a strong monobasic acid of concentration c mol/dm³, pH = -log(c).

For a strong dibasic acid (e.g. H₂SO₄, fully ionised to 2H⁺ + SO₄²⁻), [H⁺] = 2c and pH = -log(2c).

For a strong monoacidic base of concentration c mol/dm³, pOH = -log(c) and pH = 14 - pOH.

For a weak acid, pH = -log(√(Kₐ·c)) (approximate when dissociation is small).

#### Neutralisation and salt preparation

**Acid-base neutralisation**: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O. Acid + alkali → salt + water.

**Salt preparation methods**:
- Acid + metal (for moderately reactive metals): Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂.
- Acid + carbonate: CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂.
- Acid + oxide: CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O.
- Acid + hydroxide: Mg(OH)₂ + 2HNO₃ → Mg(NO₃)₂ + 2H₂O.
- Direct combination: NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl.

**Titration**: a precise method for determining the concentration of an acid or base using a standard solution. Use an indicator (or a pH meter) to detect the endpoint.

#### Salt hydrolysis

Salts of strong acids and weak bases (e.g. NH₄Cl, NH₄NO₃): cation hydrolyses to produce H⁺, solution is acidic.

Salts of weak acids and strong bases (e.g. CH₃COONa, Na₂CO₃): anion hydrolyses to produce OH⁻, solution is alkaline.

Salts of strong acids and strong bases (e.g. NaCl, KNO₃): neither ion hydrolyses, solution is neutral.

#### Volumetric analysis

A titration uses a standard solution (known concentration) and a measured volume of the unknown solution. At the equivalence point, moles of acid = moles of base × stoichiometric coefficient.

For a strong acid-strong base titration: c_aV_a = c_bV_b (if both monoprotic).

For polyprotic acids or weak acids/bases, use the appropriate stoichiometric ratio.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Cross-topic links

- **VI. Microscopic World II**: concentration and dilution are central to volumetric analysis.
- **VII. Redox reactions, chemical cells and electrolysis**: redox reactions are distinct from acid-base reactions.
- **X. Chemical Equilibrium**: weak acid/base dissociation is an equilibrium process.

#### Exam technique

- **For pH questions**: state the type of acid (strong/weak), write the dissociation equation, calculate [H⁺], then apply pH = -log[H⁺].
- **For titration questions**: write the balanced equation, identify the stoichiometric ratio, then apply c_aV_a = (ratio) × c_bV_b.
- **For salt hydrolysis questions**: identify the parent acid and base, classify as strong/weak, then predict the solution's pH.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Concentrated acids are always strong." No — concentration and strength are different properties.
- "All salts are neutral." No — salt hydrolysis can produce acidic, alkaline or neutral solutions.
- "Lemon juice is acidic because it contains H⁺ ions." Lemon juice contains citric acid; the H⁺ ions are released on dissolution in water.
