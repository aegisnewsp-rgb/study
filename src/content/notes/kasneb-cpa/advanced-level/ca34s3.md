# CA34S3: Advanced Management Accounting

Read the paper code before you read anything else. KASNEB's own qualification page lists Advanced Management Accounting as **CA34S3**, and the three specialisation papers as **CA34S1** Advanced Taxation, **CA34S2** Advanced Auditing and Assurance and **CA34S4** Advanced Public Financial Management. A number of third-party sources and older syllabi list Advanced Management Accounting as CA34 and the specialisations as CA35S1, CA35S2 and CA35S3.

This page follows KASNEB. If you are building a study timetable from a source that uses the older codes, check it against KASNEB first — a candidate who registers for the wrong paper has lost a sitting and a fee.

Beyond the papers, three further requirements sit alongside them: CA35P Business Data Analytics, a practical paper; CA36WE, a Workshop on Ethics; and CA37WP, a Workshop on Work Simulation for candidates who do not yet have one year of relevant practical experience.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> The Advanced Level does not withhold the technique from you; it asks whether the technique is the right one for the situation in front of you.

| Paper element | What it settles | The judgement being examined |
|---|---|---|
| The CA34S1 to CA34S4 specialisation codes | Which paper you register for | Whether your timetable matches a current or a superseded syllabus |
| Relevant costing | Whether a cost belongs in a decision at all | Whether the constraint is demand or capacity |
| Transfer pricing | Where profit sits between divisions | Which behaviour the chosen price will actually produce |
| Throughput, scorecards, responsibility accounting, ABC, target and kaizen costing | Which lens fits the situation | The point at which each one stops being reliable |

**Why the paper is called Advanced.** Nothing here withholds a technique you have already met; the difference is that you are now asked to defend the technique against a situation where it would mislead. Almost every Advanced question is that second case, which is why rehearsing the calculation on its own does not transfer.

**What the three hours actually buy.** Because each paper runs for three hours, the items are long enough to carry a scenario, a distractor and a follow-up condition. The marks sit in the follow-up condition — what would have to be true for your chosen method to be the wrong one — and a candidate who stops at the first correct answer leaves that part unwritten.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

> The working picture — which method the question is reaching for, and the condition under which that method starts to mislead.

| Question shape | The method it reaches for | The condition that breaks it |
|---|---|---|
| A decision under a capacity limit | Relevant costing, excluding costs that will not change | The constraint is demand rather than capacity |
| A division resisting a price | Transfer pricing reasoned from incentives | The figure is read as an accounting fact instead |
| A constrained line reported on throughput | Treat everything but truly variable cost as a period cost | The bottleneck is not where throughput is measured |
| A strategy scored on one metric | A balanced scorecard adding non-financial measures | The measures chosen are still the ones that distort |
| Overhead allocation questioned | Driver-based or activity-based costing | The drivers are not genuinely diverse, or the data does not exist |

**Sequence the paper the way the questions are built.** Identifying the technique is fast once the vocabulary is secure; what consumes the three hours is the second half of each item, where you must say what would have to be true for the method to be the wrong one. Drill sets that stop at the calculation under-prepare you for this paper in particular, because they never reach the half that is marked.

### What "advanced" changes

The Intermediate Level teaches you the technique. The Advanced Level examines judgement about when the technique applies and what it costs.

Relevant costing is the clearest example. At Intermediate you learn to exclude fixed costs from a decision because they will not change. At Advanced you are asked what happens when the constraint is capacity rather than demand, when a qualitative factor outweighs a small quantitative gain, when the fixed cost is avoidable after all, or when the information is simply too unreliable to decide. Each of those is a different answer and none of them is found by plugging into the same formula.

Same pattern with performance measurement. Throughput accounting, balanced scorecards, responsibility accounting and transfer pricing each have a case where they are the right tool and a case where they mislead. Advanced questions are almost always about the second case.

### Transfer pricing is the Advanced signature

A transfer price is the price at which one division supplies another. The methods are cost-based, market-based, negotiated and dual, and the choice determines the motivation of both divisions.

A transfer price set at market puts no incentive on the supplying division to reduce its own cost, because the benefit goes to the buying division. A transfer price at full cost transfers profit between divisions without changing the total, which makes divisional performance measures misleading. A negotiated price sets both incentives but needs negotiation every period.

An item that gives you the same cost and market transfer prices and asks why a division is resisting one of them is testing this directly. Reason from the incentives, not from the arithmetic.

### Modern approaches and their limits

Throughput accounting treats all costs except the truly variable ones as a single period cost, which is useful when a constraint limits throughput and misleading when the bottleneck is elsewhere.

Balanced scorecards add non-financial measures to a purely financial assessment and reduce the distortion caused by single-metric evaluation, but their weakness is that a measure selected for its strategic relevance still has to be measured, and the selection is a judgement that can be wrong.

Levers of value and absorbers, driver-based costing, activity-based costing, target costing and kaizen costing are all examinable and all share a weakness the exam tests: they require data that an organisation may not have.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (3mo+)

> What separates a candidate who can name the right technique from one who can defend the choice against a division that disagrees.

#### Divisional measures can mislead while every calculation is correct
This is the central idea on the paper, and it is easy to miss precisely because nothing in the arithmetic goes wrong. A transfer price at market hands the benefit of a cost reduction to the buying division, so the supplying division is not incentivised to pursue one. A price at full cost moves profit between divisions without changing the total, so a division can meet its target without the business being any better off. The distortion lives in the measure rather than in the sum, and an item that supplies identical cost and market prices and asks why a division resists one of them is testing exactly that distinction.

#### Modern approaches fail on data availability before they fail on theory
The systems examinable at this level are not competing theories of cost; they are competing demands on information. Take throughput accounting, which reports a constrained line by treating almost everything as a period cost — a defensible answer when the constraint sits where the measure looks, and a misleading one when it does not. Take a balanced scorecard, which repairs the distortion of judging a strategy on a single financial figure by adding non-financial measures, only for the replacement measure to be chosen on strategic relevance and then found unmeasurable in practice. The shared weakness is not that the reasoning is wrong but that the data may not exist. Activity-based costing is the clearest case: it beats absorption costing exactly when the overhead drivers really do differ from one another and tracing each one is expensive, and it loses on both accuracy and running cost when they are not — which is most of the time, and for no gain at all. Treating any of these as unconditionally superior is the error this level exists to expose.

#### The assessment outside the written papers is easy to leave off a plan
The written papers are the part of the qualification a candidate plans against, and the requirements assessed alongside them are the part they assume are already handled. That assumption is the risk. The practical analytics paper, the ethics workshop and the work-simulation workshop each need their own preparation time, and the last of them is tied to how much relevant practical experience you already hold rather than to anything you can revise. A plan built only around written papers can therefore leave a candidate short of a requirement that no revision session was ever going to cover — a considerably worse position than a topic that was merely under-revised.

#### Codes drift, so the registration check is not optional
The specialisation papers are identified by a coding scheme that has changed, and third-party sources still carry the older form. A candidate planning from an old list is not merely mislabelling a topic: registration is submitted against KASNEB's current list, so the paper signed up for may not be the paper prepared. That costs a sitting and a fee, and it stays invisible to a revision timetable, because the timetable looks entirely coherent while being built on the wrong label.

| Advanced question | Where the difficulty actually sits | Error category to log |
|---|---|---|
| Which costing system applies | Deciding whether the constraint is demand or capacity | Formula applied before the constraint is identified |
| Why is this division resisting | Reading the incentive the price creates | Treating the price as a fact rather than a decision |
| Which performance measure to use | Where the measure stops reflecting the objective | Selected for relevance, unmeasurable in practice |
| What is missing from the timetable | The assessment that is not a written paper | A non-written requirement assumed complete |

### Common mistakes

Planning a revision timetable from outdated paper codes. Confirm against KASNEB.

Applying a relevant-costing conclusion without asking whether the constraint is demand or capacity. The answer changes completely.

Treating a transfer price as an accounting fact rather than an incentive. The number is a decision, and the decision changes behaviour.

Forgetting that divisional performance measures can mislead even when every individual calculation is correct. The distortion is in the measure, not the arithmetic.

Assuming activity-based costing is always better than traditional absorption costing. It is more accurate where the overhead drivers are genuinely diverse and expensive to allocate, and less accurate — and much more costly to operate — where they are not.

Leaving CA35P, CA36WE and CA37WP off the plan because they are not written papers. They are assessed separately.
