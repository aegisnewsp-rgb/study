# Acute Prescribing and Safe Medication in PLAB 2

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review

PLAB 2 is an objective structured clinical examination built from 16 scenarios of 8 minutes each, and the GMC assesses three domains in every one of them: data gathering and assessment, clinical management, and interpersonal skills. Management is therefore not optional in a station — the examiner is watching what you do about the patient, not only what you diagnose.

Prescribing is the most commonly practised management skill in the format, and the most commonly failed, because the medicine is often right and the prescription is not safe.

**Most of what gets lost sits after the drug name.**

- The stem usually hands you the medicine; the prescription fields are the part being marked
- A drug named with no verb is not an instruction that anybody could act on
- Duration is the first field to disappear when the clock is pressing
- Where the medicine carries a ceiling, that ceiling is part of what makes the order safe rather than merely effective

**Four checks, said before you commit rather than after.**

- Allergy status goes first, because a station that volunteered it is testing it
- Weight only changes the answer when the dose is weight-based, and paediatric scenarios always are
- Renal and hepatic function decide whether the familiar drug is the correct one
- The medication list on the stem exists to be checked against whatever you are about to prescribe

**The arithmetic that fails is a unit problem, not a sum problem.**

- The gap between kilograms and milligrams is a factor of a thousand, and it causes more harm than any other slip in the format
- Writing the conversion out is what stops it happening silently in your head
- Concentration changes the volume you need, never the nominal dose
- Adult epinephrine, fluid resuscitation and analgesia in the unwell are the calculations worth learning properly

**Saying what happens next is close to free marks.**

- Naming the monitoring tells the examiner you treat prescribing as a decision, not a transaction
- A review interval answers the same question in one short sentence
- Where renal function was not supplied, saying what you would check beats assuming normal kidneys

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study

#### Write the whole prescription, every time

A station that asks for a prescription is usually not asking you to name a drug. It is assessing whether you produce a complete, legal, checkable order. That means the drug, the dose, the route, the frequency, the duration, and — where it applies — the indication, the maximum dose and the monitoring requirement.

Verb is the most commonly forgotten field and the easiest mark to lose. "Paracetamol 1g" is not an order. "Paracetamol 1g orally, now, then 1g every six hours to a maximum of 4g in 24 hours" is. If the examiner says "say what you would prescribe", say the whole thing out loud including the maximum.

#### Check before you commit

Four checks convert a prescription from correct to safe, and doing them out loud signals that you are doing them.

Check the allergy status before the drug. It is on most station stems and it is the check examiners watch for, because an unsafe prescription in a station that explicitly supplied the allergy is a marked failure rather than a near miss.

Check the weight and whether the dose is weight-based. Most adult prescriptions are not, but paediatric and some adult ones are, and a fixed mg/kg that ignores the stated weight is wrong however familiar the drug.

Check renal and hepatic function before renally or hepatically cleared drugs. In a station with an abnormal creatinine, the safe dose or the contraindicated drug is the point of the scenario.

Check interactions against what is already on the medication list. The list is there to be used; a prescription that ignores it is the classic PLAB 2 hazard.

#### Weight-based and high-risk drugs

Learn a small number of emergency calculations properly rather than a large number badly. Adult epinephrine in cardiac arrest, adult and paediatric fluid resuscitation, and analgesia dosing in the unwell are the ones that appear most.

Get the units right, because that is where the errors live. A weight of 70 kg is 70,000 mg, not 70 mg — the factor of a thousand between kilograms and milligrams is responsible for more medication errors than any other arithmetic slip. Write the conversion out every time: $70\,\text{kg} = 70{,}000\,\text{mg}$. And check the concentration of the ampoule before dividing, because a 1 mg/mL presentation and a 10 mg/mL presentation need very different volumes from the same nominal dose.

#### Common mistakes

Naming the drug without the full order. Lose the verb, the route or the duration and the item is incomplete however good the medicine.

Not stating a maximum dose when the drug has one. The maximum is what makes the prescription safe rather than merely effective.

Forgetting to check allergies out loud. The examiner is looking for the safety habit, not only for a safe drug.

Confusing kilograms with milligrams. Write the conversion.

Prescribing a dose appropriate to the patient\'s renal function when the station did not supply a creatinine. If renal function is not given, say what you would check — the answer that acknowledges the unknown beats the answer that silently assumes normal kidneys.

Stopping at the prescription instead of saying what you would monitor and when you would review. That sentence costs five seconds and usually carries the monitoring marks.

### 🔴 Extended — Mastery

#### Why the verb decides whether the item scored at all

A prescription is documentation before it is pharmacology, and an examiner marking one is checking whether a nurse could act on it without asking you anything. That reframes the task: the question is not whether your drug is appropriate, it is whether your order is complete enough to be checked. Two candidates can choose the identical medicine and score differently because one of them specified a route, a frequency and a limit while the other named a substance.

This is why the fields have to be spoken rather than implied. Spoken, an omission is auditable. Implied, the examiner has to infer what you meant and cannot distinguish a deliberate simplification from an oversight. The habit that fixes it is verbalising each field as you reach it, in order, which costs seconds and removes the possibility of skipping one.

#### The medication list is the clue being handed to you

Stems in this format routinely carry a current medication list even when the scenario is about a new problem. It is the easiest part of the stem to skip, because it does not read like the presenting complaint, and skipping it is what produces the classic hazard.

The check is cheap and mechanical: name each drug on the list, then say what your proposed drug adds, removes or antagonises. That framing works because it does not require memorising interaction tables. It requires only that you have looked, and the interactions that matter in a station are usually structural rather than exotic — a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory added alongside an ACE inhibitor or a diuretic, a sedating agent added to someone already drowsy, a duplicated class.

Treat a list you have not read as unknown rather than as an absence. That distinction is the difference between reasoning from the stem and reasoning from memory.

#### Concentration changes the volume, not the dose

Two presentations of the same nominal dose exist precisely so that a candidate who knows the drug still has to read the ampoule. A milligram-per-milliLitre preparation and one an order of magnitude stronger need very different volumes to deliver the same amount, and the number on the label is the only thing separating them.

The habit that prevents the error is unglamorous: state the concentration before you divide. It costs a sentence, it is visible to the examiner, and it makes the arithmetic checkable rather than internal. It also guards the reverse mistake, where a familiar volume is carried over from a preparation you are not actually holding.

#### What the same scenario sounds like done well

Using only the figures already in this note, a complete order sounds like this: the drug, the route, the timing, the interval and the ceiling, all stated — paracetamol one gram orally now, then one gram every six hours to a maximum of four grams in twenty-four hours. Set beside a drug named alone, the second version is not a worse prescription; it is not a prescription at all.

The same discipline governs a weight-based calculation. Seventy kilograms is seventy thousand milligrams, and a candidate who reaches the right answer without having written the conversion has demonstrated a fact rather than a method. Examiners mark the written version, because the written version is the one that cannot be wrong by accident.
