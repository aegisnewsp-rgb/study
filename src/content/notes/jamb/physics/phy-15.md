# Modern Physics: Photoelectric Effect

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

The **photoelectric effect** is the ejection of electrons from a clean metal surface when light of frequency ≥ **threshold frequency (f₀)** strikes it. Electrons emitted are called **photoelectrons**; each carries energy equal to the energy of one incident **photon** minus the metal's **work function (φ)**.

#### Must-know formulas:

- Photon energy: **E = hf**, where h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s and f is in Hz.
- Work function: **φ = hf₀** (minimum energy to free one electron).
- Einstein's equation: **KE_max = hf − φ**.
- Stopping potential: **eVₛ = hf − φ**.

**High-yield pointers:** Intensity changes only the *number* of photoelectrons per second, never their kinetic energy. A graph of **KE_max vs f** is a straight line of slope **h** crossing the f-axis at **f₀**. Emission is instantaneous once f ≥ f₀.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Threshold condition and work function

Classical wave theory predicted that brighter light should always knock electrons out of any metal. Experiments by Hallwachs and Lenard contradicted this: no electrons leave unless the incident frequency reaches a metal-specific **threshold frequency f₀**. The energy needed to liberate one electron from the surface is the **work function φ**, measured in joules (or electron-volts, 1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J). Typical values: caesium ≈ 1.9 eV, potassium ≈ 2.3 eV, sodium ≈ 2.75 eV, zinc ≈ 4.3 eV.

#### Einstein's photoelectric equation

A photon of frequency f delivers its entire quantum hf to a single electron. Part of this energy overcomes φ; the rest becomes kinetic energy:

> **KE_max = hf − φ = h(f − f₀)**

Because the surplus depends only on f (not intensity), doubling the brightness doubles the photoelectron current but leaves KE_max unchanged. This single result overturned the wave-only picture of light.

#### Stopping potential and the Vₛ–f graph

The **stopping potential Vₛ** is the minimum reverse voltage that halts the fastest photoelectrons. Setting KE_max = eVₛ gives **Vₛ = (h/e)f − φ/e**, a straight line whose **slope is h/e ≈ 4.14 × 10⁻¹⁵ V·s** and whose negative x-intercept is f₀. JAMB often provides a Vₛ–f graph and asks for h from the slope.

#### JAMB question patterns

- Compute φ from a given f₀, or KE_max from f and φ.
- Read f₀ off a KE–f graph; find h from its slope.
- State why classical theory fails (no threshold, wrong intensity dependence, time lag).

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Threshold wavelength and quick conversions

The **threshold wavelength** λ₀ = c/f₀ = hc/φ. Above λ₀ (lower frequency, e.g. red light on sodium) no emission occurs even at huge intensity — a classic JAMB trap. Convert safely: φ (J) ÷ (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹) gives φ in eV; multiply KE (J) by the same factor for eV units.

#### Common mistakes examiners exploit

- Writing **KE = hf** without subtracting φ — always subtract.
- Treating intensity and frequency as interchangeable.
- Mixing eV and joules without the 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ conversion.
- Reading the wrong axis on a Vₛ–f graph (x-intercept is −φ/h, not φ).

#### Worked micro-example

Light of frequency 8.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz hits a metal whose work function is 2.0 eV. Photon energy: E = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴)(8.0 × 10¹⁴) = 5.30 × 10⁻¹⁹ J ≈ 3.31 eV. Maximum KE = 3.31 − 2.0 = **1.31 eV**. Stopping potential Vₛ = **1.31 V**.

#### Links to adjacent JAMB topics

Connects to **wave–particle duality**, the **de Broglie wavelength**, and the **Bohr model** (same h, same quanta). Compare with the **Compton effect** — another photon-electron collision confirming particle behaviour.

#### Practice prompts

1. A metal has φ = 2.5 eV and is illuminated by 300 nm ultraviolet light. Find the maximum kinetic energy of emitted electrons and the stopping potential.
2. A Vₛ–f graph has slope 4.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ V·s and x-intercept 5.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz. Determine Planck's constant and the work function.
