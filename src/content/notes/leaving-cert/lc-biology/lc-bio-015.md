# Transport and Exchange in Organisms — Leaving Certificate (Established) Biology Notes

This is the highest-weighted unit in the whole Biology catalogue at **weight 5**, and it is the widest: membrane transport, the urinary system, the digestive system, gaseous exchange, the circulatory system and plant transport — six substantial topics in one unit. A paper with two 20-mark questions will more often than not draw one from here and one from unit 2.2. This is where the marks are, and it is also the unit where the surface-area-to-volume idea unlocks everything else.

That idea is worth stating plainly, because it explains why almost every structure in this unit looks the way it does. A small organism exchanges enough relative to its volume because it is small. As an organism grows, its volume increases faster than its surface area, so exchange across a bare surface becomes too slow. Evolution's answer, everywhere in this unit, is the same: **internalise and branch the surface**. The alveoli, the villi, the gills, the kidney tubules and the leaf's internal air spaces all do the same thing — take a small external surface and multiply it until it is enormous.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Diffusion** — movement of particles down a concentration gradient, no energy required, no membrane needed.
- **Osmosis** — movement of water molecules across a **selectively permeable membrane** from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential.
- **Active transport** — movement of ions or molecules **against** a concentration gradient, requiring energy from ATP and membrane carrier proteins.
- **Factors affecting osmosis rate**: **temperature, concentration gradient, surface area** (specification). Higher temperature gives faster molecular movement; a steeper gradient gives a faster net flow; a larger membrane surface gives more total flow.
- **Osmosis in food preservation and plant health.** Adding salt or sugar to food draws water out of the bacterial cells by osmosis, and the high external solute concentration prevents further water uptake, so bacteria dehydrate and cannot grow. This is why food does not spoil in brine, jam or salted meat. In a plant, waterlogging occurs when soil is saturated so oxygen cannot diffuse to root cells, killing them and causing root rot; conversely, a plant placed in a concentrated solution plasmolyses, with the protoplast pulling away from the cell wall.
- **Urinary system**: **kidneys, ureters, urinary bladder, urethra**, filtering blood and removing waste while regulating body fluids.
- **The nephron**: **glomerulus** (a dense capillary ball) → **Bowman's capsule** → **proximal convoluted tubule** (in the cortex; most reabsorption happens here) → **loop of Henle** (into the medulla; establishes the osmotic gradient) → **distal convoluted tubule** → **collecting duct**.
- **Filtration** at the glomerulus is driven by blood pressure, not by ATP. Small solutes and water are forced into Bowman's capsule; **cells and large proteins stay in the blood**. **Selective reabsorption** returns all the water, glucose, amino acids and some salts to the blood, driven by active transport. Urea, excess salts and some water are not reabsorbed and leave as urine.
- **ADH**, released from the pituitary, controls water reabsorption in the kidney. When plasma is concentrated, more ADH is released, more water is reabsorbed, and a smaller volume of more concentrated urine is produced. When plasma is dilute, ADH release falls and dilute urine is produced in larger volume.
- **Digestion**: **ingestion → digestion → absorption → egestion**, through the **mouth, oesophagus, stomach, pancreas, liver, gallbladder, small intestine, large intestine, rectum, anus**.
- **Mechanical breakdown** by chewing with the **teeth** and by **peristalsis** — wave-like muscular contractions of the oesophagus that also move material along without gravity's help.
- **Chemical breakdown.** **Bile salts**, made in the liver and stored in the gallbladder, **emulsify** fats into small droplets, increasing surface area for lipase. **Amylase** (salivary and pancreatic) digests starch to maltose at pH about 7; **protease** (pepsin, from the stomach) digests protein at pH about 2; **lipase** (pancreatic) digests fats in the small intestine at alkaline pH. The specification asks for the **production site**, the **pH at a named location**, and the **products**.
- **Absorption** occurs in the **villi** of the small intestine. Folds, villi and microvilli multiply the surface area. Digested nutrients enter the blood and lymph; fats enter the lacteals. The **hepatic portal vein** carries absorbed nutrients to the **liver**, where **glycogen** is stored and amino acids are **deaminated** (amino group removed, ammonia converted to urea).
- **Gaseous exchange** in the **alveoli**, which are the site of enormous surface area, very thin walls, and a moist lining to keep gases in solution. The **breathing mechanism** uses the **diaphragm** and **intercostal muscles** under control from the **brain**. **CO₂ levels** are the main control factor in the breathing mechanism: a rise in blood CO₂ lowers pH, detected by chemoreceptors, and increases rate and depth of breathing.
- **Circulatory system**: **septum** separating the two sides; **atria** receiving blood; **ventricles** pumping it out; **valves** preventing backflow; **cardiac muscle tissue** generating the beat.
- **Two-circuit system**: blood goes **heart → lungs → heart → body → heart**. The **pulmonary circuit** carries deoxygenated blood to the lungs; the **systemic circuit** carries oxygenated blood to the body. The **portal circuit** diverts blood through a second capillary bed — the hepatic portal vein to the liver — before it returns to the heart.
- **Pacemaker** — cells in the right atrium set the heartbeat rhythm and rate, and the cardiac cycle does not need to be voluntary. The heart muscle is supplied by the **coronary arteries** and drained by the **cardiac veins**.
- **Blood composition**: **red blood cells** (haemoglobin, carry O₂), **white blood cells** (immune function), **platelets** (clotting), **plasma** (the liquid carrying nutrients, hormones, antibodies and wastes). **Blood groups** (A, B, AB, O) and the **Rhesus factor** matter for safe transfusion — the knowledge has direct clinical value.
- **Arteries, veins, capillaries**: arteries carry blood *away* from the heart, under high pressure, with thick muscular elastic walls and a round lumen; veins carry blood *towards* the heart, under low pressure, with thin walls, wide lumen and valves; capillaries have walls one cell thick to allow exchange, but carry blood very slowly.
- **Plant tissues**: dermal (protective), ground (storage, support), vascular (**xylem** and **phloem**).
- **Water and minerals** move up in **xylem** by **transpiration pull** (cohesion of water molecules along the column, adhesion to the walls of xylem vessels, and tension generated by water evaporating from leaf surfaces), with **root pressure** as a minor additional force.
- **Transpiration** is the loss of water vapour from a plant, mainly through **stomata** and to a lesser extent through **lenticels** in the bark. **CO₂ diffuses in and water vapour diffuses out** through the stomatal pore; CO₂ concentration controls stomatal opening and closing.
- **Factors affecting transpiration rate** (specification): **air movement, temperature, surface area, presence or absence of cuticle, light intensity, CO₂ concentration.**

#### Examiner traps

- **Saying osmosis moves solute.** Osmosis is the movement of **water**; solute moves by diffusion.
- **Reversing the direction of osmosis.** Water moves from higher water potential (more dilute) to lower water potential (more concentrated).
- **Confusing filtration with selective reabsorption.** Filtration is passive and pressure-driven at the glomerulus; reabsorption is active and selective along the tubule.
- **Saying the small intestine absorbs everything.** Cellulose and some other carbohydrates are not digested by human enzymes and pass through as fibre.
- **Naming "the diaphragm" as the only breathing muscle.** Intercostal muscles are also required to change the volume of the thoracic cavity.
- **Confusing arteries and veins by oxygen content.** Arries carry blood *away* from the heart; the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood and the pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood.
- **Saying phloem carries water.** Phloem carries sugars and other organic solutes; xylem carries water and minerals.
- **Leaving a transpiration graph unlabelled.** Independent variable on x-axis, rate on y-axis, units on both.

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### Surface area to volume — the idea that explains the unit

Take a cube of side *L*. Surface area = 6L²; volume = L³; the ratio = 6/L. **Double the side length and the ratio halves.** So a large organism, with a low surface-area-to-volume ratio, cannot supply its volume fast enough by diffusion across a bare surface. Four solutions appear repeatedly in this unit:

- **Lungs:** the trachea branches into bronchioles into **alveoli**, giving a surface area of roughly 70 m² in an adult — a huge increase over the few square metres of the body's outer surface. Very thin walls (one cell) give a short diffusion distance, and a moist lining keeps O₂ and CO₂ dissolved so they can diffuse.
- **Small intestine:** circular **folds**, **villi** and **microvioles** multiply the surface of a tube that is already 6–8 m long.
- **Plants:** the internal air spaces behind the stomata and the branching venation put the photosynthetic tissue close to an internal air surface rather than exposing it.
- **Kidneys:** each kidney contains about a million nephrons, so filtering surface area is very large.

*Worked question.* A single-celled aquatic organism of side 1 mm has a surface-area-to-volume ratio of 6:1. A similar multicellular organism of side 10 mm has 6:10. Explain the consequences.
*A.* The ratio has fallen by a factor of ten, so the larger organism has ten times the volume to supply and only one-tenth of the surface per unit volume to supply it with. Diffusion across a bare surface is no longer fast enough to meet demand, so the organism must either develop specialised exchange surfaces (gills, a vascular system) or keep its cells small and near the surface. This is the reasoning behind the whole unit — from the branching of the bronchioles to the villi to the internal air spaces of a leaf.

#### The nephron, step by step

1. Blood arrives at the **glomerulus** under high pressure.
2. **Filtration** is pressure-driven: water and small solutes — glucose, amino acids, urea, ions — are forced into **Bowman's capsule**. Blood cells and plasma proteins are too large to pass and remain in the blood.
3. **Selective reabsorption** begins in the **proximal convoluted tubule** (cortex): essentially **all** the glucose, amino acids and most of the water and salts are actively or passively returned to the surrounding capillaries. This is why diabetic kidney damage, where glucose spills into urine, is a serious condition.
4. In the **loop of Henle** (into the medulla), the medullary osmotic gradient is established by the counter-current system; descending limbs lose water, ascending limbs are impermeable to water and pump out salts.
5. In the **distal convoluted tubule** and **collecting duct**, further adjustments occur, and **ADH** determines how much water is reabsorbed.
6. What remains — **urea, excess salts, some water and a little urea** — leaves as urine via the ureter, bladder and urethra. The kidney therefore does two jobs the specification states explicitly: **removing waste** and **regulating body fluids**.

#### Circulation — the routes

- **Pulmonary:** right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lung capillaries → pulmonary vein → left atrium. Sends deoxygenated blood to the lungs.
- **Systemic:** left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body capillaries → vena cava → right atrium. Delivers oxygenated blood to the tissues.
- **Portal:** a third route in which blood passes through **two capillary beds before returning to the heart** — the hepatic portal vein carries nutrients from the small intestine to the liver, allowing the liver to process absorbed nutrients and store glycogen before they reach the rest of the body.

*Worked question.* A person has a damaged left ventricle. Explain the effect on the two circuits.
*A.* The left ventricle pumps oxygenated blood into the systemic circuit. If its contraction is weakened, less blood is pumped to the body tissues, so oxygen and nutrient supply to the organs falls and waste removal by the kidneys and liver is reduced. The pulmonary circuit is affected because the weakened left ventricle returns less blood to the left atrium, so the volume returning to the right side of the heart may change, altering the pressure the right ventricle must work against. The immediate clinical sign is reduced cardiac output and the accumulation of fluid in the lungs because the pressure backs up through the pulmonary circuit.

#### Plant transport

- **Xylem** carries water and dissolved mineral ions **upward**. The main mechanism is **transpiration pull**: water evaporates from the moist mesophyll cell walls into the leaf air spaces and out through the stomata, creating a tension that pulls a continuous column up the xylem. **Cohesion** between water molecules (hydrogen bonding) keeps the column unbroken; **adhesion** to the xylem wall helps resist the pull. **Root pressure** contributes, especially at night when transpiration is low, by osmosis into the xylem in the root.
- **Phloem** carries **photosynthetic products — sucrose** — from sources (leaves) to sinks (growing tissues, roots, fruits) by **mass flow**, driven by the osmotic movement of water between a source and a sink.
- **Stomata** open and close to balance gas exchange against water loss, and their aperture is controlled by **CO₂ concentration** among other factors — the specification names it explicitly.

*Worked question.* The rate of transpiration in a plant is measured with a potometer. State the apparatus and explain how the result is calculated.
*A.* A potometer is a sealed glass tube containing water, connected to a tube containing the cut shoot, with an air bubble in the capillary between the water and the shoot. As the shoot loses water by transpiration, water is drawn from the reservoir, the bubble moves along the capillary, and the **rate of movement of the bubble** is recorded with a ruler over time. The result is **relative** to the cut shoot, not absolute water loss, because only part of the transpiration stream is being measured. Control for temperature, light and humidity, use several shoots per condition, and repeat.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Worked Higher-level questions with answers

**Q1.** Explain why glucose is absent from urine in a healthy person but present in someone with untreated diabetes.
*A.* Glucose is filtered out of the blood at the glomerulus and enters Bowman's capsule with the rest of the filtrate, because it is small enough to pass. In health it is completely reabsorbed in the proximal convoluted tubule by active transport, so none reaches the distal tubule and none is excreted. In untreated diabetes the blood glucose concentration exceeds the kidney's transport maximum, so the carriers and transporters are saturated and cannot reabsorb all of it. The excess glucose remains in the filtrate, raises its osmotic concentration, and water follows it by osmosis — which is why untreated diabetes also causes large volumes of dilute urine and thirst. This is a standard Higher-level question because every step links a transport process to a named structure to a clinical observation.

**Q2.** Explain how breathing rate controls blood CO₂ rather than the other way round.
*A.* CO₂ is constantly produced by respiration of every cell. At rest the CO₂ concentration in the blood is close to its set point, and chemoreceptors in the **medulla oblongata** detect any rise. Rising blood CO₂ forms carbonic acid, lowering pH, and the chemoreceptors respond by increasing the **rate and depth** of breathing, so more CO₂ is expelled and the level returns to the set point. When CO₂ falls — during vigorous exercise — the same mechanism reduces ventilation. So the set point is CO₂ concentration and the effector is the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, not the other way round. This is the system the specification describes when it says "CO₂ as a controlling factor in the breathing mechanism".

**Q3.** Design an investigation into the effect of light intensity on the rate of transpiration.
*A.* **Apparatus:** a potometer per light level, a beaker of water, capillary tubing with an air bubble, a metre rule, a lamp with a fixed wattage, and a heat shield (a beaker of water between lamp and plant) to prevent heating confounding light intensity with temperature. **Independent variable:** light intensity, varied by moving the lamp and measuring the distance. **Dependent variable:** rate of movement of the bubble along the capillary, in mm per minute, converted to relative transpiration rate. **Controlled variables:** shoot length and leaf area, time of day, temperature (monitored with a thermometer), humidity, CO₂ concentration, and the potometer water level. **Controls:** a shoot with the leaves removed or covered, to show the measured movement is due to transpiration from leaves rather than stem evaporation; and a dark condition as a baseline. **Repeats:** at least three shoots per light level, with means calculated. **Analysis:** plot relative rate against light intensity; expect a rise then a plateau as stomata begin to close under heat and water stress. **Safety:** keep water away from the lamp's electrics.

**Q4.** Compare artery, vein and capillary as adapted to their functions.
*A.* **Arteries** carry blood at high pressure away from the heart, so they have **thick muscular elastic walls** to withstand and smooth the pressure surge, a **round lumen** to maintain high flow at high pressure, and elastic fibres (in the aorta especially) to recoil and maintain pressure between beats. **Veins** carry blood at low pressure towards the heart, so they have **thin walls**, a **wide lumen**, and crucially **valves** to prevent backflow under gravity. **Capillaries** are where exchange happens, so they have walls **one cell thick** to minimise diffusion distance and are wide enough to allow cells to pass through into tissue fluid; they trade pressure for surface area, so blood flow is very slow. Each feature maps directly to the pressure and function, which is what the comparison question reads for.

#### How this unit pays elsewhere

- **Unit 2.2** — oxygen and CO₂ produced and consumed there; the leaf and algal surfaces in that unit are the same exchange surfaces designed here.
- **Unit 2.4** — ADH is the endocrine control for the kidney, and adrenaline acts through the circulatory system.
- **Unit 2.6 itself feeds unit 3.1** — carbon dioxide uptake and water loss through stomata are the two fluxes a plant ecosystem models.
- **Unit 3.1** — population curves depend on carrying capacity, and carrying capacity depends on water, oxygen, space, temperature and food.
- **Biology in Practice Investigation** (40% of the grade) — transpiration rate, digestion, breath rate before and after exercise, or osmosis across a semi-permeable membrane are all natural projects producing primary data with a hypothesis and a justified conclusion.

#### Mistakes students actually make

1. **Defining osmosis without naming the selectively permeable membrane**, or describing it as movement down a concentration gradient without specifying *water*.
2. **Treating filtration as selective.** Everything small is filtered; selectivity comes later, in the reabsorption.
3. **Leaving out the pH when giving an enzyme in digestion.** The specification asks for the production site, the pH at a named location, and the products — three items, three marks.
4. **Naming the hepatic portal vein without saying what it does.** Two capillary beds; nutrients go to the liver first.
5. **Describing arteries as oxygenated.** Define them by direction of travel from the heart.
6. **Giving a transpiration factor list without the mechanism.** "Higher temperature increases transpiration" is half; "because it raises the rate of water evaporation from the mesophyll cell walls, increasing the water potential gradient into the leaf air spaces" is the mark.
7. **Forgetting that osmosis needs a membrane.** In an open container, or across a freely permeable membrane, solutes simply diffuse and no osmosis occurs.
8. **Ignoring the control in an investigation plan.** The marks in a practical question are mostly in the variables, the control, the repeats and the safety, not in the description of the apparatus.
