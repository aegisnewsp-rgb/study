# Inflation: Types, Causes, and RBI's Anti-Inflationary Policy

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Inflation** is a sustained rise in the general price level of goods and services, eroding the **purchasing power of money**. The RBI measures it primarily through the **Consumer Price Index (CPI) – Combined**, base year 2012 = 100, and operates a **flexible inflation targeting** regime with a **4% target ±2% tolerance band** (operational range 2%–6%).

**Inflation rate formula:** `Inflation (%) = [(CPI_n − CPI_{n−1}) / CPI_{n−1}] × 100`, where CPI_n and CPI_{n−1} are the current and previous period index numbers.

#### Key high-yield pointers:
- WPI tracks wholesale/producer prices; **RBI targets CPI, not WPI**.
- Three headline types — **demand-pull**, **cost-push**, and **built-in** (wage-price spiral); plus **stagflation** (high inflation + stagnant growth + high unemployment).
- Anti-inflationary toolkit: **repo rate hike**, **OMO (security sales)**, higher **CRR/SLR**, **MSF corridor**, and **moral suasion**; the framework is enforced by the **6-member MPC** (3 RBI + 3 external, chaired by the Governor).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Defining and Measuring Inflation

Inflation is quantified through index numbers — the **CPI (Combined)**, **WPI**, and the **GDP deflator**. The CPI basket captures retail spending of urban and rural households and is what the RBI officially targets under Section 4 of the RBI Act (amended in 2016). WPI captures wholesale/producer-side prices and remains useful for industrial analysis but no longer drives policy. The GDP deflator (`Nominal GDP / Real GDP × 100`) covers the entire domestic economy and is broader than CPI.

#### Types by Driver and by Speed

- **Demand-pull inflation** occurs when aggregate demand outruns aggregate supply — often linked to excess money supply or expansionary fiscal policy.
- **Cost-push inflation** arises from supply-side shocks: crude oil prices, administered fuel/electricity hikes, commodity spikes, or supply-chain disruptions.
- **Built-in inflation** is the **wage-price spiral**, where past inflation feeds expectations and workers demand compensatory wage hikes.
- **Stagflation** combines rising prices with stagnant output and high unemployment, breaking the short-run Phillips Curve trade-off.

By speed: **creeping** (below 3%), **walking** (3%–10%), **galloping** (above 10%), and **hyperinflation** (above 50% per month).

#### RBI's Anti-Inflationary Toolkit

| Tool | Mechanism | Typical Use |
|---|---|---|
| Repo Rate Hike | Raises short-term borrowing cost, cools credit | Primary signalling instrument |
| OMO (Sale of G-Secs) | Absorbs banking system liquidity | When surplus liquidity persists |
| CRR / SLR Hike | Locks a larger share of deposits with RBI/Government | Crude but powerful liquidity drain |
| MSF / Reverse Repo | Sets the corridor for overnight rates | Fine-tunes inter-bank rates |
| Moral Suasion | RBI's verbal guidance to banks | Used alongside quantitative tools |

The MPC meets at least four times a year; if CPI breaches 2%–6% for **three consecutive quarters**, the Central Government, after consulting the MPC, can issue formal directions to the RBI.

#### Common MCQ Traps

- Confusion between **WPI and CPI** as the policy anchor.
- Mis-stating the tolerance band as ±4% instead of ±2%.
- Treating only the repo rate as the policy lever and ignoring OMO/CRR/SLR.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Causes: Demand-Side and Supply-Side in Detail

**Demand-side drivers** include excessive **money supply growth** (linked to the Quantity Theory: `M × V = P × T`), expansionary fiscal deficits financed via RBI, rising disposable income, credit booms, and population-driven consumption. The **money multiplier** `m = (1 + c) / (c + r + e)` determines how much deposit creation follows a base-money injection, where c is the currency-deposit ratio, r the reserve requirement, and e the excess reserve ratio.

**Supply-side drivers** include imported crude inflation (channeled through the **REER** and INR pass-through), administered price revisions for fuel and electricity, monsoon-driven food inflation, and structural rigidities in factor markets. Imported inflation is particularly important because RBI's **forex market intervention** — selling USD to defend the INR — is itself an anti-inflation tool that aspirants often overlook.

#### Fisher Equation and Real Interest Rate

The **Fisher equation** links nominal rates to real rates and expected inflation: `Real Rate ≈ Nominal Rate − Inflation Rate`. When inflation rises unexpectedly, real rates fall, hurting savers and creditors. Aspirants frequently assume nominal and real rates always move together; in reality, during quantitative easing or when policy rates lag inflation, real rates can turn **negative**, signalling accommodative financial conditions.

#### Inflation Targeting Framework — Institutional Detail

The 2016 amendment to the RBI Act institutionalised **flexible inflation targeting** with CPI as the nominal anchor. The MPC comprises **six members** — three from the RBI (including the Governor as Chair) and three external experts appointed by the Central Government — to insulate rate decisions from executive pressure. The ±2% band allows the RBI to look through supply shocks while still being accountable.

1. CPI was 158.4 in 2024-25 and 151.2 in 2023-24. Compute the inflation rate and state whether it falls inside the RBI's tolerance band.
2. Briefly explain why a supply-side oil shock is a cost-push rather than demand-pull phenomenon, and identify two RBI tools best suited to respond without choking growth.
