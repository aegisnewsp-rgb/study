# Work, Energy and Power

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Work** is energy transferred by a force through a displacement, measured in **joule (J)**. A force that makes no contribution to motion along its line of action does zero work; a force opposing motion does negative work. **Energy** is the capacity to do work, and **power** measures how fast that work happens.

| Term | Formula | SI unit |
|---|---|---|
| Work (constant force) | $W = F d \cos\theta$ | J (joule) |
| Kinetic energy | $KE = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2}$ | J |
| Gravitational PE | $PE = m g h$ | J |
| Power (average) | $P = W/t$ | W (watt) |
| Efficiency | $\eta = \dfrac{W_{\text{out}}}{W_{\text{in}}} \times 100\%$ | % |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **W-F-d-cosθ** — "**W**ork = **F**orce × **d**isplacement × **cos**(angle)." Always check the angle between $\vec F$ and $\vec d$ before multiplying; $\cos\theta = 0$ means **zero** work, not "no calculation needed."

The **work–energy theorem** ($W_{\text{net}} = \Delta KE$) ties everything together: the net work on a body equals the change in its kinetic energy, regardless of path.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Forces, Displacement, and the Cosine Factor

Work done by a **constant** force is the dot product $W = \vec F \cdot \vec d = F d \cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec F$ and $\vec d$. This makes work a **scalar** with sign:

- $\theta < 90°$: $W > 0$ (force aids motion, e.g., a push on a sled).
- $\theta = 90°$: $W = 0$ (e.g., normal reaction on horizontal travel, weight on horizontal motion).
- $\theta > 90°$: $W < 0$ (force opposes motion, e.g., friction, air drag lifting up on a descending object).

For a **variable** force along a straight line, $W = \int F\,dx$. Geometrically, this equals the **signed area under the $F$–$x$ graph** — a frequent BUET short-question pattern.

#### Kinetic, Potential, and the Work–Energy Theorem

Kinetic energy $KE = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2}$ always stays non-negative and depends on $v^{2}$, so doubling speed **quadruples** KE. Gravitational PE near Earth's surface is $PE = mgh$, with the reference level $h = 0$ chosen by the solver — only **differences** in PE are physical.

The work–energy theorem states $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta KE = KE_f - KE_i$. It collapses Newton's-second-law kinematics into a single scalar equation, letting you skip writing forces individually.

#### Conservative vs Non-conservative Forces

A force is **conservative** when its work around any closed loop is zero (equivalently, the work is path-independent). Gravity and the spring force qualify; **friction does not**. When only conservative forces act, mechanical energy is conserved:

$$KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f$$

If friction does work $W_f$ on the path, the energy balance becomes:

$$KE_i + PE_i + W_{\text{ext}} = KE_f + PE_f + |W_f|$$

#### Power — Average vs Instantaneous

Average power is $P_{\text{avg}} = W/t$. Instantaneous power is $P = dW/dt = \vec F \cdot \vec v$, which reduces to $P = Fv\cos\theta$ for a constant force. The same engine delivers higher power at higher speed; that's why climbing hills in low gear keeps the engine near its maximum $P$.

Efficiency $\eta = (W_{\text{out}}/W_{\text{in}})\times 100\%$ ties input and output: a 60 %-efficient motor converting 500 W of electrical power yields only 300 W of mechanical output, with 200 W lost as heat.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Feature | Work | Kinetic Energy | Potential Energy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Scalar, can be negative | Scalar, always $\geq 0$ | Scalar, relative to reference |
| Depends on | Force, displacement, angle | Mass, speed | Mass, $g$, height (or spring compression) |
| Path-dependent? | Yes (over the actual path taken) | No (state variable) | No for conservative forces |
| SI unit / dimension | J / $[M L^{2} T^{-2}]$ | J / $[M L^{2} T^{-2}]$ | J / $[M L^{2} T^{-2}]$ |

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** A 4 kg block slides down a smooth incline from a height of 5 m. Using energy methods, find its speed at the bottom and the instantaneous power delivered by gravity just before impact. ($g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^{2}$.)

#### Solution:

1. Apply conservation of mechanical energy (smooth incline ⇒ no friction):
$$mgh = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2} \;\Rightarrow\; v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2\times 9.8\times 5} \approx 9.90\,\text{m/s}.$$
2. At the bottom, the velocity direction is along the incline. The component of weight along the incline is $mg\sin\theta$. However, power is most directly computed as $P = \vec F \cdot \vec v$ using the **full** weight (since $\vec v$ has both horizontal and vertical components at any instant except the very bottom — see trap below).
3. At the instant before impact (slope angle $\theta$), $P = mg\,v\sin\theta$. With $h = 5\,\text{m}$ along a 13 m incline (3-4-5 triangle), $\sin\theta = 5/13$, so:
$$P = 4\times 9.8\times 9.90\times\tfrac{5}{13} \approx 149\,\text{W}.$$

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students often write $P = mg\,v$ treating the block as falling straight down. The block moves **along the incline**, so only the component of weight along $\vec v$ contributes to the instantaneous power — multiply by $\sin\theta$, not by 1. Also, do not assume $h = 5\,\text{m}$ means the path length is 5 m; if the incline length is given separately, use that for $\theta$.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Variable Force, Springs, and Beyond

When the force varies along the path (e.g., a spring with $F = -kx$), work becomes $W = \int_{x_i}^{x_f}(-kx)\,dx = -\tfrac{1}{2}k(x_f^{2}-x_i^{2})$. The spring's elastic PE is $U_s = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^{2}$, and energy conservation extends to $KE + U_g + U_s = \text{const}$ for an ideal mass–spring system.

For a **central force** $\vec F(r)$ that depends only on distance from a point, potential energy satisfies $U(r) = -\int F(r)\,dr$ (the minus sign converts from force to PE). This unifies gravity, electrostatics, and interatomic potentials under one framework.

#### Boundary Cases and Mathematical Limits

| Condition | Resulting behaviour | Exam implication |
|---|---|---|
| $\theta = 90°$ between $\vec F$ and $\vec d$ | $W = 0$ despite non-zero force | Normal force on horizontal motion does no work |
| $v \to 0$ at the highest point of vertical throw | $KE = 0$, $PE = mgh_{\max}$ | Verify $h_{\max} = v_0^{2}/(2g)$ for energy conservation |
| $\eta \to 100\%$ | Output equals input, implies frictionless / lossless process | Real machines always satisfy $\eta < 1$ |
| Impulse-style collisions | KE not conserved (inelastic); momentum conserved | Use work–energy only **before** and **after** the collision |
| $m \to 0$ in combined masses | Reduced mass formula governs two-body problems | Rare in BUET but a boundary check on elasticity |

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics

The work–energy theorem for a particle is a special case of the **virial theorem** and the **Hamiltonian formulation** in advanced mechanics. Torque × angular displacement defines rotational work $W = \tau\theta$, and rotational KE $\tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^{2}$ parallels $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2}$ — the same theorems, in rotational dress. BUET occasionally tests these analogies (e.g., flywheel problems).

#### Common Exam-Grade Errors

1. Adding KE arithmetically in inelastic collisions instead of computing $\tfrac{1}{2}(m_1+m_2)v_f^{2}$.
2. Using $g = 10\,\text{m/s}^{2}$ for kinetic estimates and $9.8\,\text{m/s}^{2}$ for potential — pick one and stay consistent.
3. Treating work as a vector and writing $\vec W$ in free-body diagrams.
4. Applying mechanical-energy conservation when friction is non-zero unless the frictional work is subtracted explicitly.
5. Confusing average power (over a journey) with peak power (instantaneous). An engine's *rated* power is usually peak.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. **Power vs velocity profile:** A car of mass 1200 kg accelerates uniformly from rest to 30 m/s in 12 s against a constant resistive force of 600 N. Plot $P(t)$ and compare the average power to the instantaneous power at $t = 12\,\text{s}$.
2. **Spring–block energy audit:** A 2 kg block compresses a spring (k = 800 N/m) by 0.4 m and is released on a rough horizontal surface ($\mu_k = 0.25$). Find the distance the block travels before stopping, accounting for both spring PE and frictional dissipation.

> 📌 **Formula Check:** $1\,\text{J} = 1\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m} = 1\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^{2}/\text{s}^{2}$. Power: $1\,\text{W} = 1\,\text{J/s}$, dimension $[M L^{2} T^{-3}]$. Always check that the units of every answer collapse to J (for energy) or W (for power) before submitting.

#### Exam Strategy for BUET

Work, Energy and Power carries roughly **5 %** of the BUET Admission Physics paper — typically **2–3 MCQs plus 1 short question**. Highest-yield sub-types:

- $W = Fd\cos\theta$ with a given angle diagram.
- Energy conservation on inclined planes and vertical throws.
- Power calculations using $P = Fv$ where $v$ is given or derived from kinematics.
- Efficiency of simple machines (pulleys, levers) — usually one option carries a correctly computed $\eta$.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** A common BUET option pairs the correct speed with the wrong $\theta$, e.g., giving the incline length instead of the height. Always cross-check which length the problem actually states, then compute $\sin\theta$ from $h/\ell$.
