# Assumptions and Implicit Premises — LSAT Logical Reasoning

Assumptions and implicit premises are unstated beliefs the argument depends on. The LSAT tests whether the test-taker can recognise a necessary assumption (the missing link without which the argument collapses) versus a sufficient assumption (a stronger claim that, if true, would guarantee the conclusion). Assumption items appear throughout the LSAT and underpin strengthen/weaken, flaw, and inference items.

### Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

#### What assumption items test

- **Necessary assumption:** the unstated belief that must be true for the argument to hold. Without it, the conclusion does not follow.
- **Sufficient assumption:** the unstated belief that, if true, would guarantee the conclusion.
- **Implicit premise:** a premise that is not explicitly stated but is required for the argument.

#### One-pass drill

1. Identify the conclusion and the premises.
2. Ask: "what must be true for the conclusion to follow from the premises?"
3. The necessary assumption is the answer that, if false, would undermine the argument.

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing necessary and sufficient assumptions. A necessary assumption is the minimum required; a sufficient assumption is more than enough.
- Picking an assumption that strengthens the argument but is not required.
- Confusing an assumption with a premise. Premises are stated; assumptions are unstated.

### Standard — Regular Study (2d-2mo)

#### Identifying necessary assumptions

To find a necessary assumption:

1. State the conclusion.
2. State the explicit premises.
3. Ask: "what must be true for the premises to support the conclusion?"
4. The necessary assumption is the unstated link.

#### Identifying sufficient assumptions

To find a sufficient assumption:

1. State the conclusion.
2. Ask: "what unstated belief, if true, would by itself guarantee the conclusion?"
3. The sufficient assumption is the answer that, combined with the premises, makes the conclusion certain.

#### Common assumption patterns

- **Methodological:** the assumption that the study, sample, or method is representative.
- **Analogical:** the assumption that the two cases being compared are similar in relevant respects.
- **Causal:** the assumption that the correlation reflects a causal relationship, or that no alternative cause is present.
- **Definitional:** the assumption that the terms are used in the same way throughout the argument.

#### Practice routine

- Take 10 assumption items. For each, identify the necessary assumption before looking at the choices.
- Eliminate choices that strengthen but are not required.
- Eliminate choices that are not beliefs (e.g., predictions, conclusions).
- Time yourself: under 1.5 minutes per item.

### Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo-6mo)

#### How assumptions connect to the other LR skills

- **Strengthen items** add evidence for the assumption; weaken items undermine it.
- **Flaw items** often target an unstated assumption that is questionable.
- **Inference items** ask what must be true given the premises, which is similar to a sufficient assumption.
- **Parallel reasoning items** require identifying the same assumption in a different context.

#### Common errors and the correction

- **"Necessary means it is true."** Necessary means the argument requires it, not that the argument proves it.
- **"Sufficient is always true."** Sufficient means it would guarantee the conclusion if true; it may or may not be true.
- **"All assumptions are stated in the argument."** Assumptions are by definition unstated.

#### Exam technique

- For necessary assumption items, use the denial test: if the choice is false, does the argument collapse? If yes, it is necessary.
- For sufficient assumption items, ask: "if this is true, does the conclusion follow?"
- Eliminate choices that are not beliefs (predictions, conclusions, recommendations).
- Use the platform's flag-for-review function for items you cannot answer.
