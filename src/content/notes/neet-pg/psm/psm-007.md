## Chapter: Environment and Health

### 1.1 Water and Health

#### Water Requirements
- Minimum: 20 liters/person/day
- Moderate: 50-100 L/p/d
- Optimal: 150-200 L/p/d

#### Waterborne Diseases (Bradley Classification)

**Waterborne** (Pathogen ingested):
- **Cholera** (Vibrio cholerae): Rice-water stools, severe dehydration
  - Treatment: ORS + Antibiotics (Azithromycin)
  - Prevention: Oral cholera vaccine
- **Typhoid** (Salmonella typhi): Enteric fever, continuous fever
  - Diagnosis: Blood culture (gold standard)
  - Prevention: Vi polysaccharide vaccine (Typbar)
- **Hepatitis A**: Self-limiting, fecal-oral, jaundice
- **Bacillary Dysentery** (Shigella): Bloody diarrhea, tenesmus

**Water-washed** (Insufficient water for hygiene):
- Trachoma (Chlamydia trachomatis): Prevents eye infection with hygiene
- Scabies, skin infections

**Water-based** (Parasite uses water intermediate host):
- **Schistosomiasis** (Bilharzia): Snail as intermediate host
- **Dracunculiasis** (Guinea worm): Cyclops water flea

**Water-related vector** (Vector breeds in water):
- **Malaria** (Anopheles mosquito)
- **Dengue** (Aedes aegypti)
- **Filariasis** (Culex)

### 1.2 Water Quality

#### Indian Standards (BIS 10500:2012)
- pH: 6.5-8.5
- Fluoride: 1.0 mg/L (deficiency → caries; excess → fluorosis)
- Nitrate: 45 mg/L (max)
- Lead: 0.01 mg/L

#### Water Purification
1. **Coagulation/Flocculation**: Alum + lime
2. **Sedimentation**: Flocs settle
3. **Filtration**: Rapid sand filter
4. **Disinfection**: Chlorination

#### Chlorination
- **Breakpoint chlorination**: Add chlorine until free residual persists
- **Free residual chlorine**: 0.2-0.5 mg/L after 30 min

#### Coliform Testing
- **Total coliform**: All coliform bacteria
- **Fecal coliform (E. coli)**: Indicates fecal contamination
- **Standard**: 0 coliform/100 mL for drinking

### 1.3 Air Pollution

#### Criteria Pollutants
1. **PM2.5** (≤2.5μm): Most harmful, alveolar deposition
2. **PM10** (≤10μm): Upper airway
3. **SO₂**: Respiratory irritant, bronchoconstriction
4. **NO₂**: Respiratory irritant
5. **CO**: Carboxyhemoglobin, reduces O₂ carrying capacity
6. **O₃**: Lung damage
7. **Pb**: Neurological damage

#### Health Effects
- **Acute**: Eye irritation, cough, asthma exacerbation
- **Chronic**: COPD, lung cancer, cardiovascular disease, stroke

#### Air Quality Index (AQI) — India
| AQI | Category | Impact |
|-----|---------|--------|
| 0-50 | Good | Minimal |
| 51-100 | Satisfactory | Minor breathing discomfort |
| 101-200 | Moderate | Breathing discomfort to sensitive |
| 201-300 | Poor | Breathing discomfort on prolonged exposure |
| 301-400 | Very Poor | Respiratory illness |
| 401-500 | Severe | Serious health effects |

### 1.4 Indoor Air Pollution

**Source**: Biomass fuel combustion (wood, crop residue, cow dung)
**Primary victims**: Women and children

**Emissions**: PM2.5, CO, PAHs, formaldehyde

**Health Effects**:
- ARI (pneumonia in children)
- COPD, lung cancer, cataracts
- 2.3 million deaths/year globally

**Solution**: Improved cookstoves (Prakashphal), LPG (PMUY - Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana)

### 1.5 Solid Waste Management

#### Municipal Solid Waste Composition
- Biodegradable: 40-50% (food, garden waste)
- Recyclable: 20-30% (paper, plastic, glass)
- Inert: 30-40% (debris, ash)

#### Treatment Methods
- **Composting**: Aerobic/anaerobic
- **Incineration**: Energy recovery
- **Landfill**: Sanitary (engineered)

#### Biomedical Waste (BMW Rules 2016)

| Color | Waste Type | Treatment |
|-------|-----------|-----------|
| Yellow | Anatomical, soiled dressings | Incineration |
| Red | Contaminated recyclable (tubular, IV sets, gloves) | Autoclave/Microwave/Chemical |
| Blue | Glassware, metallic | Autoclave → recycle |
| White (translucent) | Sharps | Autoclave → puncture/shred |

### 1.6 Sewage Treatment

**Primary Treatment**: Physical (screening, sedimentation)
**Secondary Treatment**: Biological (activated sludge)
**Tertiary Treatment**: Chemical (nutrient removal)

### 1.7 Radiation and Health

- **Non-ionizing**: Radio waves, UV (less harmful)
- **Ionizing**: X-rays, gamma rays (harmful)

**Effects**: Acute radiation syndrome, cancer, cataracts

**Protection (Time, Distance, Shielding)**:
- Minimize exposure time
- Maximize distance (inverse square law)
- Use shielding (lead, concrete)

### 1.8 Climate Change

**Health Impacts**:
- Heat-related mortality (heat waves)
- Vector-borne disease range expansion
- Water-borne diseases after floods
- Food insecurity, malnutrition
- Mental health impacts

### 1.9 Occupational Health

#### Hazards
- Physical: Heat, noise, radiation, dust
- Chemical: Lead, mercury, benzene, asbestos
- Biological: Bacteria, viruses
- Ergonomic: Repetitive strain

#### Prevention (Hierarchy of Controls)
1. Elimination
2. Substitution
3. Engineering (ventilation)
4. Administrative
5. PPE (last resort)

#### Factory Act 1948
- Working hours: ≤48 hrs/week
- IMR: ~28/1000 (India 2023)
- ESI Act 1948: Health insurance for factory workers

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Bradley's classification is the most examinable table in this chapter

Water-borne disease is not one category but four, separated by *how the organism reaches the human*. Getting the mechanism right places every disease in the right column without memorising a list.

| Class | Mechanism | Representative disease | Control point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water-borne | Ingested with water | Cholera, typhoid, hepatitis A | Disinfection and safe supply |
| Water-washed | Water absent for hygiene | Trachoma, scabies | Water *quantity* at household |
| Water-based | Water hosts a parasite's stage | Schistosomiasis, dracunculiasis | Snail control, larviciding |
| Water-related vector | Vector breeds in water | Malaria, dengue, filariasis | Drainage, drying of collections |

**Examiner trap:** trachoma is *water-washed*, not water-borne. The organism is not swallowed; it is kept out by washing. Water-washed disease responds to the **availability** of water, water-borne disease to its **purity** — which is why the same tap can fix trachoma and still transmit typhoid.

#### Fluoride is a U-shaped curve

The same element causes disease at both ends, which is why BIS 10500 permits 1.0 mg/L rather than minimising it.

- Below ~0.5 mg/L — dental caries in children, enamel formation compromised
- Around 1.0 mg/L — accepted optimum
- Above ~1.5 mg/L — dental and skeletal **fluorosis**, irreversible, stains bone as well as enamel

"Reduce fluoride" is correct advice in a fluorosis-endemic district and harmful in a caries-endemic one. An MCQ asking for the single correct intervention must say which end of the curve the population sits at; if it does not, the question is defective.

#### Breakpoint chlorination — the curve and the two numbers

Chlorine added to water containing ammonia or reducing compounds is consumed first, producing combined residual chlorine with little disinfecting power. Dosing must continue past that demand until chlorine is free to disinfect.

1. **Demand phase** — chlorine consumed by organic matter, combined residual rises slowly
2. **Breakpoint** — the inflection point; free residual chlorine appears
3. **Residual phase** — free residual rises in proportion to further dosing

Free residual should read **0.2–0.5 mg/L after 30 minutes of contact**. Note these are two different quantities: 30 minutes is contact *time*, 0.2–0.5 is residual *concentration*. Questions routinely interchange them.

| Coliform reading | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| *E. coli* detected | Fecal contamination — chlorination inadequate or source polluted |
| Total coliform present, *E. coli* absent | Non-fecal entry, e.g. industrial or soil |
| Both absent | Fit for drinking — standard 0 per 100 mL |

#### PM2.5 versus PM10 — size decides where it stops

| | PM10 | PM2.5 |
|---|---|---|
| Size | ≤10 µm | ≤2.5 µm |
| Deposition | Upper airway, nose and throat | Reaches alveoli, poorly cleared |
| Outcomes | Cough, irritation, poor visibility | Ischaemic heart disease, stroke, COPD, lung cancer |

Because PM2.5 reaches the gas-exchange surface, it is the fraction that correlates with **mortality**, not merely symptoms. That is why the two carry separate limits and why "which is more harmful" is answered by size, not composition.

#### Indoor air pollution — why the burden falls on women

The fuel, not the person, is the exposure driver. Cooking with wood, crop residue or dung in an unvented kitchen keeps women and young children in the room for hours with PM2.5, carbon monoxide and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons.

- Acute: childhood pneumonia, burns, asthma exacerbation
- Chronic: COPD, lung cancer, cataract, chronic headache
- Intervention ladder: chimney and flue → improved cookstove → **LPG under PMUY** → clean electricity

Answer a "most effective intervention" question with LPG or electricity. A better stove design still burns solid fuel indoors; the clean-fuel programme removes the combustion from the household, and the exposure falls away with it.

#### BMW Rules 2016 — colour is the whole recall aid

| Colour | Contents | Route |
|---|---|---|
| Yellow | Anatomical waste, soiled dressings, expired medicines | Incineration |
| Red | Contaminated recyclable — tubing, IV sets, gloves | Autoclave, microwave or chemical |
| Blue | Glassware, metallic body parts | Autoclave, then recycle |
| White (translucent) | Sharps — needles, blades, broken glass | Autoclave, then shred puncture-proof |

Two rules get mixed up: sharps go in the **translucent white** container and are shredded, never merely recapped; contaminated recyclables go in **red** for autoclaving *before* they can re-enter the recycling stream. Recapping a used needle before disposal is a reliable one-mark distractor and is always wrong.

#### Waste hierarchy — order by end point

1. Prevention — reduce at source
2. Minimisation — reduce mass and volume
3. Reuse — keep the product in service
4. Recycling / composting — reprocess into material
5. Energy recovery — incinerate with heat capture
6. Disposal — engineered landfill, last resort

Composting suits the 40–50% biodegradable fraction. The inert 30–40% of debris and ash gains nothing from it, so "more composting plants" only addresses part of the stream — and a question asking you to rank options should put source reduction above it.

#### Radiation — three controls, and why distance dominates

- **Time** — shorter exposure, smaller absorbed dose
- **Distance** — dose falls with the inverse square law, so doubling distance quarters it
- **Shielding** — attenuate with lead or concrete

Distance is strongest in practice because it is the only control that improves without changing how long someone must stay or how much material must be interposed. Lead suits X-rays and gamma because of its high density; it is nearly useless against alpha.

#### Hierarchy of controls — the most misordered list in public health

1. Elimination — remove the hazard
2. Substitution — a less hazardous material
3. Engineering controls — enclosure, local exhaust ventilation, isolation
4. Administrative controls — training, rosters, standard procedures
5. PPE — respirator, gloves, apron

Effectiveness falls as you descend. Respiratory protection for silica dust is defensible only after ventilation has been tried; naming PPE as the *primary* answer when elimination exists is the standard distractor. PPE depends on correct fit and constant use — both behavioural — whereas an enclosure works whether or not anyone remembers it.

#### Examiner trap sheet

- Sewage **primary** is physical (screening, sedimentation), **secondary** is biological (activated sludge), **tertiary** removes nutrients chemically.
- Bradley separates by **route of transmission**, which is why no two columns are interchangeable.
- Bis standards worth holding as pairs: lead 0.01 mg/L, nitrate 45 mg/L, fluoride 1.0 mg/L.
- Attributing dengue recovery to the vitamin C in guava is wrong; the leaf extract question rests on platelets, not on any antiviral action.
