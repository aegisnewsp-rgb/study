# Reading Comprehension

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Reading Comprehension (RC) on the LAT (Law Admission Test) English section measures your ability to read, interpret, infer, and evaluate passages drawn from legal, socio-political, and academic writing. RC carries roughly 10–12 of the 20 English MCQs, making it the single highest-yield skill inside English. The test gives you about 30 minutes for English, which works out to roughly 2 minutes per RC question once passages are read.

- **Skim first, scan second**: read the first sentence of each paragraph to lock the main idea, then scan for keywords to locate explicit facts.
- **Four question families**: main idea, supporting detail, inference, tone/purpose, and vocabulary-in-context.
- **Answer rule**: paraphrase the correct option in your own words; the right answer is a *restatement*, not a verbatim lift.
- **Negative cues win**: NOT, EXCEPT, ALWAYS, NEVER, ONLY, COMPLETELY are statistically more likely to be the correct option on HEC LAT item patterns.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"SPIN-V"** — the five question types you must master: **S**pecific detail, **P**urpose/tone, **I**nference, **N**egative (EXCEPT/NOT), **V**ocab-in-context. Read the question stem first, label it with one SPIN-V letter, then hunt the passage.

| Question type | What the stem asks | Tactic |
|---|---|---|
| Specific detail | Who/what/when/where | Scan for keyword; answer is explicit |
| Purpose / Tone | Author's intent or attitude | Look at diction + modality verbs |
| Inference | Implicit meaning | Must be *supported*, not invented |
| Negative (NOT/EXCEPT) | Which is NOT true | Mark the negation, then find the outlier |
| Vocab-in-context | Meaning of a phrase | Substitute each option into the sentence |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

RC for the LAT is graded on a passage-plus-items model: one 300–500-word passage (frequently on Pakistani constitutional debate, judicial independence, or contemporary legal commentary) followed by 4–6 MCQs. HEC's official LAT syllabus at `hec.gov.pk` lists English comprehension of legal and general academic texts as a core competency. The other English MCQs (grammar, vocabulary, sentence correction) draw from the same lexical base, so RC doubles as vocabulary training.

#### Core Mechanism: How RC Items Are Built

Each item has a stem, a correct answer, and three distractors engineered to lure specific errors. HEC/NTS LAT items typically deploy: (a) an *outside-knowledge* distractor (true in real life, unsupported in the passage), (b) an *extreme-language* distractor (overstates the author's claim), (c) a *scope-shift* distractor (true of a paragraph but not the whole passage), and (d) the correct restatement. Recognizing which distractor type you are falling for is half the battle.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Concept | Often confused with | How to tell them apart |
|---|---|---|
| Tone | Topic | Tone = author's *attitude* (cynical, reformist, didactic); Topic = *subject* (judicial reform) |
| Inference | Assumption | Inference is *supported by text*; Assumption is a hidden *premise* the author needs but never states |
| Main idea | Supporting detail | Main idea covers the *entire* passage; a detail covers one paragraph or example |
| Explicit fact | Opinion | Explicit = directly stated; Opinion = evaluative judgment signaled by adjectives/verbs |
| Purpose | Main idea | Purpose = *why* the author wrote (to persuade, criticize); Main idea = *what* the author claims |
| Skimming | Scanning | Skim for gist (read selectively); Scan for a known keyword (read locator-only) |

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** *A passage argues that Pakistan's superior judiciary has, since 2007, increasingly asserted itself in executive policy matters through suo motu powers. The author cites the Judges' Case, the 18th/19th/20th Amendments, and the establishment of military courts as evidence. Which of the following best describes the author's tone?*

*(A) Detached and academic, presenting both sides without endorsement*
*(B) Critical and concerned about judicial overreach into democratic functions*
*(C) Supportive of parliamentary sovereignty and critical of judicial activism*
*(D) Indifferent, simply cataloguing constitutional amendments chronologically*

#### Solution:

1. **Locate the evaluative diction**: words such as *"increasingly asserted"*, *"suo motu powers"*, and listing three politically charged examples signal the author is not neutral.
2. **Test Option A**: "both sides without endorsement" — the passage gives *no* counter-argument or pro-judiciary voice, so this fails.
3. **Test Option C**: "supportive of parliamentary sovereignty" — the passage never mentions parliament favourably, only the judiciary acting. Fail.
4. **Test Option D**: "indifferent … cataloguing" — a neutral catalogue would not lead with *"increasingly asserted"* (a value-laden verb). Fail.
5. **Test Option B**: "critical and concerned about judicial overreach" — matches the cumulative, citation-heavy framing of an expanding power. **Correct.**

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students pick (A) because the passage *sounds* formal and cites cases — formal diction ≠ neutral tone. Always check whether the author evaluates or merely describes; here, *"increasingly asserted"* is the giveaway evaluative verb.

#### Standard Question Types in Order of Frequency

1. **Main idea / central theme** — usually the first or last item.
2. **Vocabulary-in-context** — "the word *X* most nearly means…".
3. **Inference** — "it can be inferred that the author would likely…".
4. **Tone / purpose** — "the author's attitude toward *X* is best described as…".
5. **Supporting detail** — explicit locator questions.
6. **Negative / EXCEPT** — "all of the following are mentioned EXCEPT…".

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Advanced Traps

| Trap | What it looks like | Counter-move |
|---|---|---|
| Pronoun antecedent swap | "The legislature… *it* ruled that…" where *it* actually refers to the court | Re-read the previous two sentences; mark every pronoun and its referent |
| Paraphrase word-substitution trap | Correct answer uses one synonym swapped from passage text | Compare meaning, not words; identical words in passage = wrong answer |
| Half-true inference | Option is partly supported but adds a claim the passage never makes | Delete any clause not in the passage; the residue must still hold |
| Main-idea scope shift | Option describes a paragraph, not the passage | Ask: does this cover *every* paragraph's contribution? |
| Tone–topic confusion | Option names a reform agenda rather than the author's attitude | Ask: is the option describing *what* or *how the author feels about what*? |
| Extreme language | "completely", "always", "without exception" | Substitute into the passage; if even one counter-example exists, the option fails |

#### Exam Strategy Specific to HEC LAT

- **Time budget**: with ~30 minutes for English and 10–12 RC items, spend ≤4 minutes per passage including reading time. If you exceed 5 minutes, mark and move.
- **First-pass reading**: read only the first and last sentence of each paragraph; this captures 70% of thesis statements in LAT passages.
- **Question stem first**: read the 4–6 stems *before* the second-pass read; the stems tell you which keywords to scan for.
- **Elimination order**: outside-knowledge → extreme language → scope mismatch → only then the paraphrase match. LAT distractors rarely fail on more than one of these axes.
- **Legal-context priming**: passages frequently cite the *Judges' Case* (1996, 1997, 2009), the *18th/19th/20th Amendments*, *suo motu* jurisdiction under Article 184(3), and the *Supreme Judicial Council*. A 30-second mental refresh of these before the exam recovers 20–30 seconds per legal passage.

#### Two Advanced Practice Prompts

1. **Prompt A — Inference discipline**: take any 200-word opinion piece from *Dawn* or *The News* editorial page. Write a single sentence paraphrasing the author's thesis, then list three inferences the author would *not* endorse, even if those inferences are factually true in real life.
2. **Prompt B — Tone under pressure**: locate a *Dawn* editorial on judicial activism and a *The News* op-ed on the same topic. Without reading both end-to-end, predict each author's likely tone (reformist, defensive, polemical, didactic) from headline and lede alone, then verify by full read.
