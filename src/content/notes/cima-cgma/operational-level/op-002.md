# P1 Management Accounting

P1 is one of nine Objective Tests in the CGMA Professional Qualification, alongside P1's siblings E1 and F1 at Operational Level and the six modules above it. Each Objective Test is 90 minutes, computer-based and computer-marked, available on demand throughout the year, and AICPA & CIMA states that the items "test all component learning outcomes across the entire subject" — a short multiple-choice question and a drag-and-drop item, drawing on every part of the syllabus rather than on the chapter you have just read.

That has one practical consequence: breadth pays. A candidate who has covered every part of P1 thinly will outscore one who has mastered two parts, because the exam samples across all of it.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> The night before: the mechanism behind every P1 calculation, plus the traps the format is built on.

**What it is.** P1 is one of nine Objective Tests in the CGMA Professional Qualification. Each is 90 minutes, computer-based, computer-marked, available on demand through the year, and AICPA & CIMA states the items "test all component learning outcomes across the entire subject".

**The cost behaviour graph in one line.** Within the relevant range, total fixed cost is constant and total variable cost moves with activity — so total cost rises at a decreasing rate while unit cost falls.

**Marginal vs absorption in one line.** The entire difference is fixed production overhead: absorption carries it forward in inventory and releases it as cost of sales later, so profit moves with production volume rather than with trading.

**Break-even and margin of safety.** Units = fixed cost ÷ contribution per unit, where contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost per unit. Margin of safety = actual or budgeted volume less break-even volume, as a percentage of actual volume — a risk measure, not a profit measure.

**Learn the units and write them down.** Mixing currency with currency is the most common arithmetic error in this topic, and it is entirely avoidable.

**The four variance effects, and what each one asks for.**

- **Materials price** — did we pay more or less per unit bought?
- **Materials usage** — did we need more or less per unit produced?
- **Labour efficiency** — did the hours match the standard hours?
- **Variable overhead efficiency and capacity** — do these exist at all? They only arise where an actual hour rate is given.

**The link that carries the higher marks.** A favourable materials price variance with an unfavourable usage variance usually means cheaper material was bought and more was needed — a purchasing decision with a production consequence. Finding that link beats computing four correct numbers.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> The working understanding — the material that carries most of the marks.

| Mechanism | One-line statement | Where it is examined |
|---|---|---|
| Cost behaviour | Fixed in total, variable per unit, within a relevant range | Several question forms |
| Step cost | Variable but not proportional — fixed within a shift, variable across shifts | Break-even, threshold data |

| Variance decomposition | Price/volume for materials and labour; efficiency/capacity for variable overheads | Four-finger analysis |

#### Cost behaviour, and why it is the first thing

A cost is fixed in total over a relevant range and variable per unit. Within that range, total fixed cost is constant and total variable cost moves with activity, so total cost rises at a decreasing rate while unit cost falls. That single graph is the foundation everything else sits on, and questions about it appear in several forms.

The classic trap is cost that is variable but not in proportion to activity — a stepped cost such as a machine changeover, or a supervisor paid per shift. It behaves as fixed within a shift and variable across shifts. If a question gives you a cost that jumps at a threshold, that is what it is, and treating it as smoothly variable produces the wrong break-even.

#### Marginal and absorption costing

Marginal costing includes variable costs in inventory and treats fixed production costs as a period cost. Absorption costing includes fixed production overhead in inventory.

The entire difference is fixed production overhead: under absorption it is carried forward in inventory and released as cost of sales later, so profit in any single period moves with production volume. Produce more than you sell and profit rises; produce less and profit falls — for reasons that have nothing to do with trading.

This is the most heavily examined idea in P1 and the one where a well-chosen explanation earns marks. Learn it as a mechanism rather than as a set of rules.

#### Break-even

Break-even is where contribution equals fixed costs: units = fixed cost divided by contribution per unit, where contribution per unit is selling price less variable cost per unit.

Margin of safety is actual or budgeted volume less break-even volume, expressed as a percentage of actual volume. Its meaning is how far sales can fall before the business loses money, which is a risk measure rather than a profit measure.

Learn the units and write them down. Mixing currency with currency is the most common arithmetic error in this topic and it is entirely avoidable.

#### Budgeting, variances and standard costing

A variance has a cause, and the mark is in identifying it correctly. Standard costing decomposes cost and revenue differences into price and volume effects for materials and labour, plus efficiency and capacity effects for variable overheads.

The four-finger analysis is worth memorising: a favourable materials price variance with an unfavourable usage variance usually means the cheaper material was bought and more was needed, and that is a purchasing decision with a production consequence. Finding that link is what earns the higher marks, rather than correctly computing four numbers.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (3mo+)
> Where the marks are won, and the boundary cases that separate a competent script from a top one.

#### Absorption and marginal side by side

| | Marginal costing | Absorption costing |
|---|---|---|
| Variable costs in inventory | Included | Included |
| Fixed production overhead in inventory | Excluded — a period cost | Included — carried forward |
| Fixed production overhead release | Immediate | Later, as cost of sales |
| Profit in a single period | Moves with **sales** volume | Moves with **production** volume |
| Over a full cycle | Same total profit | Same total profit |

The mechanism is the whole point: under absorption, fixed production overhead is deferred into inventory and released when that inventory sells. Produce more than you sell and reported profit rises even though nothing extra was traded; produce less and reported profit falls for the same reason. Saying absorption gives "a better" profit figure is wrong — it defers overhead, it does not create profit.

#### Why step costs break the break-even answer

A cost that is variable but not proportional to activity — a machine changeover, or a supervisor paid per shift — behaves as fixed within a shift and variable across shifts. If a cost in the question jumps at a threshold, that is a step cost, and treating it as smoothly variable gives the wrong break-even. The examiner's tell is a threshold figure in the data.

#### The four-finger variance analysis

Standard costing decomposes cost and revenue differences into price and volume effects for materials and labour, plus efficiency and capacity effects for variable overheads. Four correct numbers earn materially less than the same four numbers with a causal story attached.

| Observed pair | Usual reading | The question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Favourable materials price, unfavourable usage | Cheaper material bought, more needed | Was this a purchasing decision with a production consequence? |
| Unfavourable price, favourable usage | Expensive material bought, less wasted | Is the extra price buying a quality or speed gain? |
| Adverse revenue price, favourable volume | Discounting to hold volume | Is the volume gain worth the margin given? |
| Variable overhead efficiency **n/a** | — | The variance only exists where an actual hour rate is given |

That last row is the standing trap: variable overhead efficiency and capacity variances cannot be computed at all unless the question supplies an actual hour rate. A question that omits it is testing whether you will invent one.

#### Worked break-even with the units kept visible

Fixed cost £120,000 per period, selling price £40 per unit, variable cost £25 per unit. Contribution per unit = 40 − 25 = £15 per unit. Break-even = 120,000 ÷ 15 = 8,000 units. Margin of safety at a budgeted volume of 10,000 units = (10,000 − 8,000) ÷ 10,000 = 20% of volume. Every figure carries its unit; "fixed cost divided by contribution" is only meaningful as a number of units.

#### The scope trap the format creates

Because items "test all component learning outcomes across the entire subject", a question can reach across sections — cost behaviour into break-even, absorption into variance analysis. Answer the question that was asked. A revenue variance caused by price and one caused by volume are different answers, and picking whichever is easier to compute is how a correct-technique answer still scores nothing.

#### Common mistakes

Saying absorption costing gives "a better" profit figure. It defers fixed production overhead; it does not create profit, and it makes profit depend on production volume.

Treating a stepped cost as smoothly variable in the break-even calculation.

Omitting the unit from a break-even answer. "Fixed cost divided by contribution" is only meaningful as a number of units.

Analysing variances without linking them. Four correct numbers and no explanation scores materially less than the same four with the purchasing-versus-usage story.

Confusing a revenue variance caused by price with one caused by volume, and answering with whichever the question did not ask for.

Forgetting that variable overhead efficiency and capacity variances only arise where an actual hour rate exists, which is a standard examiner trap in a question that omits it.
