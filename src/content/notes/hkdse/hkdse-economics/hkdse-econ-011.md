# Elective 1 — Monopoly Pricing, Anti-competitive Behaviours and Competition Policy — HKDSE Economics Notes

This topic covers Elective 1 of the HKDSE Economics Elective Part. Candidates choose either Elective 1 or Elective 2. The topic addresses sources of monopoly power; price discrimination (first, second and third degree); predatory pricing and limit pricing; mergers and anti-competitive agreements; the Hong Kong Competition Ordinance and the role of the Competition Commission.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Sources of monopoly power**: barriers to entry (economies of scale, brand loyalty, control of essential inputs, patents, network effects, strategic behaviour).
- **Price discrimination**: charging different prices to different customers for the same good. Conditions: market power, ability to identify groups, ability to prevent resale.
- **First-degree price discrimination (perfect)**: each consumer pays their maximum willingness to pay. Captures all consumer surplus.
- **Second-degree price discrimination**: discounts for bulk purchase or other self-selection. Examples: quantity discounts, loyalty programmes.
- **Third-degree price discrimination**: different prices for different groups. Examples: student discounts, senior discounts, regional pricing.
- **Predatory pricing**: setting a very low price (below cost) to drive rivals out of the market, then raising prices once the rival is gone.
- **Limit pricing**: setting a price just below the level at which new entrants could profit, to deter entry.
- **Mergers**: horizontal (between competitors), vertical (between suppliers and customers), conglomerate (unrelated businesses).
- **Anti-competitive agreements**: price-fixing cartels, market-sharing agreements, bid-rigging.
- **Hong Kong Competition Ordinance (2012)**: prohibits anti-competitive agreements, abuse of market power, and mergers that substantially lessen competition. Excludes certain sectors (e.g. telecommunications under separate regulation).
- **Competition Commission (Hong Kong)**: the statutory body responsible for enforcing the Competition Ordinance.
- **Competition Tribunal**: hears cases brought by the Competition Commission.

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing predatory pricing with limit pricing — predatory is to drive out; limit is to deter entry.
- Treating all mergers as anti-competitive — some mergers are efficiency-enhancing.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Price discrimination

Conditions for price discrimination:

1. **Market power**: the firm has some control over price (monopoly or imperfect competition).
2. **Ability to identify groups**: the firm can distinguish consumers with different elasticities of demand.
3. **Ability to prevent resale**: no arbitrage between groups.

Types:

- **First-degree (perfect)**: the firm charges each consumer their maximum willingness to pay. Captures all consumer surplus. Rare in practice (auctions are an example).
- **Second-degree**: the firm offers a menu of options (e.g. quantity discounts, versioned products), and consumers self-select. Examples: airline tickets (multiple fare classes); software (free vs paid versions).
- **Third-degree**: the firm charges different prices to different identifiable groups. Examples: student discounts, regional pricing, peak/off-peak pricing.

Conditions for profit-maximising price discrimination: MR₁ = MR₂ = MC across all groups.

Effects: price discrimination can increase total surplus (compared to uniform pricing) because it expands output. But it transfers surplus from consumers to producers.

#### Predatory pricing and limit pricing

**Predatory pricing**: a firm sets a price below average variable cost (or even below average total cost) to drive rivals out of the market. Once the rival exits, the firm raises prices to monopoly levels. The predator bears short-run losses for long-run monopoly profit.

**The Areeda-Turner rule**: prices below average variable cost are presumptively predatory; prices above AVC but below ATC may be competitive or may be predatory depending on intent.

**Limit pricing**: a firm sets a price just below the level at which new entrants could profit (the limit price). The incumbent earns less than monopoly profit but deters entry. Limit pricing requires barriers to entry.

#### Mergers

Types:

- **Horizontal**: between competitors in the same market (e.g. two airlines merging).
- **Vertical**: between firms at different stages of production (e.g. a manufacturer merging with a supplier).
- **Conglomerate**: between firms in unrelated businesses (e.g. a tobacco company merging with a food company).

**Effects of horizontal mergers**:

- **Pro-competitive**: efficiencies (economies of scale, lower costs), increased R&D investment.
- **Anti-competitive**: increased market power, higher prices, less output.

Competition authorities review mergers to assess whether they substantially lessen competition.

#### Anti-competitive agreements

Examples:

- **Price-fixing cartels**: competitors agree on prices.
- **Market-sharing agreements**: competitors divide markets.
- **Bid-rigging**: competitors agree on who will win a contract.

In Hong Kong, the Competition Ordinance prohibits these agreements. The Competition Commission investigates and prosecutes.

#### Hong Kong Competition Ordinance

Key features:

- **Prohibitions**: anti-competitive agreements (the First Conduct Rule), abuse of market power (the Second Conduct Rule), mergers that substantially lessen competition (the Merger Rule).
- **Exclusions**: certain sectors (telecommunications under separate regulation), statutory bodies, services of general economic interest, public policy.
- **Enforcement**: the Competition Commission investigates; the Competition Tribunal adjudicates; penalties include fines, behavioural remedies, and structural remedies.

#### Penalties and remedies

- **Fines**: up to 10% of annual Hong Kong turnover in Hong Kong for each year of violation (capped).
- **Behavioural remedies**: cease and desist orders, mandatory compliance programmes.
- **Structural remedies**: divestiture (selling part of the business).

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Cross-topic links

- **D. Competition and Market Structure**: monopoly, oligopoly, collusion.
- **E. Efficiency, Equity and the Role of Government**: market failure and regulation.
- **I. Macroeconomic Problems and Policies**: regulation of financial institutions.

#### Exam technique

- **For price discrimination questions**: identify the type; state the conditions; discuss the welfare effects.
- **For predatory pricing questions**: identify the test (Areeda-Turner); discuss the welfare effects.
- **For competition policy questions**: identify the rule violated; cite the Hong Kong Competition Ordinance.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Predatory pricing is illegal everywhere." The Areeda-Turner test (price below AVC) is presumptive; above AVC, intent and recoupment must be shown.
- "All price discrimination is unfair." Some price discrimination expands output and can be Pareto-improving compared to uniform pricing.
- "Competition policy is always beneficial." Competition policy can prevent efficient mergers and may have unintended consequences.
