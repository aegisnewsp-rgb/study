# Cell Injury and Adaptation

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your FMGE exam.

**Cell injury** is the sequence of cellular events triggered when stress exceeds adaptive capacity, progressing from **reversible injury** (hydropic or fatty change) to **irreversible injury** ending in **necrosis** or **apoptosis**. The most common cause is **hypoxia** (typically from ischemia), which collapses ATP production.

Key high-yield facts for FMGE MCQs:

- **Reversible injury** = cellular swelling + fatty change; **irreversible injury** = mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening + severe plasma-membrane damage.
- **Coagulative necrosis** is the default pattern in solid-organ infarcts (heart, kidney, spleen) except the brain; **liquefactive necrosis** dominates CNS infarcts and bacterial abscesses.
- **Apoptosis** is energy-dependent, programmed, and non-inflammatory; **necrosis** is uncontrolled, membrane-rupturing, and triggers inflammation.
- The four adaptations are **hypertrophy** (size↑), **hyperplasia** (number↑), **atrophy** (size↓), and **metaplasia** (one mature cell type replaced by another, e.g., Barrett's esophagus).
- **Dystrophic calcification** deposits on damaged tissue with normal serum Ca²⁺; **metastatic calcification** deposits in normal tissue due to hypercalcemia.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for working understanding before FMGE.

#### Hypoxic Injury Mechanism
Reduced oxygen delivery fails oxidative phosphorylation, so ATP falls within minutes. The **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase** stalls, intracellular sodium and water accumulate, and the cell swells (**hydropic change**). Simultaneously, cytosolic **Ca²⁺ rises**, activating phospholipases, proteases, endonucleases, and opening the **mitochondrial permeability transition pore (MPTP)** — the defining biochemical threshold of irreversibility.

#### Reversible vs Irreversible Morphology
Early changes are reversible: cellular swelling, ribosomal detachment, plasma-membrane blebbing, and organelle dilation. The cell crosses into irreversibility when **mitochondrial dysfunction becomes permanent** and the plasma membrane cannot be resealed — at which point the cell heads toward necrosis or apoptosis.

#### Necrosis — Patterns and Organ Examples

| Pattern | Morphology | Classic Site |
|---|---|---|
| Coagulative | Preserved cell outline, denatured proteins | Heart, kidney, spleen infarcts |
| Liquefactive | Enzymatic dissolution → pus-like | Brain infarct, bacterial abscess |
| Caseous | Cheese-like, structureless | Tuberculous granuloma |
| Fat | Chalky-white saponification | Pancreatitis, breast trauma |
| Fibrinoid | Smudgy eosinophilic vessel wall | Immune vasculitis |
| Gangrenous | Coagulative ± liquefactive | Limb ischemia (wet vs dry) |

#### Adaptations Compared
- **Hypertrophy**: ↑ cell size in permanent cells (e.g., left ventricular myocytes in hypertension, skeletal muscle with exercise).
- **Hyperplasia**: ↑ cell number in labile/stable cells — physiologic (breast in pregnancy) or pathologic (BPH, goiter).
- **Atrophy**: ↓ cell size from disuse, denervation, ischemia, or malnutrition — mediated by **autophagy** and the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway.
- **Metaplasia**: reversible substitution of one differentiated epithelium by another; squamous metaplasia in smokers' bronchi and intestinal metaplasia (Barrett's) at the GE junction are the FMGE classics.

> **Trap.** Barrett's esophagus is a *pre-neoplastic* metaplasia (intestinal type → adenocarcinoma), not merely a benign adaptive change.

#### Apoptosis Pathways
- **Intrinsic (mitochondrial)** pathway: cellular stress → **Bax/Bak** oligomerize → cytochrome c release → **caspase-9** → executioner **caspases 3, 6, 7**. **Bcl-2** and **Bcl-xL** block it.
- **Extrinsic (death-receptor)** pathway: **Fas-FasL** binding recruits **FADD**, activating **caspase-8**, which converges on the same executioner caspases.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage integrating mechanisms, edge cases, and exam strategy.

#### Free-Radical Injury
Reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS/RNS) damage lipids (peroxidation of membrane phospholipids), proteins (cross-linking, misfolding), and DNA (strand breaks). Endogenous defenses are **superoxide dismutase (SOD)**, **glutathione peroxidase**, **catalase**, and dietary antioxidants **vitamin E** (lipid phase) and **vitamin C** (aqueous phase). Ischemia–reperfusion injury classically exemplifies free-radical damage: xanthine dehydrogenase is converted to **xanthine oxidase** during ischemia, and on reperfusion it generates a burst of superoxide.

#### Intracellular Accumulations
- **Lipids**: steatosis (fatty liver), cholesterol clefts in atheromas.
- **Proteins**: **Mallory bodies** (intermediate filament aggregates in alcoholic hepatitis), **Russell bodies** (Ig-laden plasma cells in chronic inflammation), **hyaline change** in arterioles.
- **Glycogen**: nuclear glycogen vacuolation in diabetes.
- **Pigments**: **lipofuscin** = brown "wear-and-tear" pigment of aging/atrophy; **hemosiderin** = iron-storage pigment from RBC breakdown (Prussian blue positive); **melanin**, **bilirubin**, **anthracosis** (coal dust in lung), tattoo ink.

#### Pathologic Calcification
- **Dystrophic**: normal serum Ca²⁺; calcium deposits on necrotic/damaged tissue — atherosclerotic plaques, TB granulomas, damaged heart valves.
- **Metastatic**: hypercalcemia (hyperparathyroidism, vitamin D toxicity, immobilization, bone destruction from metastases or multiple myeloma) deposits calcium in normal lung, kidney, gastric mucosa, and systemic arteries.

#### Subcellular and Nuclear Changes
Irreversibly injured cells show mitochondrial swelling with amorphous matrix densities, ER dilation, ribosome detachment, and nuclear changes progressing through **pyknosis → karyorrhexis → karyolysis** in necrotic cells. **Councilman bodies** (eosinophilic apoptotic hepatocytes in viral hepatitis) are a recurring FMGE image question.

#### High-Yield Pitfalls
- **Coagulative necrosis** in infarcts becomes **fibrotic scar**; **brain infarct** liquefies and leaves a **fluid-filled cyst** — that single organ exception explains most wrong answers.
- **Apoptosis** involves cell shrinkage and apoptotic bodies phagocytosed without inflammation; necrosis always involves enzyme leakage and an inflammatory infiltrate.
- **Cytosolic Ca²⁺ INCREASE**, not decrease, drives irreversible injury.
- Lipofuscin is **not** the same as hemosiderin — the first is aging pigment, the second is iron-storage.

#### Practice Prompts
1. A 55-year-old chronic smoker has a bronchial biopsy showing stratified squamous epithelium replacing the normal pseudostratified ciliated columnar lining. Name the adaptation, identify a precursor lesion risk, and explain why ciliary function is lost.
2. A renal infarct shows preserved tissue architecture with "ghost outlines" of tubules and denatured eosinophilic cytoplasm on H&E. Identify the necrosis pattern, justify why the brain would look different, and list three enzymes whose cytosolic activation makes the change irreversible.
