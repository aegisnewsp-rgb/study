# Geometry and Trigonometry — Leaving Certificate (Established) Mathematics Notes

Geometry and Trigonometry is the strand most likely to surface as a long, multi-part extended-response question at the end of either Higher-level paper. At weight 4 in the catalogue, it sits with Statistics and Probability in the second tier of strand weight, and it carries distinctive marks because of the synthetic-geometry chain proofs that examiners set at Higher level. At Ordinary level the strand settles on the sine rule, the cosine rule, the geometry of the circle and basic coordinate geometry of the line. At Higher level it adds the geometry of the circle in detail, transformations, constructions and the 2D/3D trigonometry questions that routinely begin "ABC is a triangle with..." and run across two pages of paper.

What separates a H3 from a H6 in this strand is rarely the procedural computation — both candidates can apply the cosine rule correctly. The gap is in the *synthetic* (non-coordinate) geometry chain: a proof that an angle is 90°, a proof that two triangles are similar, or a construction that drops a perpendicular from a given point to a given line. Candidates who treat synthetic geometry as a separate, optional topic routinely forfeit a third of the available marks.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Strand weight & spread**: Weight 4/5 in the catalogue. Marks are dispersed across both papers at Ordinary and Higher level, with the largest single block at the back of Higher Paper 1 or 2 — confirm the live placement on examinations.ie each year.
- **Levels that include each sub-topic**: Coordinate geometry of the line, Pythagoras and the basic sine/cosine rules appear at all three levels. Synthetic geometry proofs, the geometry of the circle in detail, transformations and 3D trigonometry are Higher level only.
- **First move on any trigonometry question**: Draw the triangle. Even when the question provides a diagram, redraw it with the given values labelled — candidates who skip this lose the half-mark for setting up the working.
- **Right triangle vs general triangle**: If the triangle has a right angle, use Pythagoras and SOHCAHTOA. If not, use the sine rule or the cosine rule. Examiners will not award marks for the sine rule applied to a right-angled triangle when Pythagoras was the intended route.
- **Sine rule**: a/sin A = b/sin B = c/sin C. Use when you know an angle and its opposite side plus one other piece of information. Two ambiguous-case (SSA) outcomes are possible — the examiner may ask the candidate to identify which case applies.
- **Cosine rule**: a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A. Use when you know all three sides, or two sides and the included angle. It collapses to Pythagoras when A = 90°.
- **Area of triangle**: (1/2)ab sin C. Use this rather than (1/2) × base × height when only two sides and the included angle are known.
- **Coordinate geometry of the line**: slope-intercept form y = mx + c; distance formula; midpoint formula; the equation of a line through two points. These four lines cover roughly 60% of the coordinate-geometry marks at Ordinary level.
- **Geometry of the circle (Higher)**: angle at centre is twice angle at circumference; angle in a semicircle is 90°; opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral sum to 180°; tangent is perpendicular to radius at the point of contact. Each is a one-line mark in a synthetic proof.

#### Examiner traps

- Quoting "the angle in a semicircle is 90°" without identifying the diameter as the chord. The diameter is the chord; the proof requires the candidate to name it.
- Using the cosine rule to find a side when the sine rule would have given two valid triangles and the examiner wants both.
- Computing the gradient of a line as rise over run in the wrong direction — slope = (y2 − y1) / (x2 − x1) is the only consistent form.
- Solving a 3D Pythagoras question by squaring all three axes and adding — confirm which two edges form the right angle before treating the third as the hypotenuse.
- Treating a transformation question as a "drawing exercise" rather than a "state the matrix or describe the transformation" exercise. The mark is for naming the transformation, not for the diagram.

#### 30-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Can you write the sine rule, the cosine rule and the area formula from memory, with the variables labelled on a sketch?
- [ ] Can you name each of the four standard circle theorems and the condition that triggers each?
- [ ] Can you derive the equation of a line from two points in under 60 seconds?
- [ ] Can you identify whether a 3D problem is a Pythagoras-in-3D, a cosine-rule-in-3D, or a vector question before reaching for the formula sheet?

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### Coordinate geometry of the line and the circle

Coordinate geometry questions at Leaving Certificate level follow a recognisable sequence. (1) Find the equation of a line through two given points. (2) Find the slope and intercept. (3) Find where two lines intersect. (4) Use the distance formula or midpoint formula to answer a sub-question. The procedural route is straightforward; the higher-order thinking is in deciding *which* of these four operations the question is asking for. A Higher-level extension places the line in the context of a circle: find the equation of a tangent to a circle from an external point, or show that a given line is tangent by computing its distance from the centre and comparing to the radius.

The geometry of the circle at Higher level involves three formulas: the equation of a circle in standard form (x − a)² + (y − b)² = r²; the general form x² + y² + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0; and the tangent line formula xx₁ + yy₁ + g(x + x₁) + f(y + y₁) + c = 0. The procedural skill is to recognise which form the question has given and convert it accordingly. Many candidates lose marks by writing the standard form when the general form was expected, or vice versa.

#### Synthetic geometry at Higher level

Synthetic geometry at Leaving Certificate Higher level is taught as a chain of theorems: angle in semicircle, angle at centre, alternate segment theorem, cyclic quadrilateral properties, similar triangles, congruent triangles. The exam's favoured question type is "Prove that angle ABC = 90°" — the candidate must cite two or three theorems in a logical order, with each citation justified by the diagram. Marks are awarded per cited theorem, not per step in the arithmetic.

The revision strategy is to learn each theorem with its *trigger condition* (what you must see in the diagram to invoke it). For example: angle in semicircle requires *a chord that is a diameter*; alternate segment theorem requires *a tangent and a chord meeting at a point on the circle*; cyclic quadrilateral requires *all four vertices on the circle*. Without the trigger, the theorem is just a phrase; with it, the candidate can construct a proof from a blank page.

#### Trigonometry in 2D and 3D

Trigonometry questions split into two families. The 2D family uses the sine rule, the cosine rule and the area formula within a single plane. The 3D family (Higher only) places the same triangle inside a rectangular prism or a pyramid, and the candidate must first identify a plane within the 3D shape, then solve the 2D triangle in that plane. The procedural skill is the same; the navigation skill is the new requirement.

A typical Higher-level 3D question describes a rectangular box ABCDEFGH and asks for the angle between two diagonals, the angle between a diagonal and the base, or the length of a diagonal. The candidate must (a) sketch the 3D shape, (b) identify the triangle in question, (c) draw that triangle separately as a 2D figure, (d) apply Pythagoras or the cosine rule as appropriate. The mark scheme gives marks for each of these four steps; candidates who skip the separate 2D sketch lose the diagram mark.

#### Transformations and constructions (Higher)

Transformations at Higher level include translations, reflections, rotations, enlargements and the composition of two transformations. Each can be described geometrically or matrix-style; the matrix form is the more concise but requires the candidate to remember the matrices for each transformation. The composition of two transformations is assessed by giving a composite matrix and asking the candidate to identify the two component transformations — the procedural skill is to factor a 2×2 matrix into a product of a known transformation and another.

Constructions appear at the end of Higher Paper 1 or 2, typically worth a small number of marks but disproportionately easy to lose. Each construction has a defined sequence of compass-and-straightedge steps; the mark scheme gives a mark for each step. Candidates who attempt the construction by eye forfeit every step mark.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Full Mastery (1mo+)

#### Higher-level synthetic geometry chains

The hardest synthetic geometry questions at Higher level chain three or four theorems together to prove a non-trivial result. The pattern: "Prove that triangle ABC is isosceles" or "Prove that the line PQ is perpendicular to RS". Candidates must (a) identify all the points and lines involved, (b) list every theorem whose trigger condition is satisfied, (c) chain the theorems so that the conclusion follows. A useful technique is to write the conclusion at the bottom of the page, then write each cited theorem above it, working backwards until a cited theorem's premise matches a given fact.

Mark-scheme audits show that the most common Higher-level synthetic geometry error is *over-citing* theorems the diagram does not support. "Angle ABC equals angle DEF by alternate angles" loses marks when the angles are not alternate — the examiner's note will record this as "incorrect justification". Candidates must check each citation against the trigger condition before writing it.

#### 3D trigonometry at the highest level

At the highest level, 3D trigonometry combines vector methods with classical trigonometry. A typical extended-response question: "ABCDE is a pyramid with a square base ABCD of side 6 cm and apex E directly above the centre of the base. The slant edge EA has length 10 cm. Find (a) the height of the pyramid, (b) the angle between the slant edge EA and the base, (c) the angle between two adjacent triangular faces, (d) the volume of the pyramid." Each part requires the candidate to identify a different triangle in the 3D structure.

The procedural skill is the same as the standard 3D trigonometry; the navigation skill is to recognise that part (c) requires the angle between two *planes*, which requires the candidate to find a line perpendicular to both planes — typically the line through the apex and the midpoint of an edge of the base. Candidates who attempt (c) by measuring angles directly from the 3D sketch forfeit the mark.

#### Common mistakes catalogue

1. **Sine rule ambiguous case**: given SSA, two triangles are possible. The Leaving Certificate rarely asks the candidate to choose between them; it usually asks for *one* valid answer, then offers a follow-up about which case applies.
2. **Cyclic quadrilateral direction**: opposite angles sum to 180° only when all four vertices are on the circle. A common error is to apply the theorem to a quadrilateral that *looks* cyclic but isn't.
3. **Cosine rule sign**: −2bc cos A — the negative sign is easy to drop, particularly when the angle is obtuse and cos A is negative, making the entire term positive.
4. **3D Pythagoras identification**: applying Pythagoras to three edges that do not meet at a common vertex. The theorem requires a right angle at one vertex; the candidate must check this before computing.
5. **Transformation matrix composition**: writing AB instead of BA when the question asks for transformation A followed by B. Matrix multiplication is not commutative — the order matters.

#### Long-run mastery schedule

A four-week pre-mock plan for Geometry and Trigonometry at Higher level:

- **Week 1**: Drill the four standard circle theorems and the sine/cosine/area rules. Time yourself — a Higher-level candidate should be able to write each in under 30 seconds from memory.
- **Week 2**: Work every past paper's synthetic geometry question. Draw the diagram, label every angle and side, write the chain of theorems. Mark yourself against the published marking scheme, not against your own judgement.
- **Week 3**: Drill 3D trigonometry. For each Higher-level past paper, identify which 3D shape is involved, sketch the 2D triangle inside it, then solve. Time each question to 8 minutes — the live paper allocation is tight.
- **Week 4**: Mock-exam week. Solve three past papers under timed conditions; mark against the marking scheme; write the "errors I keep making" list; re-drill those errors. Confirm the live paper pattern and the construction question placement on examinations.ie before final revision.
