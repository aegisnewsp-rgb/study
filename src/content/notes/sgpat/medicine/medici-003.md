# Pathophysiology

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> The single thing to lock in before the exam: pathophysiology asks *why* the patient looks the way they do, linking molecular events to bedside findings.

- **Etiology** names the cause (smoke, *H. pylori*, gene mutation). **Pathogenesis** names the mechanism chain from cause to clinical sign.
- Cellular responses to stress form a five-word ladder: **Atrophy, Hypertrophy, Hyperplasia, Metaplasia, Dysplasia** — reversible until dysplasia crosses into neoplasia.
- Two deaths to keep apart: **apoptosis** (energy-requiring, silent, no inflammation) versus **necrosis** (membrane rupture, ATP-independent, inflammatory).
- Quick pressure math: **MAP = DBP + ⅓(SBP − DBP)**, units mmHg; normal ≈ 70–100.
- Filtration across a capillary: **Jv = Lp · S · [(Pc − Pi) − σ(πc − πi)]**, with Pc, Pi, πc, πi all in pascals and σ dimensionless.
- Acid-base pocket rule: **pH = 6.1 + log([HCO₃⁻] / [0.03 · PCO₂])**; a raised **anion gap** (AG = Na⁺ + K⁺ − Cl⁻ − HCO₃⁻, normal 8–16 mmol/L) flags an organic-acid acidosis (DKA, lactic, uraemic, toxic).

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"ATP on = apoptosis quiet; ATP out = necrosis loud."** A cell with mitochondria still working shrinks and gets eaten quietly; a cell that loses ATP swells, lyses, and calls in neutrophils. Pair it with the shock mnemonic **"Cold & Clammy = low CO (hypovolaemic, cardiogenic, obstructive); Warm & Dry = low SVR (distributive)."**

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Distinguishing the words examiners test
Saudi GP Board stems will often hinge on a single misused term. The table below is the most-tested contrast pair:

| Term | What it really is | Exam trap |
|------|-------------------|-----------|
| Etiology | The *cause* of disease (agent, gene, behaviour) | Students write a mechanism instead of a cause |
| Pathogenesis | The *sequence* of cellular and molecular events from cause to lesion | Students write a cause and call it pathogenesis |
| Adaptation | Reversible cell change preserving function | Confused with irreversible injury |
| Apoptosis | Programmed, ATP-dependent, caspase-mediated | Confused with necrosis |
| Necrosis | Uncontrolled, membrane-failure cell death | Confused with apoptosis |
| Hyperplasia | **More cells** (only in replicating tissues) | Confused with hypertrophy |
| Hypertrophy | **Bigger cells** (cardiomyocytes, skeletal muscle) | Confused with hyperplasia |
| Metaplasia | Reversible swap of one mature cell type for another | Confused with dysplasia |
| Dysplasia | Disorderly growth, often pre-malignant | Confused with metaplasia |

#### Acid-base: the two-line method
A pH outside 7.35–7.45 names the primary disturbance; the direction of the *other* limb (PCO₂ or HCO₃⁻) tells you whether compensation is appropriate. Use the **Henderson-Hasselbalch** equation in steady states only — during an acute acid load the equation still gives you the right trend, but the in-vivo buffering by intracellular proteins and haemoglobin makes the numerical pH predictively soft.

Always **correct the anion gap for hypoalbuminaemia**: corrected AG = measured AG + 2.5 × (4 − serum albumin g/dL). A "normal" gap in a sick, hypoalbuminaemic patient may still hide an organic acidosis.

#### Starling forces and oedema
Net fluid movement across a capillary is governed by **four pressures**, two hydrostatic (Pc, Pi) and two oncotic (πc, πi). The net direction flips in predictable clinical scenarios:

| Clinical state | Δ Jv direction | Dominant force change |
|----------------|----------------|----------------------|
| Heart failure oedema | More filtration out | ↑ Pc (venous congestion) |
| Nephrotic oedema | Less reabsorption in | ↓ πc (protein loss) |
| Hepatic ascites | Less reabsorption in | ↓ πc (loss of synthesis) |
| Lymphoedema | Less clearance | Lymphatic obstruction |
| Inflammation | More filtration out | ↑ Pc + ↑ πi (leaky membrane) |

#### 🩺 Clinical reasoning prompt
A 54-year-old with dyspnoea, bibasilar crackles, JVP elevation, and pitting oedema to the mid-shin: identify which Starling term has shifted upward (Pc, via venous congestion) and which compensatory system (RAAS, ADH) is making the oedema worse by retaining sodium and water. Linking the symptom to the pressure change — not just naming "heart failure" — is the answer the GP Board rewards.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Candidates who quote MAP as "the diastolic" or "the systolic" lose the mark immediately. Use **MAP = DBP + ⅓(SBP − DBP)**, then use it to interpret cerebral and coronary perfusion pressure.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Shock states — when physiology meets the bedside
| Type | CO | SVR | PCWP | Classic skin | Example |
|------|----|----|------|---------------|---------|
| Hypovolaemic | ↓ | ↑ | ↓ | Cool, clammy | Haemorrhage, burns |
| Cardiogenic | ↓ | ↑ | ↑ | Cool, clammy | Acute MI, tamponade physiology |
| Obstructive | ↓ | ↑ | ↑ or variable | Cool, clammy | Tension pneumothorax, massive PE |
| Distributive | ↑ or normal | ↓ | ↓ or normal | Warm, vasodilated | Sepsis, anaphylaxis, neurogenic |

Compensation travels through three predictable lines: **baroreceptor reflex** (seconds), **RAAS + ADH** (minutes to hours), and **tissue ischaemia → lactate** (late). When MAP falls below the organ's autoregulatory threshold — roughly 60 mmHg for kidneys and brain — symptoms and lab signs (oliguria, confusion, lactataemia) emerge.

#### Cell-injury pathway in one sentence
#### ATP depletion → Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase failure → cell swelling → Ca²⁺ influx → mitochondrial damage → free-radical release → membrane phospholipid loss → either apoptosis (if ATP still partial) or necrosis (if ATP gone).

#### Inflammation — acute versus chronic

- **Acute**: vascular dilation, increased permeability, neutrophil emigration; mediators include histamine, prostaglandins, leukotrienes, IL-1, TNF-α; cardinal signs = rubor, calor, tumour, dolor, functio laesa.
- **Chronic**: mononuclear infiltrate (macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells), tissue destruction, attempted repair by angiogenesis and fibrosis; granuloma formation when the inciting agent cannot be cleared (TB, sarcoid, foreign body).

#### Two advanced practice prompts
1. A 67-year-old with CKD stage 4 (eGFR 22) presents with Kussmaul respirations and plasma: Na⁺ 138, K⁺ 5.4, Cl⁻ 100, HCO₃⁻ 12 mmol/L. Calculate the **anion gap**, identify the **primary disturbance**, predict the **expected PCO₂** (Winter's formula: PCO₂ = 1.5 × HCO₃⁻ + 8 ± 2), and explain why his serum potassium is elevated despite total-body depletion.
2. A 45-year-old develops warm, vasodilated extremities, MAP 58 mmHg, lactate 4.8 mmol/L, and a likely intra-abdominal source. Classify the shock, name the dominant mediator driving the haemodynamic picture, and justify why an early fluid bolus improves preload *and* why a vasopressor must follow once SVR is restored.

#### Edge cases worth flagging
- **Ischaemia–reperfusion injury**: paradoxical worsening when blood flow returns; mechanism is ROS burst on re-oxygenation plus calcium overload — explains why a successful angioplasty still kills myocardium at the border zone.
- **Apoptosis in the absence of injury**: physiological remodelling (thymic involution, endometrial shedding, digit webbing in embryogenesis) — same caspase machinery, no disease.
- **Dysplasia is reversible**: it is not yet carcinoma *in situ*; the cell atypia can regress if the stimulus (HPV, chronic irritation) is removed.
- **Metaplasia has a price**: Barrett's oesophagus swaps squamous for columnar epithelium to resist reflux acid, but the new epithelium carries a higher adenocarcinoma risk.

> 📌 **Formula Check:** Henderson-Hasselbalch uses **0.03** as the solubility coefficient of CO₂ in plasma (mmol·L⁻¹·mmHg⁻¹). Don't round it to 0.03 ± anything — the constant is fixed.
