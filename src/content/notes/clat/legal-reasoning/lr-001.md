# Legal Maxims

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Legal maxims are short Latin (or occasionally English) phrases that pack a complete rule of law into a few words. CLAT tests them through three question formats: direct meaning recall, identification in a fact pattern, and application of the legal consequence.

- **Mens Rea** = guilty mind; **Actus Reus** = guilty act. Both are required for most IPC offences (Section 6 IPC).
- **Audi Alteram Partem** = hear the other side; pairs with **Nemo Judex In Causa Sua** (no one should be judge in their own cause). Together they form the two pillars of natural justice.
- **Caveat Emptor** = let the buyer beware; substantially diluted by the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 and Section 14 of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930.
- **Volenti Non Fit Injuria** = to a willing person, no injury is done; applies only where consent to risk is free and with full knowledge.

Memorise the Latin, its translation, and the exact statutory anchor — that triad is what the CLAT MCQ distractor options attack.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Categories of Maxims Tested in CLAT

Maxims in the Legal Reasoning section fall into four functional buckets. Identifying the bucket is the fastest way to eliminate wrong options.

| Category | Function in Law | Example Maxims |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Substantive criminal law | Define fault elements | Mens Rea, Actus Reus, Ignorantia Juris Non Excusat |
| Contract interpretation | Resolve ambiguity in agreements | Expressio Unius Est Exclusio Alterius, Noscitur a Sociis, Ejusdem Generis, Pacta Sunt Servanda |
| Evidence and procedure | Govern proof and fair hearing | Res Ipsa Loquitur, Audi Alteram Partem, Nemo Judex In Causa Sua, Habeas Corpus |
| Tort and consumer protection | Allocate risk between parties | Volenti Non Fit Injuria, Caveat Emptor, Res Ipsa Loquitur, Ubi Jus Ibi Remedium |

#### How CLAT Frames Application Questions

A passage describes a fact pattern, then asks: "Which maxim applies?" The skill tested is mapping, not translation.

- A buyer purchases a car without inspection and later sues for a manufacturing defect → **Caveat Emptor** is the traditional answer, but the better answer in a 2019-onwards statute is the implied condition under Section 14, Sale of Goods Act.
- A factory smoke injures a neighbouring resident, and the only explanation is the factory's machinery → **Res Ipsa Loquitur** lets the court infer negligence from the event itself.
- A person knowingly joins a brawl and is injured → **Volenti Non Fit Injuria** defeats the tort claim only if consent to risk was free and informed.

#### Statutory Anchors to Remember

| Maxim | Indian Statute | Provision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mens Rea | Indian Penal Code, 1860 | Section 6 (definition of "wrongful gain/loss") |
| Free consent | Indian Contract Act, 1872 | Section 10 (what constitutes a valid agreement) |
| Implied conditions in sale | Sale of Goods Act, 1930 | Section 14 |
| Consumer protection override | Consumer Protection Act, 2019 | Sections 2(9), 2(47) — "defect" and "deficiency" |

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### High-Frequency Distractors in Past Papers

CLAT setters build options around three classic confusions. Pre-empt them and the answer falls out.

1. **Mens Rea vs Actus Reus** — if the fact pattern names only a thought ("X planned to rob a bank"), the correct maxim is Mens Rea; if only the conduct is described without intent, the answer is Actus Reus.
2. **Audi Alteram Partem vs Nemo Judex In Causa Sua** — the first is the *hearing* rule (cross-examination, right to be heard), the second is the *bias* rule (the decision-maker must be impartial). They are not interchangeable.
3. **Volenti Non Fit Injuria vs Consent in Criminal Law** — Volenti operates in tort to defeat civil claims; in criminal law, consent is a defence only in specific offences (Sections 88–92 IPC). Selecting Volenti for a criminal assault is a frequent wrong answer.

#### Worked Example

> Fact: A surgeon operates on a patient after a written acknowledgement that the procedure carries a 10% risk of nerve damage. The damage occurs. The patient sues.

**Correct maxim**: *Volenti Non Fit Injuria* — the patient, with knowledge of the risk, freely consented. The suit fails unless the surgeon exceeded the scope of consent or acted negligently beyond the disclosed risk. Distractor options in CLAT typically include *Res Ipsa Loquitur* (wrong — implies negligence, not consent) and *Caveat Emptor* (wrong — applies to sales of goods, not medical services).

#### Practice Prompts

1. Translate *Ignorantia Juris Non Excusat* and give one Indian statutory situation where courts have refused to apply it strictly (juvenile offenders, mistake of foreign law).
2. *Expressio Unius Est Exclusio Alterius* appears in a contract clause stating only Monday and Wednesday deliveries are permitted. Is Friday delivery permissible? Cite the maxim's operation and one Indian precedent (SBP & Co. v. Patel Engineering, 2005).

#### Exam Strategy for This 4% Slice

- Read 25–30 maxims with Latin + meaning + statute.
- Allocate ≤45 seconds per maxim-application MCQ; the passage is the time sink, not the maxim recall.
