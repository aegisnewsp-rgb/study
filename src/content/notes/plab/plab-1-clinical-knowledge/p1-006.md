# Data Interpretation and Clinical Reasoning in PLAB 1

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review

PLAB 1 is 180 single-best-answer questions in a single three-hour examination, and each question opens with a short scenario followed by five answers. That is one minute per question including reading, and the minute is mostly spent reading. The GMC describes the paper as assessing "the knowledge and skills expected of a doctor entering the second year of the UK Foundation Programme", which is why the scenario is a patient rather than a topic — you are being asked what you would do, not what a disease is called.

The scenario text is not decoration. Every clinical detail in it is there either to point at a diagnosis or to be a distractor you must consciously discard. Candidates who read quickly to reach the question get the distractors wrong, because they were never identified as distractors.

**Read the verb before you read anything else.**

- "Most appropriate next step" wants a management decision, not a diagnosis
- "Most likely cause" and "best initial investigation" want different answers to the same stem
- "Which finding would distinguish" wants the discriminator between two options you already hold
- The most frequent error in this paper is answering a management question with the diagnosis

**Every detail in the stem is either signal or deliberate noise.**

- Age, sex, onset, duration, severity and the timing modifier carry most of the diagnostic weight
- A stated absence is evidence, and usually it is what separates fourth-best from best
- Look for the one line that does not fit every option you are considering
- If you cannot name the distractor, you have not read the stem

**Four presentations dominate a safe-practice paper.**

- Chest pain, breathlessness, abdominal pain and altered consciousness recur more than any others
- Learn each as a decision tree rather than a list of causes
- For each, know what you cannot miss, what excludes it, and the first investigation and management step
- A diagnostic question and a management question drawn from the same tree have different right answers

**Supplied data tests three things at once.**

- Whether you can read the value, whether you know the reference range, and what the deviation means here
- A mildly abnormal number in a patient with another explanation is a trap
- A normal number in a patient whose features make it serious is the more dangerous trap

**Where candidates lose time.**

- Reading the stem twice and the options once, which reverses the correct order
- Choosing the rarest option because it sounds more clinical than the common one
- Carrying a threshold or referral route from another system into a UK answer
- Spending more than a minute per item; three over-runs cost you six answerable questions

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study

#### Reading a scenario in order

Start at the sentence that gives the problem. Age, sex, onset, duration, severity and the key modifier — "on exertion", "at night", "after eating", "sudden" — carry most of the diagnostic weight. Then read what the scenario deliberately withholds: no fever, no weight loss, no rash, no travel history. The absence of a feature is evidence, and in a five-option question it is usually what separates the fourth-best answer from the correct one.

Then find the clinical detail that does not fit every option. "Blood pressure 96/58 despite fluids" tells you something the age and the complaint do not. Isolating that single line is faster than reading forward three times.

Finally, before you read the options, say what you think is going on. Thirty seconds spent forming a hypothesis shortens the option-reading, because you then know which option you are looking for and which you are looking for reasons to reject.

#### The four presentations that recur

Chest pain, breathlessness, abdominal pain and altered consciousness between them appear in a large share of any safe-practice paper, and they are worth learning as decision trees rather than as lists of causes. For each, know the two or three you cannot miss, the one feature that excludes each of them, and the first investigation and first management step.

That framing matters more than it looks. A question asking for the "most appropriate next step" is not asking for the diagnosis; it is asking whether you would investigate further, treat immediately, refer, or admit. Candidates who jump straight to naming a diagnosis frequently pick the option that is diagnostically impressive and clinically unsafe — the rare disease over the common one, or the drug that treats the complication instead of the cause.

#### Results, charts and films

A question that supplies a blood gas, a full blood count, a renal profile or a drug chart is testing three things: can you read the value, do you know the reference range, and do you know what the abnormality means for the patient in front of you.

The commonest trap is a value that is technically abnormal and clinically irrelevant for that patient — a mildly low haemoglobin in someone with an alternative explanation, a borderline creatinine in a patient who is otherwise well. The reverse trap is more dangerous: a value that is unremarkable in isolation and serious in context, such as a normal potassium in a patient with Addisonian features.

#### Common mistakes

Answering with the most likely diagnosis when the question asked for the next management step. Read the verb. "Most appropriate next step", "most likely cause", "best initial investigation" and "which finding would distinguish" want different answers, and picking the diagnosis for a management question is the single highest-frequency error in this paper.

Selecting the rarest option because it sounds more clinical. PLAB 1 tests safe practice at the Foundation level, not differential diagnosis at consultant level.

Ignoring a deliberately stated negative. It is in the scenario because it excludes something.

Reading the stem twice and the options once. Options are where a stem is misread, so reverse that.

Spending more than a minute on one question. With 180 items, three over-runs cost you six items you could have answered in the time.

Carrying a home-country practice assumption into a UK answer. Guidance thresholds and referral routes differ; where the GMC gives a figure, use it.

### 🔴 Extended — Mastery

#### Single best answer is a claim about confidence, not about probability

The format does not ask which condition is likeliest. It asks which option a competent doctor should choose, and in this paper those are different questions whenever one option is safer but less probable. A dangerous but plausible diagnosis is exactly the situation the format exists to expose, which is why the rare answer is frequently the intended one when the stem describes an unsafe presentation.

That reframing explains the instruction to read the verb before the options. Each verb defines a different decision procedure over the same clinical picture, and a candidate who reads the stem for the diagnosis has already committed to a procedure the question never requested. The options are then read as variations on their hypothesis rather than as answers to what was actually asked.

The practical consequence is that hypothesising too early is actively harmful, even though forming a hypothesis feels productive. Those seconds are seconds not spent reading the options correctly, and the guess then filters what you are willing to see.

#### Distractors are written backwards from the intended answer

Every distractor in this paper exists because a specific mistake produces it. A common presentation misread as an urgency, a diagnosis named without its investigation, a treatment that addresses the complication rather than the cause, a threshold borrowed from another system — each is a specific error, and recognising the error is faster than evaluating the option.

Working backwards is the most efficient preparation available. For a recurring presentation, write the correct option and then the four ways a candidate could arrive at each wrong one. That produces a decision tree you can apply under time pressure, and it doubles as a check on whether you know why the right answer is right rather than merely recognising it.

It also exposes weak knowledge early. Where you cannot generate the distractor, you have usually not got the mechanism, and those are the questions that will cost you under a one-minute limit.

#### The absent feature is doing work

Stems in this format carry negatives on purpose, and they are the easiest data in the paper to skip because an absent feature produces no sentence you feel the need to read. A whole line of withheld findings — the fevers ruled out, the weight loss denied, the rash and travel history simply never mentioned — is a constraint on the option set, and ignoring them leaves you reasoning about a patient who is sicker than the one described.

There is a harder version of this. Where nothing is stated, the question is whether you would ask. A stem that does not mention a drug history has not told you there is one, and an answer depending on a drug you were never given rests on an assumption. In an OSCE that assumption would be exposed by asking; in a written paper it is exposed by the option that names the assumption.

Treat every silence in the stem as unknown rather than as absent, and let the option set tell you which unknowns matter.

#### Time is a clinical variable in this paper

The arithmetic is unforgiving: a fixed budget across a fixed number of items, with no partial credit anywhere, so the only thing that compounds is over-running. Three questions taken two minutes each is six questions that did not get attempted, and the six are as answerable as the three.

The failure mode is not slowness so much as re-reading. A stem misread once becomes a stem read twice, and options are where misreading is detected rather than where it begins — which is why reading the options once and the stem twice inverts the correct order.

Marking your own uncertainty is the practical countermeasure. If you cannot place an item after a genuine attempt, move on and return to it only if the paper allows, because a candidate stationary at the end has answered fewer questions overall than one who moved on and came back.
