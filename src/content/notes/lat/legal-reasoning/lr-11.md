# Legal Reasoning: Problem Solving

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Legal Reasoning: Problem Solving is the skill of taking a **fact pattern** (a short story of events) and working out the correct legal outcome by stating the rule, applying it to the facts, and reaching a conclusion. On the LAT (conducted by HEC Pakistan for 5-year LLB admissions), this skill sits inside the compulsory Legal Reasoning section and rewards **structured answers**, not narrative essays.

**The IRAC scaffold** is the highest-yield tool: **Issue** (what legal question is raised), **Rule** (which constitutional provision or Pakistani statute applies, e.g. PPC, CrPC, CPC, Contract Act 1872), **Application** (match facts to the rule, including any exceptions), **Conclusion** (the legal outcome).

- **Deductive (syllogistic)** reasoning is dominant: Major Premise (rule) + Minor Premise (facts) → Conclusion.
- **Analogical reasoning** compares facts to a precedent, asking whether material facts match the **ratio decidendi**.
- Read every trigger word: *minor*, *consent*, *good faith*, *reasonable doubt* — they switch the rule.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### IRAC and the Syllogism

Every LAT problem-solving answer should follow **IRAC** (Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion) or its mirror **ILAC** (Issue, Law, Application, Conclusion). Beneath this scaffold sits a **deductive syllogism**: a Major Premise (the legal rule, drawn from a statute or binding precedent), a Minor Premise (the specific facts of the scenario), and a Conclusion (the legal outcome). Markers award credit for an *explicit* Rule statement — paraphrasing fairness in place of a cited section is a guaranteed zero on rule-based questions.

#### Four Reasoning Modes Tested

LAT setters rotate between four reasoning modes within the same paper. Recognising which mode a question demands lets you allocate time and structure:

| Reasoning mode | When LAT uses it | What the answer must show |
| --- | --- --- | --- |
| Deductive (syllogistic) | Applying a known statute to given facts | Major Premise + Minor Premise → Conclusion |
| Analogical | A precedent case is supplied in the stem | Compare material facts; decide same rule or distinguish |
| Inductive | A series of fact snippets without a named rule | Derive a general principle from patterns, then apply |
| Rule-based / statutory | Constitution, PPC, CrPC, CPC, Contract Act quoted | Word-by-word matching of statutory text to facts |

#### Issue Spotting

The single most-tested micro-skill is **issue spotting**: the fact pattern often disguises the legal question inside emotionally charged language ("Ali pushed Bilal during an argument and Bilal fell"). The correct issue is *whether the act amounts to culpable hurt under Section 332 PPC*, not "did Ali do a bad thing". Markers for issue spotting look for the candidate's ability to name a specific legal category before any rule is invoked.

#### Common Traps in Application

- Confusing **ratio decidendi** (the binding reason) with **obiter dicta** (persuasive remarks by the judge).
- Forgetting **statutory exceptions** — e.g. the five exceptions to Section 300 PPC that reduce murder to culpable homicide.
- Treating factual similarity as legal similarity in analogical questions without checking whether the *material* facts match.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### How LAT Setters Mark Problem-Solving Answers

HEC's LAT marking scheme awards points at each IRAC stage: typically 1 mark for a correctly framed **Issue**, 2 marks for the **Rule** (must cite a specific statutory section or precedent name), 2 marks for **Application** that engages with exceptions and trigger words, and 1 mark for a **Conclusion** that follows logically. A 5-mark problem-solving question therefore punishes any candidate who skips the Rule — half the marks vanish. This is why IRAC is non-negotiable even when time is short.

#### Worked Mini-Example

**Facts:** A, a 17-year-old, signs a contract to sell his laptop for Rs 50,000 to B. A later refuses to deliver.

**Issue:** Whether the contract is enforceable against a minor under the Contract Act 1872.

**Rule:** Section 11 of the Contract Act 1872 requires every party to be of the age of majority (18 years) to be competent to contract. The leading precedent *Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose* (Privy Council) holds that a minor's agreement is void *ab initio*.

**Application:** A is 17, below the age of majority. The agreement is therefore void from inception. B cannot enforce specific performance, though B may seek restitution of any consideration A still holds.

**Conclusion:** The contract is unenforceable; B's remedy is limited to restitution.

#### Edge Cases and Adjacent Topics

- **Distinguishing precedent**: if the supplied precedent has materially different facts (different capacity, different subject matter), argue *distinction* rather than blind application.
- **Statutory interpretation tools**: *literal*, *mischief*, and *purposive* rules appear when LAT tests ambiguous wording — useful for Constitution-based questions on Articles 4, 19, or 25.
- **Time-per-question**: allow 90 seconds per problem-solving MCQ; flag, return, and skip if the issue is unclear after 30 seconds.

#### Practice Prompts

1. A driver parks on a hospital ambulance lane blocking an emergency vehicle. Identify the issue and the statutory rule under Pakistani traffic/CrPC law that applies.
2. Two tenants sign a lease; one is discovered to be 16 years old. Decide, using IRAC, whether the landlord can recover unpaid rent.
