# Vocabulary in Context

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Vocabulary in Context (VIC) is the MUET Reading Paper 2 component that asks you to identify the meaning of an unfamiliar word or phrase **as it is used** in a short passage, not as the dictionary defines it. Items in most keys appear as a brief paragraph with one highlighted target word and four options; your job is to pick the option whose meaning matches the surrounding context.

| Term | What it means in a VIC item |
|---|---|
| Context clue | A word, phrase, or punctuation mark in the sentence that signals the target word's meaning |
| Denotation | The literal, dictionary-level meaning of a word |
| Connotation | The emotional or evaluative load a word carries (positive, neutral, negative) |
| Collocation | The natural pairing of two words (e.g. *heavy* rain, not *strong* rain) |
| Semantic prosody | The consistent feeling (positive/negative) a word takes from its neighbours |
| Register | The formality level — academic, neutral, or informal |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"DREAM-CAP"** — the seven context clue types: **D**efinition/restatement, **R**estatement/synonym, **E**xample, **A**ntonym/contrast, **M**arker punctuation (dashes, commas), **C**ause–effect, **A**nd **P**ronoun/reference. Spot the clue first, then choose.

Three rules carry 80% of the marks. Read the sentence containing the target word plus one sentence before and one after. Ignore your prior knowledge of the word if the surrounding text contradicts it. Match the **register** and **polarity** of the surrounding discourse, not just the topic.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### How a VIC item is built

Every MUET VIC item pairs a short discourse (1–3 sentences) with a single highlighted target word. The correct option paraphrases the contextual meaning; the three distractors in most keys belong to the same semantic field but break one of three rules — wrong register, wrong collocation, or wrong polarity.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Concept | Look-alike | Why students confuse them | How to tell them apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| Denotation | Connotation | Both are "meanings" | Denotation = literal; connotation = emotional colouring |
| Synonym clue | Antonym clue | Both involve similar words | Synonym = *in other words, that is*; antonym = *but, however, unlike* |
| Collocation | Semantic field | Both group related words | Collocation = fixed pair (*make* a decision); semantic field = loose topic set (decision, choice, verdict) |
| Register | Formality level | Same idea, different label | Register shifts with audience: academic ↔ colloquial |
| Semantic prosody | Polarity | Both judge good/bad | Prosody = word's habitual mood; polarity = this sentence's positive/negative load |
| Word family | Inflection | Both extend the headword | Word family = all derived forms; inflection = grammar-only (singular/plural, tense) |

#### The Seven Clue Types (DREAM-CAP expanded)

1. **Definition / restatement** — "The defendant, *that is*, a person accused of a crime…" The appositive defines the target.
2. **Synonym / paraphrase** — nearby words echo the target's meaning.
3. **Example** — "such as, for example, like" introduces a concrete instance that clarifies an abstract target.
4. **Antonym / contrast** — *however, although, unlike, whereas* flip the expected sense.
5. **Punctuation marker** — dashes, commas, brackets, or quotation marks set off a gloss.
6. **Cause–effect / inference** — the result or reason in the sentence restricts the target to a cause or outcome sense.
7. **Pronoun / reference** — a later pronoun (*it, they, this*) refers back to the target, narrowing its referent.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

#### Question:
> *"The CEO's announcement was met with a flurry of criticism. Although some employees found the plan **judicious**, others argued it was reckless and short-sighted."*
> In the context of the passage, the word **judicious** most nearly means:
> (A) harsh (B) wise (C) popular (D) vague

#### Solution:
1. Identify the contrast signal: **"Although … others argued … reckless"** sets up an antonym relation.
2. "Judicious" must therefore carry the **opposite meaning** of *reckless and short-sighted*.
3. *Reckless* = rash, unwise → the opposite sense is *wise, well-judged*.
4. Match to options: (A) harsh → no contrast with reckless; (C) popular → not implied; (D) vague → contradicts the certainty of a "plan". Only **(B) wise** fits both the antonym cue and the positive polarity of the first clause.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Many students know *judicious* contains the root *judge* and assume it means "judgmental" or "harsh." That is a denotation bias. The antonym cue *although … reckless* forces a positive, prudent meaning — register is also academic-positive, ruling out (C) popular, which is informal-affective.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Distractor Mechanics You Will Meet on Paper 2

| Trap type | How it is constructed | How to defuse it |
|---|---|---|
| Semantic inertia | Distractor = the word's default dictionary sense | Force yourself to read the **local sentence**, not your memory |
| Polarity flip | Distractor carries the wrong +/- sign | Check connective markers (*however, yet, despite*) before choosing |
| Collocational mismatch | Distractor pairs with the noun/adverb unnaturally (*do a decision* instead of *make a decision*) | Mentally plug the option back into the original collocation slot |
| Register slip | Distractor is informal where the text is academic (or vice versa) | Scan surrounding vocabulary for academic/chatty tone |
| Hypernym / hyponym confusion | Distractor is broader or narrower than the contextual sense | Ask: is the text talking about the category or one specific kind? |

#### Theoretical Grounding

VIC items rest on Firth's principle that "you shall know a word by the company it keeps." In Halliday's systemic-functional framework, context of situation (field, tenor, mode) restricts the lexical choices a writer makes. A MUET passage on, say, environmental policy will draw vocabulary from a coherent **semantic field**; the highlighted target will collocate with neighbours in that field, so deducing the meaning is partly a discourse-coherence task, not purely a single-sentence lookup.

#### Lexical Coverage Background

Chulalongkorn MUET research places adequate reading comprehension at roughly **6,000–8,000 word families** for 95–98% text coverage. Because VIC items intentionally test low-frequency academic words, candidates who systematically learn the **Academic Word List (AWL)** and practise inflectional–derivational families (*decide, decision, decisive, decisively, indecisive*) gain measurable accuracy gains on this section.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. Take any MUET past-year passage and highlight every word you do not know. For each, write one sentence using the word with its correct collocational partner, then one false sentence where you deliberately break the collocation. Notice why the false version sounds wrong.
2. Build a mini glossary of 20 AWL headwords. For each, record (a) one synonym, (b) one antonym, (c) one fixed collocation, (d) one example sentence. Review weekly. This is the highest-leverage habit for MUET Reading Paper 2.

#### Common Mistakes Catalogue

- Skipping the connective markers that flip polarity.
- Choosing the option that *can* fit a generic sentence, even though it does not fit *this* one.
- Ignoring quotation marks and dashes — they in many papers hide a direct gloss.
- Treating a pronoun later in the passage as unrelated; it in most keys indexes the target.
