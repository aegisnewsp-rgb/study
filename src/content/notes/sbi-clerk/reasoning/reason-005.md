# Input-Output & Machine Rearrangement: reading the "Reasoning Ability & Computer Aptitude" paper

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> One paper, two subjects, and a marks split the bank publishes but rarely explains.

The mains paper is called **Reasoning Ability & Computer Aptitude** — not "Reasoning Ability". The distinction is in the bank\'s own advertisement, and it changes the paper:

| Phase | Test | Questions | Marks | Time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Prelims (Phase-I) | Reasoning Ability | 35 | 35 | 20 min |
| Mains (Phase-II) | Reasoning Ability & Computer Aptitude | 50 | **60** | 45 min |

**50 questions carrying 60 marks.** The mains paper is not one mark each. In the preliminary Reasoning Ability paper the ratio is exactly 1:1 (35 questions, 35 marks). In the mains it is not, and candidates who rehearse the prelims habit of equating questions with marks misjudge the paper they are sitting.

Two more published facts that shape the plan:

- **Negative marking is one quarter of the mark assigned for the question**, in both phases. The penalty attaches to the mark assigned, which is why a paper where marks are uneven punishes wrong answers unevenly too.
- **Phase-I marks are not added.** The advertisement states that marks obtained in the Preliminary Examination "will not be added for the selection and only the aggregate marks obtained in Main Examination (Phase-II) will be considered". Prelims is a filter. Survive it, then treat mains as the entire competition.

Also published: no minimum qualifying marks are prescribed for an individual subject — the bank decides the minimum on aggregate. So no section can disqualify you, but a section you score poorly in still caps your aggregate.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> A machine that reorders letters must be watched, not solved. Here is how to watch it.

#### What the question format is

A machine-rearrangement question gives you an **input sequence** and a **rule**, either stated in words ("all letters are arranged alphabetically and the first letter is replaced by a vowel") or demonstrated by two input–output pairs followed by a third input. You produce the output for the third input.

Four formats, in rising difficulty:

1. **Stated rule, unseen application.** The rule is written out; you apply it once.
2. **Two worked pairs, third pair inferred.** The rule must be deduced from the examples.
3. **Step-count questions.** Given the output, work backwards to find the input, or ask how many steps a rule takes.
4. **Mixed machine** — a symbol shift combined with a positional change and a substitution, sometimes across multiple lines.

#### The method that actually works

Every input–output question is a **trace**, not a puzzle. Write the input, then write the output underneath, then fill the steps between them one line at a time. The candidates who lose time are the ones who try to see the answer in their head and then reconstruct it afterwards.

For the two-pair format, the first thing to do is align the two given pairs vertically and look at **what stayed the same**. Characters that did not move and did not change are the frame the rule works inside. Only then look at what moved, and only then at what changed.

A worked habit worth adopting: mark position. Write each character with its index above it. Most machine rules are positional (first, last, middle, every second, all but one) and a positional rule is invisible until you have written the positions down.

#### The direction rule is the commonest change

If a rule moves characters, it moves them **forward** or **backward** through the alphabet, usually by one or two, and sometimes by a different amount for odd and even positions. Three things to check every time:

- **Wrap-around.** X moving forward by one becomes Y, not an invalid symbol. Wrap both ends.
- **Uneven shifts.** A rule can move even positions forward two and odd positions backward one. Read the pair twice before assuming uniformity.
- **Changes are not moves.** A substitution can look like a move if you skip the intermediate step.

#### Computer Aptitude: the other half of the paper

Because the paper is named for both, treat computer basics as examinable rather than incidental. Work through these areas deliberately:

- **Input and output devices** — keyboard, mouse, scanner, printer, monitor, speaker, microphone; and which of them is which class. This is the highest-frequency cluster.
- **Memory** — primary versus secondary; RAM as volatile and ROM as non-volatile; what a disk, pen drive and SSD each are.
- **Software categories** — system software versus application software; an operating system as system software; an antivirus as application software.
- **Basic networking and hardware terms** — what a network, server and bandwidth are, and what a URL or an IP address does.

The trap here is category confusion, not ignorance. "Is an operating system system software or application software?" is not a knowledge question, it is a classification question, and candidates who have studied the list rather than the categories get it wrong.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Squeezing the last marks out of an uneven-marks paper.

#### Question counts and marks counts are not the same number

The mains paper runs 50 questions for 60 marks. That is not a typo in the advertisement, and it should change how you allocate time. If the computer aptitude cluster carries a higher per-question mark than the reasoning cluster, then a question of moderate difficulty in that cluster can be worth more than a hard reasoning question — and the rational response is to attempt the higher-mark questions first.

Practise reading the paper for **marks per question** rather than difficulty. Where the paper does not signal per-question marks, infer it from the structure: a paper named for two subjects and weighted above its question count implies that at least some cluster is marked above 1.

#### The two-pass discipline under quarter negative marking

With a penalty of one quarter of the mark assigned, and marks uneven inside the paper, the optimal rule is still one-in-four odds — but it is applied to *marks*, not to questions. Guessing a 2-mark question at one-in-five odds loses more than guessing a 1-mark question at one-in-five odds, even though both are "a guess".

So build the paper in two passes:

1. **Pass one — the certainties.** Syllogisms with clean deductive structure, calendar and clock, direction sense, coding-decoding with an explicit rule, machine questions where the rule is stated rather than inferred. These are your floor.
2. **Pass two — the inferences.** Machine questions with two worked pairs, mixed multi-line machines, and computer-appliance classification. Slower, but a correctly traced machine is worth as much as any other question.

Skip everything else and return only with time and only where you can eliminate options.

#### Why prelims reasoning and mains reasoning are different skills

Thirty-five questions in twenty minutes is 34 seconds each. That is triage speed: pattern recognition, no extended tracing, immediate abandon on anything that needs a second read.

The mains Reasoning Ability & Computer Aptitude paper gives 45 minutes for 50 questions — 54 seconds each, on a paper that also includes a subject with a factual syllabus. That is closer to normal working speed, which is where a two-pair machine deduction becomes affordable.

The practical preparation split: build prelims readiness with a large volume of fast pattern items, and build mains readiness with a smaller volume of carefully traced machines and classification questions. Preparing only one of the two is the most common reason a candidate clears the call and then finishes mid-table.

#### What the advertisement does not tell you

State it plainly to yourself before you build a plan: the advertisement fixes the paper, not the topic list. It gives test names, question counts, marks, durations, the medium of examination state-wise, the negative-marking rule, the pay scale (starting basic pay Rs.26730 for graduates) and the six-month probation with e-lessons for confirmation. It does not publish a question-by-question syllabus.

So treat the topic list as a coverage map you are responsible for, and treat the published pattern as the hard constraint you must fit inside. Anything else — including any coaching claim about how many questions come from which topic — is not in the bank's notice.

#### Common mistakes and exam strategy

1. **Equating questions and marks because prelims does.** The mains paper is 50 questions for 60 marks. Allocate time against marks.
2. **Assuming a weak subject disqualifies you.** No per-subject minimum is prescribed; the bank sets an aggregate minimum.
3. **Carrying over time between tests.** Each test is separately timed.
4. **Guessing without elimination.** A quarter-mark penalty on an uneven paper punishes wrong answers unevenly; guess only above one-in-four odds for that question's mark value.
5. **Attempting the prelims paper as though prelims marks count.** Phase-I marks are not added to the merit list. Clear the call, then rebuild for mains.

> **Exam strategy:** on the mains paper, sweep for the stated-rule machine questions and the appliance-classification questions first. They are the highest certainty per second in a paper that mixes deduction with recall, and they are exactly the items most often abandoned in favour of a harder reasoning question that is worth no more.

#### Practice prompts

1. *"A mains paper carries 50 questions for 60 marks. What does that imply about marks per question, and how should it change the order of attempt?"* — Reason from the weight per question, then from the quarter-mark penalty.
2. *"Why do two worked pairs matter more than the rule statement in a machine-input question?"* — Explain the vertical alignment method and what constant characters reveal about the frame.
3. *"Computer Aptitude is named in the mains paper title. Prepare the classification questions a bank is most likely to ask, and say why category confusion is the usual failure."* — Cover system versus application software, primary versus secondary memory, and input/output device classes.
