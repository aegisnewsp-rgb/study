# Algebraic Expressions and Operations

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

An **algebraic expression** combines numbers, variables, and operation signs — never an equals sign. The four standard expansions below cover roughly 80% of NECO Paper I questions on this topic.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"FOIL-BOD"** — *First, Outer, Inner, Last* for two-bracket expansion; *Brackets, Orders, Division, Multiplication, Addition, Subtraction* for order of operations. Two words. One acronym. Saves marks.

| Expansion | Result | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| (a + b)² | a² + 2ab + b² | Perfect-square trinomial |
| (a − b)² | a² − 2ab + b² | Negative middle term |
| (a + b)(a − b) | a² − b² | Difference of two squares |
| (x + a)(x + b) | x² + (a + b)x + ab | Quadratic with leading 1 |

**Like terms** share the same variable part — only their coefficients combine. *Unlike terms* (e.g. 3x and 3x²) cannot be added directly, a frequent NECO trap.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Working knowledge of expansions, factorisation, and the laws that govern them.

#### Classification and Degree

Expressions are sorted by the number of terms they contain. A **monomial** has one term (5x), a **binomial** has two (2x + 3), a **trinomial** has three (x² + 4x − 7). Any expression with one or more terms is a **polynomial**.

The **degree** is the highest exponent on any single variable in the expression. So x³ + 2x has degree 3, not degree 1. NECO candidates frequently misread this — they average exponents instead of taking the largest.

#### The Distributive Law at Work

Multiplication distributes over addition and subtraction:

> 📌 **Formula Check:** a(b + c) = ab + ac, where a, b, c ∈ ℝ (dimensionless). Sign carries through every term — a(b − c) = ab − ac.

This single law powers every expansion from two brackets to a polynomial of degree five. Removing brackets with a negative sign in front requires flipping every internal sign: −(2x − 5) becomes −2x + 5.

#### Comparison Matrix — Commonly Confused Expansions

| Expression | Correct expansion | Wrong expansion students choose | Why it's wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a + b)² | a² + 2ab + b² | a² + b² | The middle term 2ab is missing |
| (a − b)² | a² − 2ab + b² | a² − 2ab − b² | Last sign is wrong |
| a² − b² | (a + b)(a − b) | (a − b)² | Factors differ in sign |
| (x + a)(x + b) | x² + (a + b)x + ab | x² + ab | The x-term is missing |

#### Worked Problem

Simplify 3(2x − 4) − (x² − 5x + 6) and factorise the result where possible.

Step 1 — Distribute: 3(2x − 4) = 6x − 12.

Step 2 — Remove the second bracket: −(x² − 5x + 6) = −x² + 5x − 6.

Step 3 — Combine: 6x − 12 − x² + 5x − 6 = −x² + 11x − 18.

Step 4 — Factorise. Find two numbers that multiply to −18 and add to 11: those are +18 and −1. So −x² + 11x − 18 = −(x² − 11x + 18) = −(x − 9)(x − 2).

Answer: −(x − 9)(x − 2), or equivalently (9 − x)(x − 2).

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Candidates often drop the negative sign in step 2 and write +x² − 5x + 6. A single missed sign flips the factorisation entirely — check each internal term.

#### Substitution and Evaluation

Replacing every variable with its numerical value follows BODMAS strictly. For the expression 2x² − 3x + 7 when x = −2: 2(4) − 3(−2) + 7 = 8 + 6 + 7 = 21. Brackets first, then powers, then the order of operations.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Edge cases, common pitfalls, and the mechanics examiners exploit.

#### Operations on Directed Numbers Inside Expressions

Negative coefficients behave normally under arithmetic, but signs interact inside brackets in ways that confuse Paper II candidates. Multiplying two negatives yields a positive: (−2x)(−3x) = 6x². Multiplying a negative by a positive keeps the negative: (−2x)(3x) = −6x². A bracket preceded by a negative sign flips every term inside, including any negative coefficients.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** In −2(3x − 4), the result is −6x + 8 — the −8 comes from multiplying −2 by −4, not from any rule about "subtracting 4". Candidates write −6x − 8 and lose two marks.

#### Factorisation Limits and Polynomial Identity

Every quadratic ax² + bx + c factorises over the integers if and only if b² − 4ac is a perfect square. Take 2x² + 5x + 3: b² − 4ac = 25 − 24 = 1, so it factorises as (2x + 3)(x + 1). But 2x² + 3x + 5: b² − 4ac = 9 − 40 = −31, which is not a perfect square, so the expression does not factorise neatly. Candidates must stop hunting for factors once the discriminant fails — leaving the quadratic in its standard form is the correct answer.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. Show that (2x + 1)² − (2x − 1)² simplifies to 8x. Use this result to evaluate (2001)² − (1999)² without direct multiplication.
2. Factorise x⁴ − 16 completely and state each factor's degree. Hint: treat x⁴ as (x²)² and apply the difference of two squares twice.

#### Mastery Checklist

- Distribute a single bracket, including negative signs.
- Expand (a + b)(c + d) using FOIL.
- Apply the three standard quadratic identities.
- Distinguish like terms from unlike terms at a glance.
- Know when a quadratic refuses to factor and why.
- Evaluate expressions by substitution using BODMAS.
