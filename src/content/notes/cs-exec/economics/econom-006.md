# Demand Analysis

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Demand analysis** studies how much of a good buyers are willing and able to purchase at different prices, with other factors held constant (ceteris paribus). The **Law of Demand** states an inverse price–quantity relationship, formalised as Qd = f(P, P_r, Y, T, E, N), where Qd is quantity demanded, P is the good's own price, P_r is the price of related goods, Y is income, T is tastes, E is expectations, and N is the number of buyers.

- **Price elasticity (Ed)** = (% change in Qd) / (% change in P); |Ed| > 1 is elastic, < 1 inelastic, = 1 unitary.
- **Income elasticity (Ey)** is positive for **normal goods**, negative for **inferior goods**, and >1 for **luxuries**.
- **Cross elasticity (Ec)** is positive for **substitutes** and negative for **complements**.

CS Executive expects a 5-mark definition question almost every session, often paired with a 2-mark elasticity calculation.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Law of Demand and the Demand Function

The Law of Demand states that, ceteris paribus, quantity demanded of a good varies inversely with its price. The functional form Qd = f(P, P_r, Y, T, E, N) captures this: a rise in P lowers Qd (movement along the curve), whereas a change in any of P_r, Y, T, E, or N shifts the entire curve. A change in **price of the good** causes movement; a change in **determinants** causes shift.

#### Elasticity of Demand

Elasticity measures responsiveness of Qd to a change in its determinant, expressed as a ratio of percentage changes (dimensionless).

| Concept | Key point |
| --- | --- |
| Price elasticity (Ed) | %ΔQd / %ΔP; |Ed|>1 elastic, <1 inelastic, =1 unitary |
| Income elasticity (Ey) | %ΔQd / %ΔY; positive = normal, negative = inferior, >1 = luxury |
| Cross elasticity (Ec) | %ΔQd of A / %ΔP of B; positive = substitutes, negative = complements |

The **point elasticity** formula Ed = (ΔQ · P) / (ΔP · Q) is what CS Executive numericals test. Slope of the demand curve is NOT elasticity — elasticity depends on the P/Q ratio at the chosen point, which is why the same linear demand curve is elastic in its upper half and inelastic in its lower half.

#### Total Expenditure Test

Total Expenditure (TE) = P × Q (₹). When price falls: if TE rises, demand is elastic; if TE falls, demand is inelastic; if TE is unchanged, demand is unitary. This test lets you classify elasticity without computation.

#### Determinants and Forecasting

Key determinants are income, prices of substitutes and complements, tastes, expectations, and population size. **Demand forecasting** methods — survey of buyers' intentions, expert opinion, time-series projection, and econometric models — feed managerial planning, inventory, and pricing decisions.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Worked Numeric: Price Elasticity from a Demand Schedule

Suppose price drops from ₹40 to ₹30 and quantity rises from 50 units to 80 units.

Using point elasticity at P = ₹40, Q = 50:
ΔQ = 30, ΔP = –10, so Ed = (30 × 40) / (–10 × 50) = 1200 / –500 = –2.4; |Ed| = 2.4, which is elastic.

The total-expenditure test confirms it: before, TE = 40 × 50 = ₹2,000; after, TE = 30 × 80 = ₹2,400. Expenditure rises when price falls — consistent with elastic demand.

#### Edge Cases and Classification Traps

| Situation | Sign / Magnitude | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ey = –0.8 | Negative | Inferior necessity |
| Ec between tea and coffee = +1.5 | Positive | Strong substitutes |
| Ec between cars and petrol = –0.6 | Negative | Complements |
| Perfectly inelastic (Ed = 0) | Zero | Necessities like salt |
| Perfectly elastic (Ed = ∞) | Infinite | Homogeneous commodities |

#### Short-Run vs Long-Run Analysis

Demand is usually more elastic in the long run because consumers can find substitutes, adjust habits, or change durable-goods purchases. Salt, insulin, and matchsticks stay inelastic even over time because of essentiality or habit.

#### Common Mistakes in the Exam Hall

1. Treating slope as elasticity — slope is constant on a straight-line demand curve, but elasticity varies along it.
2. Forgetting the modulus — Ed is reported as an absolute value; the sign just shows the direction of change.
3. Misreading cross elasticity signs — positive means substitutes, not "more demand".
5. Ignoring ceteris paribus when stating the Law of Demand and getting caught by Veblen/Giffen counter-examples.
4. Using the Total Expenditure test backwards — TE falling when price falls means inelastic, not elastic.

#### Practice Prompts

1. A 10% price rise reduces quantity demanded by 25%. Compute Ed and state whether demand is elastic, inelastic, or unitary.
2. Income rises 8% and quantity demanded of brand X rises 2%. Classify X as a luxury, normal necessity, or inferior good, and justify.
