# The Writing Task: A Profession-Specific Letter


### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review

The OET Test Handbook 2026 specifies a single Writing task of 45 minutes, split into five minutes of reading time followed by forty minutes of writing. The candidate writes to a health professional who is caring for the patient, working from a set of case notes. The handbook tailors the task to your profession and marks it on six criteria, whose scores combine and convert to the same 500 scale.

**The reading minutes are assessed.** Five minutes is a fifth of the task, and within the notes sits one item that redirects it: a result that changes the management, a request addressed to you rather than to the reader, something already done and mentioned only in passing, or a detail included precisely so that you will recognise it as noise.

**Read in three passes.** Facts and sequence, then anything that instructs the reader, then what is missing — a gap is usually a prompt to ask.

**The letter has a fixed job.** Start from the reader's role, what they must be told, and the outcome you need back from them.

- Open with the patient and the reason for writing in the first two or three lines.
- Summarise rather than transcribe; the reader already has the notes.
- Make action requests explicit and, where appropriate, dated. "Please arrange" beats "it might be worth considering".
- Close with safety-netting: what the patient was told, what to watch for, what to do if it worsens.

**Recurring discriminators.** Audience appropriateness, organisation and prioritisation, accuracy against the notes, and unambiguous language.

**Write for the fastest correct reader,** not the most sympathetic one, and give every clinical recommendation its own subject and its own verb.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study

The official OET Test Handbook 2026 is specific about the Writing sub-test: one task, 45 minutes in total, and the handbook gives that total as "5 minutes reading time and 40 minutes writing time". The task is a letter to a health professional caring for a patient, written in response to a set of case notes. Writing is tailored to the candidate's profession, and the handbook states it is assessed using six criteria whose scores are combined and converted to the 500 scale.

Five minutes of reading is a fifth of the task, and treating it as a formality is the single most expensive mistake available in OET Writing.

#### The reading minutes are part of the assessment
The case notes will contain something that changes what you write: a detail that alters the plan, an instruction to the reader, a previous action already taken, or a piece of information that is deliberately irrelevant so that you can tell it is irrelevant.

Candidates who skip to writing within a minute produce a well-composed letter that answers a slightly different task, and the error is invisible to them because the prose is good. The five minutes exist because OET wants to see you identify which details carry instructions and which carry background.

Read the case notes three times in those five minutes. First pass for the facts and the sequence. Second pass for anything that instructs the reader — a suggested action, a question, a request for information. Third pass for what is missing, because a gap in the notes is usually a deliberate prompt to ask rather than an oversight.


#### What the letter has to do
A clinical letter is a functional document with a fixed job, and the structure follows from that job. Who the reader is, what they need to know, and what you are asking them to do.

Establish the patient and the reason for writing in the first two or three lines. A reader who does not know in the opening sentence why this letter exists will not find it later.

Summarise rather than transcribe. The reader has the notes and needs the significant points. Six lines of chronological detail is less useful than three lines of what has changed and why it matters.

Be explicit about action. If you want the reader to do something, say it plainly and say by when. "Please arrange" is clearer than "it might be worth considering". This is also where the register of the letter matters: the register of a referral is not the register of a discharge summary.

Close with safety-netting where it is appropriate — what the patient has been told, what to watch for, and what to do if it worsens. It is consistently under-written and it is part of what a safe letter contains.


#### Writing against the criteria, not against your taste
The handbook states the writing sub-test is assessed on six criteria. That tells you to practise against criteria rather than against your own sense of good writing, because those are not the same thing.

The recurring discriminators are whether the content is appropriate to the audience, whether it is organised and prioritised, whether it is accurate against the case notes, and whether the language is clear and unambiguous. Those four generate most of the score in most scripts.

Two habits follow. Write for the fastest correct reader, not for the most sympathetic one, because the examiner is marking quickly against criteria. And write every clinical recommendation as a complete sentence with a subject and a verb — "increased the dose" is not a letter, it is a note fragment.


#### Common mistakes
Starting to write in the first minute. The 5 minutes of reading time is specified in the handbook for a reason.

Transcribing the case notes chronologically instead of summarising the significant points.

Leaving the reader to work out what you want them to do. Action requests must be explicit and, where appropriate, dated.

Using fragments as the main sentences of the letter. A letter made of note-form entries scores poorly on the language criteria even when every fact is right.

Hedging everything into vagueness. "The patient seems to have perhaps had some reaction" is not safer than stating what was observed.

Forgetting safety-netting in a letter where the clinical situation warrants it.

Writing to the wrong audience. A letter to a pharmacist about a dispensing query and a letter to a GP about a management plan share vocabulary and almost nothing else.

### 🔴 Extended — Mastery

#### Where the letter is actually decided

Most lost marks in this task are lost before writing starts. The notes are built to contain a detail that silently redirects the task: a result already received, an instruction from the referring clinician, a change of plan. A candidate who starts composing inside the first minute produces fluent prose answering a slightly different question, and cannot detect the error because the writing is good. Reading time is not a formality the handbook attached to a task; it is the phase in which you discover what kind of letter this one is.

#### Gaps in the notes are prompts

A third pass looking for what is absent earns its minute. Missing values, undated events and unanswered questions in a case summary are usually deliberate: they tell you the reader expects to be asked. A letter that invents an answer to a missing detail scores worse on accuracy than one that asks for it, because accuracy here means fidelity to the notes rather than completeness of the story.

#### The reader's next action sets the register

The same clinical facts produce different letters for a pharmacist with a dispensing query and a GP managing a plan, and the difference is not vocabulary alone — it is which facts get led with and what the letter asks for at the end. A referral leads with the reason for referral and closes with the requested action; a discharge summary leads with the episode and closes with what the GP should watch. Decide what the reader must do next, then decide what they must know to do it.

#### Explicit and dated requests

An action request has three parts: what you want, who should do it, and by when. Vague modal phrasing in a letter reads as deference and is scored as imprecision. "Please arrange" is clear; "it might be worth considering" hands the decision back to a reader who is trying to close a request on a busy day.

#### Safety-netting is structural

Good safety-netting is not a closing platitude but three specific items: what the patient has been told in what terms, what signs would mean the situation is worsening, and what the patient should do if they appear. It is consistently under-written, and in a profession-tailored task it is one of the clearest signals of whether you are writing as a clinician who has handed over responsibility properly.

#### Fragment loss and hedge loss

Two language failures pull in opposite directions. Writing the letter as a sequence of note-form entries loses marks on language criteria even when every fact is correct, because each entry has no subject, no verb and no reader. Hedging everything into vagueness loses them differently: a phrase that hedges the observation as well as the conclusion concedes that what was seen cannot be relied on, when your job in the letter is to state what was seen and let the reader weigh it.
