# Business Scenario Analysis

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Business Scenario Analysis** in XAT Decision-Making is a passage-based reasoning task. A short business case (300–500 words on strategy, HR, ethics, or markets) is followed by 2–4 linked questions testing judgment, not arithmetic. Success hinges on identifying the real **decision-maker**, mapping **stakeholders**, isolating **sunk vs. relevant costs**, and weighing **short-term gains against long-term positioning**.

- The dominant XAT question type: ~4–6 cases × 2–3 questions = roughly 8–14 questions in the Decision-Making section.
- Core numeric tool: **EMV = Σ (Pᵢ × Vᵢ)**, where Pᵢ is outcome probability (0–1) and Vᵢ is the monetary value (₹). When unsure, ask "would this number change if I picked a different option?"
- Two traps to avoid: treating cases as calculation drills, and choosing the "boldest-sounding" option over the defensible one.

#### Must-know framing tools
| Tool | When to deploy |
| --- | --- |
| Stakeholder Map | Always — list decision-maker, affected parties, silent parties |
| SWOT | When the case is about firm strategy or new launch |
| EMV table | When probabilities or payoffs are explicit |
| Urgency–Importance (Eisenhower) | When prioritising among multiple action items |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Anatomy of an XAT business scenario
Each case compresses a managerial dilemma into a paragraph: a firm faces declining margins, a vendor breach, an ethical lapse, or a hiring crisis. The questions rarely ask "what is the answer?" — they ask "what *should* the protagonist do, and why?" You score by demonstrating structured reasoning: name the decision-maker, separate facts from assumptions, then evaluate options against **ethical, legal, and economic** lenses.

#### The reasoning sequence
1. Read the case once for the protagonist's goal and the binding constraint (cash crunch, regulation, brand promise).
2. Apply **5-Whys** to distinguish the symptom (falling sales) from the root cause (weak distribution).
3. Build a stakeholder table — decision-maker, affected, and silent parties — and weight their claims.
4. Score each option on three axes: profitability, legality, and reputation. Discard options that fail legality or ethics regardless of margin.
5. Pick the option with the best **defensible trade-off**, then justify using EMV or opportunity-cost language.

#### Decision tools you must handle fluently
| Concept | Key point |
| --- | --- |
| Sunk vs. Relevant Cost | Ignore sunk; only future, incremental costs affect the choice |
| Opportunity Cost | Value of the best foregone alternative, applied to time, capital, and strategy |
| Marginal Analysis | Continue an action while marginal benefit > marginal cost |
| Information Asymmetry | Watch for principal–agent gaps: who knows what, and who acts on it? |
| Pareto / Nash intuition | A Pareto move helps no one by hurting another; Nash is a stable mutual best response |

#### Common examiner traps
- Confusing correlation with causation when diagnosing the firm's problem.
- Picking the "most aggressive" option because it sounds decisive.
- Ignoring **implicit constraints** embedded in the passage (a hidden cash crunch or brand promise).
- Treating qualitative cases as arithmetic problems — XAT rewards reasoning, not raw calculation.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Worked micro-example
A mid-sized FMCG firm faces a 12% volume drop after a competitor launches a cheaper variant. The CEO must choose between (A) a ₹2 price cut, (B) a ₹50 lakh ad blitz, or (C) a new packaging line at ₹3 crore capex.

Apply EMV-style reasoning. Suppose internal estimates give Option A a 60% chance of recovering 8% volume (₹6 crore benefit) and a 40% chance of triggering a price war (₹4 crore loss). EMV(A) = 0.6×6 − 0.4×4 = **₹2.0 crore**. Option B's EMV is uncertain and brand-dependent; Option C ignores the **time value** of waiting two years while competitors entrench. The defensible recommendation depends on cash reserves — an *implicit constraint* the passage may not state, which is precisely why XAT rewards readers who flag hidden assumptions rather than compute blindly.

#### Advanced frames for mastery
| Frame | Use it when the case involves… |
| --- | --- |
| Decision Tree (EVPI) | Information has a price; ask whether paying for market research pays off |
| Externalities | Pollution, layoffs, or community impact the firm is externalising |
| Game-Theory intuition | Competitor reactions matter; unilateral optimisation loses |
| Hypothesis-Testing mindset | Treat the passage as evidence; discount claims that aren't supported |
| Strategic Decision Frame | Long-run positioning vs. quarterly margin |

#### Edge cases that decide top-percentile scores
- A case where the legally correct and ethically correct options diverge.
- A stakeholder conflict where satisfying one group (shareholders) directly harms another (employees).
- A scenario where doing nothing is the *optimal* choice — XAT rewards recognising this.

#### Practice prompts
1. A supplier is caught using child labour in a tier-2 facility. Should the firm publicly cut ties, audit quietly, or stay silent pending verification? Map stakeholders, list trade-offs, and defend your call.
2. A startup's founder must choose between accepting a lowball acquisition offer (3× revenue) or raising a priced round that dilutes them below 30%. Compute opportunity cost of each path using qualitative EMV.
