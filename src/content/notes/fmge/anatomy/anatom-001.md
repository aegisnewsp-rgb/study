# Upper Limb — Brachial Plexus, Axillary Artery, Shoulder Joint & Muscles of Arm and Forearm

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

The **brachial plexus** is formed by ventral rami of **C5–T1**, organised into **roots → trunks (upper, middle, lower) → divisions (anterior, posterior) → cords (lateral, medial, posterior) → five terminal branches**: musculocutaneous, axillary, radial, median, ulnar. The **axillary artery** begins at the lateral border of the 1st rib and ends at the inferior border of teres major; **pectoralis minor divides it into three parts**, each giving specific branches.

The **shoulder (glenohumeral) joint** is a synovial ball-and-socket articulation between the head of humerus and the glenoid cavity. The **rotator cuff (SITS)** — supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis — provides dynamic stability.

- **Erb's palsy (C5–C6)**: "waiter's tip" deformity.
- **Klumpke's palsy (C8–T1)**: claw hand ± Horner's syndrome.
- **Cubital fossa contents (medial→lateral)**: Median Nerve, Brachial Artery, Biceps Tendon, Radial Nerve (MN–BA–BT–RN).
- **Biceps brachii** is the chief supinator of the forearm, not a pure flexor.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Brachial Plexus Organisation
The plexus lies in the posterior triangle and axilla. **Roots (C5–T1)** form three **trunks** behind the clavicle; each trunk splits into **anterior and posterior divisions**. Divisions re-organise into **cords** named for their position relative to the axillary artery.

| Cord | Major Terminal Branches | Key Collateral |
|------|------------------------|----------------|
| Lateral | Musculocutaneous + lateral root of median | Lateral pectoral nerve |
| Medial | Ulnar + medial root of median | Medial pectoral, medial cutaneous of arm/forearm |
| Posterior | Axillary + radial | Thoracodorsal (latissimus dorsi), upper/lower subscapular |

> **Trap:** The **long thoracic nerve** (C5, C6, C7) arises directly from roots — injury → winged scapula (paralysed serratus anterior).

#### Axillary Artery
The artery is crossed by pectoralis minor, giving it three parts:

- **Part 1** (proximal): superior thoracic artery.
- **Part 2** (deep to pectoralis minor): thoracoacromial and lateral thoracic.
- **Part 3** (distal, up to teres major): anterior and posterior circumflex humeral; the **posterior** one passes with the **axillary nerve** through the **quadrangular space**.

#### Shoulder Joint
A ball-and-socket joint with maximum mobility but minimum bony stability. Static restraints include the **glenoid labrum**, capsule, **coracohumeral ligament**, and three **glenohumeral ligaments** (superior, middle, inferior). The **inferior glenohumeral ligament** is the chief static block to anterior dislocation when the arm is abducted and externally rotated. Innervation is via **axillary and suprascapular nerves**; the **regimental badge area** over the lower deltoid is the axillary nerve's sensory signature.

#### Arm and Forearm Muscles
**Arm anterior compartment** (musculocutaneous nerve): biceps brachii (flexes elbow + **chief supinator**), brachialis (pure flexor to ulnar tuberosity), coracobrachialis. **Posterior compartment** (radial nerve): triceps brachii (long head also extends shoulder). **Forearm anterior compartment**: superficial group from the **common flexor origin** at the medial epicondyle (pronator teres, FCR, palmaris longus, FDS), deep group (FDP, FPL, pronator quadratus). **Posterior compartment** from the **common extensor origin** at the lateral epicondyle (ECRL/B, ED, EDM, ECU, supinator, APL, EPB, EPL, EI).

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Clinical Correlations and Edge Cases
**Fracture of the surgical neck of humerus** endangers the **axillary nerve** in the quadrangular space, producing deltoid paralysis and anaesthesia over the lower deltoid. **Mid-shaft humeral fractures** threaten the **radial nerve** in the spiral groove, causing wrist drop. **Pronator syndrome** arises from median nerve compression between the humeral and ulnar heads of pronator teres, mimicking carpal tunnel but sparing the thenar eminence's purely-innervated muscles.

#### Rotator Cuff vs Prime Movers
A frequent FMGE distractor is mixing prime movers with stabilisers. The **deltoid** (axillary nerve) and **pectoralis major** (medial + lateral pectoral nerves) are prime movers, **not** cuff members. **SITS** muscles act as dynamic ligaments: supraspinatus initiates the first 15° of abduction; the remaining 15–90° is deltoid; subscapularis medially rotates; infraspinatus and teres minor laterally rotate.

#### Cubital Fossa Micro-Example
> **MN–BA–BT–RN** (medial to lateral): Median nerve lies most medial, then the brachial artery, then the biceps brachii tendon centrally; the radial nerve lies **deep to brachioradialis** and therefore is not in the roof but along the lateral floor.

#### Two Practice Prompts
1. A newborn delivered after shoulder dystocia holds the arm adducted, internally rotated, forearm pronated, wrist flexed. Identify the lesion and list three muscles paralysed.
2. During axillary lymph node clearance, the posterior circumflex humeral artery is ligated. Which nerve is at risk, and what is the resulting motor and sensory deficit?
