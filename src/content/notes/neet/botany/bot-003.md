# Enzymes

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> One sentence to hold the whole unit: enzymes lower **activation energy (Ea)**; they never touch ΔG, and they emerge unchanged at the end of every catalytic cycle.

Enzymes are mostly **globular proteins** (the exceptions are catalytic RNAs called ribozymes). They bind substrate at the **active site**, form a transient **enzyme–substrate (ES) complex**, and release product with the free enzyme intact.

| Term | What it means | One-line hook |
|---|---|---|
| Apoenzyme | Protein part only (inactive on its own) | "Apo" = the bare scaffold |
| Cofactor | Non-protein helper (Zn²⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺) | Needed to switch the enzyme on |
| Coenzyme | Loosely bound organic cofactor (NAD⁺, FAD, CoA) | Carries electrons / groups away |
| Prosthetic group | Tightly / covalently bound cofactor (FAD, heme) | Stays attached through the reaction |
| Holoenzyme | Apoenzyme + cofactor = the active form | Apo + helper = Holo |

The Michaelis–Menten equation governs every graph you'll see:

$$v = \frac{V_{max}\,[S]}{K_m + [S]}$$

- **v** = initial velocity (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹)
- **Vmax** = maximum velocity (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹)
- **[S]** = substrate concentration (mol L⁻¹)
- **Km** = substrate concentration at which v = Vmax/2 (mol L⁻¹); lower Km = tighter binding

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook — "Apo needs a Co to become Holo":** **Apo**enzyme alone is dead in solution; add a **Co**factor (metal / coenzyme / prosthetic group) and you get the working **Holo**enzyme. Walk into the exam remembering that single line.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### The catalytic cycle, drawn in words

Substrate docks at the active site. The **lock-and-key model** (Fischer, 1894) treats the active site as a rigid template; **induced fit** (Koshland, 1958) replaces this — the binding site moulds around the substrate, straining bonds and excluding water. NEET accepts induced fit as the modern view.

The cycle runs:

$$E + S \rightleftharpoons ES \rightarrow EP \rightarrow E + P$$

The free enzyme **E** is regenerated, which is why one enzyme molecule turns over thousands of substrate molecules per second.

#### Competitive vs non-competitive inhibition

| Feature | Competitive | Non-competitive |
|---|---|---|
| Binds at | Active site | Allosteric site (separate) |
| Effect on Km | **Increases** apparent Km | **Unchanged** |
| Effect on Vmax | Unchanged | **Decreases** |
| Overcome by raising [S]? | Yes | No |
| Lineweaver–Burk slope | Steeper, same y-intercept | Same x-intercept, higher y-intercept |
| Classic example | Malonate vs succinate (succinate dehydrogenase) | Heavy-metal block of -SH groups |

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Many students write "non-competitive inhibition decreases Km." Wrong — it decreases **Vmax**. Only competitive inhibition raises apparent **Km** (because substrate must out-compete the inhibitor), while Vmax stays reachable at high [S].

#### Factors that move the rate curve

- **Temperature:** rate roughly doubles per 10 °C rise (Q10 ≈ 2) until the optimum near 35–40 °C, then drops sharply as the protein denatures.
- **pH:** bell-shaped around an enzyme-specific optimum. Pepsin ≈ 2, salivary amylase ≈ 6.8, trypsin ≈ 8, alkaline phosphatase ≈ 9–10. Extremes break ionic bonds in the tertiary structure.
- **[S]:** hyperbolic saturation (Michaelis–Menten).
- **[E]:** linear at saturating [S].

#### IUBMB classification (1964)

Six classes, each with an EC number:

1. **Oxidoreductases** — redox (EC 1)
2. **Transferases** — move functional groups (EC 2)
3. **Hydrolases** — cleave with water; esterases, peptidases (EC 3)
4. **Lyases** — add/remove groups without hydrolysis (EC 4)
5. **Isomerases** — geometric/structural rearrangements (EC 5)
6. **Ligases / Synthetases** — join using ATP (EC 6)

Example: **EC 3.1.1.7** = acetylcholinesterase. Memorise the first digit, the rest is recognition.

> 📌 **Formula Check:** When using the Arrhenius form k = A·e^(−Ea/RT), the gas constant **R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹** demands **T in Kelvin (K)**, not °C. Plugging 310 instead of 37 °C is a classic silent error.

#### Worked example — competitive inhibition on a Lineweaver–Burk plot

A student runs an enzyme assay without inhibitor: Vmax = 100 µmol L⁻¹ min⁻¹, Km = 0.5 mM. Adding a competitive inhibitor doubles the apparent Km to 1.0 mM while Vmax stays at 100.

1. **Without inhibitor:** slope = Km/Vmax = 0.5 / 100 = **0.005 mM·min µmol⁻¹**; y-intercept = 1/Vmax = **0.01 min µmol⁻¹**.
2. **With inhibitor:** slope = 1.0 / 100 = **0.010 mM·min µmol⁻¹**; y-intercept still **0.01 min µmol⁻¹**.
3. **x-intercept:** −1/Km = **−2.0 mM⁻¹** (no inhibitor) and **−1.0 mM⁻¹** (with inhibitor).
4. **Interpretation:** lines pivot on the **same y-intercept**; slope doubles. That geometry is the fingerprint of competitive inhibition.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students misread the graph and report that Vmax dropped because the lines "look further down." The y-intercept (1/Vmax) is the test, not the slope — and that intercept does not move under competitive inhibition.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Boundary cases the examiner can probe

- **Ribozymes break the "all enzymes are proteins" rule.** Catalytic RNA includes the hammerhead ribozyme, self-splicing Group I introns, ribonuclease P, and the **peptidyl transferase** activity of the large ribosomal subunit. Whenever the stem says "enzyme," look for whether the answer must read "ribozyme."
- **Catalytic efficiency ceiling:** kcat/Km cannot exceed the diffusion limit of ~10⁸–10⁹ M⁻¹ s⁻¹. Triose phosphate isomerase and acetylcholinesterase sit near this ceiling. A question offering an option above 10⁹ M⁻¹ s⁻¹ is wrong by physics, not by biology.
- **Turnover number, kcat:** defined as kcat = Vmax / [E]total, with units s⁻¹. Catalase runs near 4 × 10⁷ s⁻¹ — among the fastest known.
- **Isozymes** (lactate dehydrogenase LDH-1 to LDH-5) are different proteins catalysing the same reaction in different tissues; they confuse students who assume one gene → one enzyme.
- **ΔG is untouched.** Lowering Ea changes **how fast** equilibrium is reached, never **where** equilibrium lies. ΔG°' and Keq are properties of the reaction, not the enzyme.

#### Advanced trap list

1. **Saying enzymes "create" product.** They accelerate; they do not invent.
2. **Writing "Km = affinity" without the inverse sign.** High Km = low affinity.
3. **Mixing up allosteric with active site.** Non-competitive inhibitors bind **away** from the active site.
4. **Calling a coenzyme a prosthetic group.** Coenzymes leave the enzyme (NAD⁺ → NADH); prosthetic groups do not (FAD stays bound in succinate dehydrogenase).
5. **Reading a v vs [S] graph as showing Vmax at infinite [S].** It is approached asymptotically; Vmax is the asymptote.
6. **Ignoring that enzymes are also substrates at extreme pH.** Acid/alkali denature them by protonating/deprotonating side chains, breaking the tertiary fold.
7. **Forgetting ribozymes in the IUBMB classification question.** Ribozymes still get EC numbers (e.g., peptidyl transferase activity), but the "all enzymes are proteins" answer is what fails.

#### Practice prompts

1. A Lineweaver–Burk plot shows lines crossing to the **left of the y-axis on the x-axis** at the same x-intercept but different y-intercepts. Identify the inhibitor type, state which kinetic parameter changes, and predict whether raising [S] recovers Vmax.
2. An enzyme has kcat = 500 s⁻¹ and Km = 0.05 mM. Compute catalytic efficiency in M⁻¹ s⁻¹ and judge whether the enzyme is diffusion-limited. Convert Km to mol L⁻¹ first.

> 📌 **Exam Strategy:** In NEET, two Botany questions routinely come from this unit — usually one on the **ES complex / induced fit**, the other on **inhibition graphs or cofactor nomenclature**. Practising the Lineweaver–Burk geometry pays off disproportionately because the same diagram recycles across years.
