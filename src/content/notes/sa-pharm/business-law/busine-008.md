# Medicines & Related Substances Act (Act 101 of 1965)

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

The **Medicines and Related Substances Act 101 of 1965** (as amended by Act 90 of 1997 and Act 14 of 2015) is South Africa's principal statute controlling the **manufacture, distribution, sale, importation, advertisement and scheduling of medicines and scheduled substances**.

- The **regulator** is **SAHPRA** (South African Health Products Regulatory Authority), established under Act 72 of 2008; the older Medicines Control Council (MCC) no longer exists.
- **Section 18A** prohibits the sale of any **unregistered medicine** unless a **Section 21** named-patient/compassionate-use exemption is granted by SAHPRA.
- The Act is drafted as a **blanket prohibition with exceptions** — assume conduct is unlawful unless the Act specifically permits it.
- Exam trap: confusing the Medicines Act (which regulates **products**) with the **Pharmacy Act 53 of 1974** (which regulates the **profession and pharmacy premises**).

| Schedule | Access control | Who may sell/dispense |
|----------|----------------|----------------------|
| S1 | General sale | Any authorised outlet |
| S2 | Pharmacy (OTC) | Pharmacist / pharmacist's assistant (S1–S2 under supervision) |
| S3 | Pharmacist-initiated therapy | Pharmacist |
| S4 | Prescription-only (POM) | Authorised prescriber + pharmacist dispense |
| S5 | Psychotropic, tightly controlled | Authorised prescriber + licensed pharmacist |
| S6 | Narcotic, most restrictive | Authorised prescriber + locked storage |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Purpose and Structure
The Act creates a single national framework for regulating **medicines, medical devices and scheduled substances**. Its underlying logic is **prohibition first, permission second**: every act (manufacture, possession, sale, import, export, advertise) is forbidden unless a specific provision or exemption allows it. SAHPRA administers the Act and maintains the register of approved medicines.

#### The Scheduling System (Section 22A)
**Section 22A** empowers the **Minister of Health**, on SAHPRA's recommendation, to place any substance into a **Schedule (S1–S6)** by notice in the Government Gazette. Scheduling determines who may lawfully possess and supply the substance. **Section 22C** then makes it an offence to sell a scheduled substance to anyone other than an *authorised person* (pharmacist, authorised prescriber, licensed manufacturer or wholesaler, or the end user via the correct channel).

#### Selling and Dispensing — Authorised Persons
A **pharmacist** is the central authorised figure. A **pharmacist's assistant** may handle **S1 and S2** transactions under the personal supervision of a pharmacist (codified in the *Pharmacy Act* and *Bonitas* case-law principle: institutional pharmacies must be under the **direct personal supervision of a responsible pharmacist**).

#### Registration and the Section 21 Exemption
**Section 18A** prohibits the sale of any **unregistered medicine**. The narrow **Section 21** authority allows SAHPRA to authorise a **named patient** or **compassionate-use** supply on application — it is **not a general parallel-import licence**. Parallel importation is governed separately by **Section 35A**.

#### Common Exam Scenarios
- An MCQ asking which schedule requires a *prescription* (S4 and above require an authorised prescriber).
- A scenario: trainee sells S4 without a prescription — identify the **Section 22C** offence and who bears liability.
- Distinguish: medicines *registration* (Medicines Act) from pharmacy *licensing* (Pharmacy Act).

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Interfaces
The Act does not stand alone. **Pricing** is governed by the **Single Exit Price (SEP) regulations**; **pharmacy premises and personnel** by the **Pharmacy Act 53 of 1974**; **professional conduct** by the South African Pharmacy Council (SAPC). Confusing these in an essay costs marks.

#### Strict-Liability Nature of Offences
Once the scheduled status of a substance is established, liability for unauthorised **sale, supply or possession** is largely **strict** — the prosecution need not prove *mens rea* for the Schedule offence itself, only the actus reus. This shifts practical risk onto pharmacists, who must verify each transaction's authority.

#### Common Mistakes to Avoid
- Citing the **MCC** as the current regulator — it was replaced by **SAHPRA** in 2008 (Act 72 of 2008); the Medicines Act was **amended, not repealed**.
- Treating a **Section 21** authorisation as a "right" — it is discretionary, named-patient, and granted per application.
- Assuming S4 sales can be verbal — a **valid written prescription** from a medical practitioner, dentist, veterinarian or other authorised prescriber is required.
- Ignoring **Regulation 11** record-keeping: prescriptions must be retained for **5 years**.

#### Worked Scenario (Practice)
> A community pharmacist sells a strip of Schedule 2 codeine tablets to a walk-in customer without keeping a record. Is there an offence?

The sale itself is **lawful** (S2 = pharmacist-supervised OTC), but failure to record breaches **Regulation 11** record-keeping. Compare with selling an S5 substance without a prescription — that is a **Section 22C** offence regardless of the pharmacist's knowledge.

#### Practice Prompts
1. Explain why the Act is structured as a blanket prohibition with exceptions, and how this affects interpreting an S4 sale scenario.
2. Distinguish the role of SAHPRA from that of the SAPC, and identify which body enforces a Section 22C offence versus a breach of the *Pharmacy Act*.
