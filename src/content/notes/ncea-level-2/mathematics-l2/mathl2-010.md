# Conduct an experiment to investigate a situation using statistical methods (91265) — NCEA Level 2 Mathematics Notes

Achievement Standard 91265 is an internal standard (3 credits) requiring students to plan and carry out an experiment, manage data, select and apply appropriate statistical methods, and report on findings. It pairs with 91264 (inference) and is the practical-experiment companion to the 91944 statistical enquiry process at Level 1. The Level 2 version emphasises experimental design (control, replication, randomisation) more than the Level 1 version.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **3 credits**, internally assessed by the school, NZQA moderated.
- Topics: experimental design (independent vs dependent variable, control, replication, randomisation, blocking), data collection, summary statistics, displays, inference, conclusions in context.
- **Merit** requires relational thinking — selecting the right experimental design for the question.
- **Excellence** requires extended abstract thinking — justifying design choices, reflecting on the validity of the conclusions.

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing the independent and dependent variables.
- Not controlling for confounding variables (e.g., testing a fertiliser on plant growth without controlling for sunlight).
- Reporting conclusions without linking back to the experimental context.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Experimental design

**Independent variable** — the variable the experimenter manipulates. e.g., the type of fertiliser applied.

**Dependent variable** — the variable measured to see the effect. e.g., plant height after 4 weeks.

**Controlled variables** — variables held constant so they cannot affect the dependent variable. e.g., same amount of water, same amount of sunlight, same soil type, same plant species, same initial plant height.

**Replication** — repeating each treatment multiple times to estimate the variability and reduce the effect of random variation. Aim for at least 5 replicates per treatment.

**Randomisation** — assigning treatments to experimental units at random, to avoid systematic bias.

**Blocking** — grouping similar experimental units together to control for known sources of variability (e.g., all plants in one tray get the same lighting).

#### Conducting the experiment

1. **Plan.** Write a clear plan with the variables, the controls, the number of replicates, and the randomisation scheme.
2. **Pilot test.** If feasible, do a small pilot test to identify issues with the protocol.
3. **Carry out.** Follow the plan, recording data carefully.
4. **Manage data.** Check for errors, missing values, outliers. Tabulate the data.
5. **Analyse.** Calculate summary statistics for each treatment, display the data (box plot, bar chart with error bars, scatter plot), and test for differences (Level 2: informal comparison; Level 3: formal inference).
6. **Report.** Summarise findings in a written report with reference to the experimental context.

#### Selecting statistical methods

The choice of summary statistics and displays depends on the data:

- **Categorical:** frequency table, bar chart, mode.
- **Numerical (one group):** mean, median, range, IQR, standard deviation; histogram, box plot, dot plot.
- **Numerical (two or more groups):** compare means, medians, spreads; parallel box plots, bar chart with error bars.
- **Numerical (paired):** calculate the difference for each pair, then analyse as a single group.

#### Informal inference

At Level 2, compare the treatments by eye and by descriptive statistics:

- "Treatment A had a higher mean (24.3) than Treatment B (19.7), but the spread was also larger (SD 4.2 vs 2.1). With only 5 replicates per treatment, the difference might be due to chance; a formal test would be needed to confirm."

The move to formal inference (confidence intervals, hypothesis tests) happens in Level 3 standard 91582.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Why replication matters

A single replicate gives one data point per treatment. The result is one observation — we cannot estimate variability, cannot check for outliers, and cannot make a reliable inference. Multiple replicates let us estimate the variability and check whether observed differences are likely to be real.

Aim for at least 5 replicates per treatment at Level 2; more is better. The standard error of the mean decreases with √n (e.g., 4 replicates gives SE = σ/2; 25 replicates gives SE = σ/5).

#### Why randomisation matters

Without randomisation, the experimenter might (consciously or unconsciously) assign the "best" plants to one treatment and the "worst" plants to the other. This systematic bias is not removed by replication. Randomisation equalises the effect of all unknown variables across treatments.

#### Why blocking matters

If there is a known source of variability (e.g., one side of the greenhouse gets more sunlight), blocking groups the experimental units by that source so the variation is captured within blocks rather than between treatments.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Replication means doing the experiment once." No — replication means repeating each treatment multiple times.
- "Randomisation means no pattern." No — randomisation removes systematic patterns, not the random variation.
- "Bigger differences mean more reliable." Not necessarily — a large difference with high variability might be due to chance; a small difference with low variability might be reliable.
- "I can change the design mid-experiment." No — pre-register the design to avoid p-hacking and confirmation bias.
