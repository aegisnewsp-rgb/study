# Recognising assumptions and flaws in UCAT arguments

Decision Making includes short argument-evaluation items where a passage makes a claim and you have to identify the unstated premise it relies on, or the step in the reasoning that does not follow. The UCAT does not call these items by formal names — there is no question that says "spot the straw man" — but the wording of the answer choices tests the same skill. The candidate who can name the flaw recognises it faster, and the candidate who can spot the unstated premise recognises when the conclusion is unsupported.

> Source for subtest size, time, marking and scoring (35 items / 37 minutes / 300–900 / single-answer 1 mark, multiple-statement 2 marks with partial credit): UCAT Consortium, *Test Format*, https://www.ucat.ac.uk/about-ucat/test-format/. The UCAT Decision Making question tutorial covers the same item types this note builds on.

## What an "assumption" actually is in this subtest

An assumption is the unstated link between a premise and a conclusion. The argument depends on it being true. If the assumption is false, the conclusion does not necessarily follow.

Three common shapes:

- **Hidden universal.** The premise says "Most patients improved". The conclusion says "The treatment works for everyone with this condition". The hidden assumption is that "most" generalises to "all".
- **Hidden comparator.** The premise says "Drug A was better than Drug B in this trial". The conclusion says "Drug A is better than Drug B in general". The hidden assumption is that the trial population represents the wider population.
- **Hidden cause.** The premise says "After the new triage system was introduced, wait times fell". The conclusion says "The new triage system caused the fall in wait times". The hidden assumption is that nothing else changed over the same period.

In each case, the conclusion is not necessarily wrong. The argument is just incomplete. The Decision Making item rewards the candidate who can point to the gap.

## The five flaws that come up most often

The UCAT does not name the flaws in the items, but the wrong-answer patterns test a small set. The five that come up most often are:

- **False dilemma.** The argument presents two options as if they are the only ones, when in fact a third or fourth is plausible. The phrase to look for is "either X or Y" when Z is also possible.
- **Post hoc ergo propter hoc.** The argument assumes that because B followed A, A caused B. Look for any argument that says "after X happened, Y happened, so X caused Y".
- **Ad hominem.** The argument attacks the person making the claim rather than the claim itself. Look for any argument that says "the speaker is unreliable, so the claim is wrong".
- **Straw man.** The argument misrepresents the opposing view and then refutes the misrepresentation. Look for any argument that says "they claim X, but X is wrong", where the X described is not actually the opposing view.
- **Begging the question.** The conclusion is smuggled into the premise. Look for any argument where the premise already assumes what the conclusion is trying to prove.

These five cover most of the wrong-answer traps in argument-evaluation items. There are others (slippery slope, appeal to authority, appeal to popularity), but they appear less often.

## A four-question checklist for any argument

When you read a Decision Making argument, run it through four checks before you pick a conclusion:

1. **What is the conclusion?** Identify it in one sentence. If you cannot, the argument is unclear and you should flag the item.
2. **What is the evidence?** Identify the data, the example or the analogy the argument uses. If there is none, the argument is asserting without evidence and the conclusion is unsupported.
3. **What is the hidden assumption?** Ask: what would have to be true for the conclusion to follow from the evidence? If the answer is something not stated, that is the assumption.
4. **What is the strongest objection?** Identify the most reasonable counter-argument. If the conclusion cannot answer it, the conclusion is weaker than the argument suggests.

If the conclusion survives all four checks, it is the best-supported answer. If it fails one, pick the conclusion that does survive.

## Worked example — spotting the hidden assumption

Argument:

> A primary care network introduced a same-day telephone triage system in January. By April, the average time from booking to clinician contact had fallen from 5 days to 1.8 days, and patient satisfaction scores had risen from 72% to 84%. The network plans to roll the same system out to two more practices next year.

Which assumption does the argument rely on?

- A. Patient satisfaction scores are an accurate measure of care quality. — Plausible but not the load-bearing assumption for the rollout claim.
- B. The same-day triage system caused the improvements in wait time and satisfaction. — *Yes.* The argument moves from "after X, Y improved" to "we should roll X out further". That move depends on X having caused Y. If something else changed over the same period — a new funding stream, a staffing restructure, a seasonal drop in demand — the cause attribution is unsupported.
- C. Telephone triage is cheaper than face-to-face triage. — Not in the passage.
- D. The two new practices have similar patient populations to the pilot. — Also an assumption, but a secondary one. The argument assumes the result will generalise, but the primary assumption (the one the rollout plan rests on most heavily) is the causal claim.

The best answer is B. The reasoning chain is: "we observed improvement" → "the system caused it" → "roll it out elsewhere". The middle link is the hidden assumption.

## Worked example — spotting a false dilemma

Argument:

> To reduce pressure on A&E, the trust must either extend opening hours at urgent treatment centres or open a new minor injuries unit. Extending urgent treatment centre hours costs less, so we should extend those hours.

Which flaw does the argument contain?

- A. The argument assumes that only urgent treatment centres can reduce A&E pressure.
- B. The argument assumes that extending opening hours is the cheapest way to reduce A&E pressure.
- C. The argument assumes that the trust has the staff to extend urgent treatment centre hours.
- D. The argument assumes that A&E pressure should be reduced.

The best answer is A. The argument presents two options ("either extend hours or open a new unit") as if they are the only ways to reduce A&E pressure. Other plausible options — same-day GP appointments, community outreach, ambulance redirection — are not in the dilemma. That is the false-dilemma pattern, and the trap is that the cost comparison inside the dilemma looks reasonable, so the reader accepts the framing.

## Worked example — spotting post hoc reasoning

Argument:

> The new electronic prescribing system was introduced in March. In April, the number of prescribing errors fell from 9 to 4. The system has clearly reduced errors.

Which flaw does the argument contain?

- A. It assumes one month is enough data to judge a trend.
- B. It assumes the system caused the reduction.
- C. It assumes that all prescribing errors are equally serious.
- D. It assumes that prescribing errors are an important measure of care quality.

The best answer is B. The argument observes that B followed A and concludes that A caused B. That is the post hoc fallacy. The other answers may also be true, but B is the load-bearing flaw.

## Common mistakes to correct now

- Naming the flaw without explaining the move. The UCAT scores the match between the chosen answer and the flaw, not the label. If you cannot say in one sentence why the argument fails, you have not identified the flaw.
- Treating "the data does not say that" as a flaw. The data not addressing a point is not a flaw; it is a limitation. The UCAT scores flaws in the reasoning chain, not gaps in the data.
- Skipping argument-evaluation items because they look subjective. They are subjective only on a careless read. The four-question checklist above makes them mechanical.
- Reading only the conclusion and guessing. The conclusion is the part that sounds reasonable. The premise and the hidden assumption are where the error lives.

## What to do next

- Pick one Decision Making argument from the official UCAT practice tests. Write down the conclusion, the evidence, the hidden assumption and the strongest objection in one paragraph each.
- Build a one-page reference card listing the five flaws above (false dilemma, post hoc, ad hominem, straw man, begging the question) with one example sentence for each. Review the card before each Decision Making drill.
- After each drill, classify every wrong argument-evaluation answer as "missed the hidden assumption" or "missed the named flaw". The category tells you what to drill next.
- Take one argument from a newspaper editorial and run it through the four-question checklist. The point is to make the checklist automatic, not to agree with the editorial.
