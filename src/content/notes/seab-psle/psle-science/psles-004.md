# Energy — PSLE Science Notes

Energy is the combined Primary 5–6 theme that runs through the MOE Primary Science syllabus. At Primary 5, the strand covers forms of energy, sources of energy, and energy conversion. At Primary 6, the strand extends into electrical energy: conductors, insulators, simple circuits, and the electromagnet. The strand is assessed through both multiple-choice items on Paper 1 and structured questions on Paper 2, and is a common OEQ topic.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### What every Primary 6 student must know

1. **Forms of energy**: heat, light, sound, electrical, kinetic, chemical, elastic potential, gravitational potential.
2. **Sources of energy**: the Sun (renewable), fuels (coal, oil, gas — non-renewable), wind, water, biomass (renewable).
3. **Energy conversion**: energy can be converted from one form to another but cannot be created or destroyed.
4. **Electrical conductors**: materials that allow electricity to flow through them (most metals).
5. **Electrical insulators**: materials that do not allow electricity to flow through them (plastic, rubber, wood).
6. **Series circuit**: a single loop; the same current flows through every component; if one component breaks, all components stop working.
7. **Parallel circuit**: components are connected across the same two points; each component has its own path; if one component breaks, the others keep working.

#### Key terms

- **Renewable energy source**: an energy source that can be replenished naturally (e.g. sunlight, wind).
- **Non-renewable energy source**: an energy source that will run out because it cannot be replenished on a human timescale (e.g. coal, oil, natural gas).
- **Energy conversion**: the change of energy from one form to another (e.g. chemical energy in a battery → electrical energy in the circuit → light and heat energy in a bulb).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Forms and sources of energy

Energy exists in many forms:

- **Heat (thermal) energy** — energy due to the temperature of an object. A cup of hot tea has more heat energy than the same cup of cold tea.
- **Light energy** — energy carried by light. The Sun is the main natural source of light energy on Earth.
- **Sound energy** — energy carried by sound waves. Produced by vibrating objects.
- **Electrical energy** — energy carried by an electric current. Produced by generators, batteries, solar cells.
- **Kinetic energy** — energy of motion. A moving object has kinetic energy.
- **Chemical energy** — energy stored in chemicals (food, fuel, batteries). Released in chemical reactions.
- **Elastic potential energy** — energy stored in a stretched or compressed object (e.g. a stretched rubber band).
- **Gravitational potential energy** — energy stored in an object raised above the ground.

Energy can be converted from one form to another. Examples:

- A moving ball has kinetic energy; when it hits the ground, the kinetic energy converts to heat and sound.
- A battery stores chemical energy; in a torch, it converts to electrical energy, then to light and heat energy in the bulb.
- A stretched rubber band stores elastic potential energy; when released, this converts to kinetic energy.

Energy cannot be created or destroyed — this is the law of conservation of energy.

#### Renewable and non-renewable sources

**Renewable** sources can be replenished on a human timescale. The Sun provides sunlight, which drives wind (through uneven heating of the Earth's surface) and the water cycle (through evaporation). Biomass (plant material and animal waste) can be burned for energy or converted to biofuels. Geothermal energy comes from the heat inside the Earth.

**Non-renewable** sources are finite — coal, oil and natural gas took millions of years to form and will run out if used at the current rate. Nuclear energy (uranium) is technically non-renewable on a human timescale, though nuclear fission releases a huge amount of energy per kilogram of fuel.

#### Electrical conductors and insulators

A **conductor** allows electricity to flow through it. Most metals are good conductors — copper is the most commonly used in household wiring.

An **insulator** does not allow electricity to flow through it. Plastic, rubber, wood, glass and dry cloth are common insulators. Insulators are used to coat the outside of electrical wires so a person touching the wire does not get an electric shock.

Materials can be tested for conductivity with a simple circuit: a battery, a bulb and a gap into which the test material is placed. If the bulb lights up, the material is a conductor; if it does not, the material is an insulator.

#### Simple circuits

**Series circuit**: components are connected end-to-end in a single loop. The same current flows through every component. If one component breaks (e.g. a bulb blows), the circuit is broken and all components stop working. Adding more bulbs in series makes each bulb dimmer because the same current has to drive more bulbs.

**Parallel circuit**: components are connected across the same two points of the circuit. Each component has its own path for the current. If one component breaks, the others keep working. Adding more branches does not make the remaining bulbs dimmer, because the current through each branch is independent.

In a series circuit, the current is the same at every point. In a parallel circuit, the total current is the sum of the currents in each branch.

#### Electromagnet

An electromagnet is a coil of wire wrapped around an iron core; when current flows through the wire, the iron core becomes magnetised. The strength of the electromagnet depends on:

- The number of turns in the coil (more turns → stronger magnet).
- The current through the coil (more current → stronger magnet).
- The presence of a soft-iron core (soft iron makes the magnet much stronger than air alone).

The electromagnet is a temporary magnet — it is only magnetic while the current is flowing. When the current is switched off, the magnetism is lost.

Electromagnets are used in electric bells, scrap-metal cranes, circuit breakers and relays.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Where Energy appears across the paper

- **PSLE Science Paper 1**: multiple-choice items on forms of energy, renewable vs non-renewable sources, conductors vs insulators, and the behaviour of series and parallel circuits.
- **PSLE Science Paper 2**: structured questions on energy conversion chains, the variables that affect the strength of an electromagnet, and the differences between series and parallel circuits in practical situations (e.g. household wiring).
- **Across both papers**: drawing a clear energy-conversion chain ("chemical → electrical → light + heat") is the highest-leverage exam technique for energy questions.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Energy is used up." Energy is converted, not used up. The total amount of energy in a closed system is conserved.
- "Renewable energy is free." Renewable sources are replenished but the technology to capture them is not free; the choice between sources is also a choice between different land-use and cost profiles.
- "All metals are conductors." Most metals are conductors, but the conductivity varies widely. Copper is the standard for household wiring because of its high conductivity and reasonable cost.
- "Series circuits are always used in homes." Most household wiring is in parallel, because parallel wiring allows each appliance to be switched on and off independently.
- "A stronger current makes a brighter bulb." A stronger current makes a brighter bulb in the same circuit, but comparing across circuits, the brightness depends on both current and voltage.

#### Exam technique

- For energy-conversion questions, write the chain explicitly: "from [form 1] to [form 2] to [form 3]…". The marker can then award the method mark even if the chain is incomplete.
- For "renewable or non-renewable" questions, write the classification AND the reason. "Renewable because the Sun provides a continuous supply" is the format that earns the explanation mark.
- For circuit questions, draw the circuit diagram with clear labels for every component. The diagram is the method mark.
- For electromagnet questions, state the variables that affect strength, and the direction of the effect (more turns → stronger, more current → stronger, soft-iron core → stronger).
