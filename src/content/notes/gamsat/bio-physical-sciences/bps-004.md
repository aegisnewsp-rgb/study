# Human Physiology Systems

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

ACER describes the Biological and Physical Sciences section as 75 multiple-choice questions in 150 minutes that assess the ability "to identify knowledge in new contexts, and analyse and interpret data", and it sets the knowledge ceiling at the biology and chemistry that "generally corresponds to the first year of university studies". So this topic is not a list of facts to memorise: it is a set of systems you read from a trace, a curve or a diagram.

ACER publishes no per-topic weightage for this section, so the split into circulation, gas exchange, renal function, nerve signalling and endocrine control is StudyRoadmap's own study organisation.

**The five variables worth tracking.** Every question in this topic reduces to a number moving out of range.

| System | Variable to track | Reference point | What a deviation tells you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Circulation | Heart rate and stroke volume | CO = HR × SV; output falls if either falls | Whether a low blood pressure is a pump problem or a volume problem |
| Gas exchange | Partial pressures and saturation | PaCO₂ near 40 mmHg; Hb saturation tracks PaO₂ | Whether the lung is failing to ventilate or failing to exchange |
| Kidney | Glomerular filtration rate and clearance | GFR is roughly 125 mL/min in a young adult | Whether low urine output is perfusion or tubular injury |
| Nerve | Membrane potential and transmission | Resting potential near −70 mV | Where along the axon or the synapse a process failed |
| Endocrine | Concentration of the regulated variable | Feedback keeps the variable within a band | Which limb of the feedback loop has failed |

**The cardiac cycle in nine words.** Atrial systole tops up the ventricles; isovolumetric contraction closes both valves and raises pressure; ejection sends blood into the arteries; isovolumetric relaxation opens the aortic and pulmonary valves; then filling happens passively. The first heart sound belongs to valve closure at the start of systole, the second to valve closure at the start of diastole.

| ECG component | What it actually records | The trap |
|---|---|---|
| P wave | Atrial depolarisation | It is not atrial contraction, which follows it |
| PR interval | Time from atrial depolarisation to ventricular depolarisation | Prolonged PR means delayed conduction, not a bigger P wave |
| QRS complex | Ventricular depolarisation | Atrial repolarisation is buried inside it |
| T wave | Ventricular repolarisation | It is not the end of ejection |

**Gas exchange in five lines.** Oxygen is carried almost entirely bound to haemoglobin, because only a tiny amount dissolves in plasma. Carbon dioxide travels mainly as bicarbonate, which is why a CO₂ change shifts pH and an oxygen change does not. The amount of oxygen in the blood is driven by haemoglobin concentration times saturation, plus a small dissolved contribution. The lungs work only if ventilation matches perfusion.

**The kidney in five lines.** Glomerular filtration is driven by a net filtration pressure, and the tubule reclaims what the body needs. Clearance is the volume of plasma cleared of a substance per minute, calculated as urine concentration times urine flow rate divided by plasma concentration. A substance filtered but not reabsorbed or secreted has a clearance equal to GFR.

**Nerve signalling in five lines.** The sodium-potassium pump keeps the resting membrane potential negative inside. A threshold opens voltage-gated sodium channels and the action potential runs forward. At the synapse, calcium entry triggers transmitter release, the transmitter binds a receptor, and the response is either excitatory or inhibitory depending on the ion the receptor opens.

**Endocrine in five lines.** Most hormone systems run on negative feedback: the hormone lowers the variable it regulates, and that lowers further release. A few run on positive feedback: oxytocin in labour and the LH surge are the standard examples. The pituitary sits at the centre of several cascades, and the hypothalamus supplies the releasing hormones that drive it.

**Memorise for the Lite tier:**
- CO = HR × SV; MAP ≈ DBP + ⅓ pulse pressure.
- S1 and S2 are valve-closure sounds, not contraction and relaxation.
- The QRS complex hides atrial repolarisation.
- Oxygen content ≈ 1.34 × Hb × saturation + 0.003 × PaO₂.
- Dead space is ventilated but unperfused; shunt is perfused but unventilated.
- Clearance = (urine concentration × urine flow) ÷ plasma concentration.
- Resting membrane potential about −70 mV; threshold about −55 mV.
- Voltage-gated sodium channels open on depolarisation; potassium channels open on repolarisation.
- The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase moves 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in.
- ADH is made in the hypothalamus and released from the posterior pituitary.
- Insulin lowers blood glucose and blood potassium; glucagon does the reverse for glucose.

### 🟡 Standard — Full Coverage (1–2d)

#### Worked example: reading an ECG strip

You are handed a strip with regular QRS complexes. The R waves are separated by 4 large boxes at the standard paper speed of 25 mm per second.

**Rate.** One large box is 5 mm, which at 25 mm/s is 0.2 s. Four large boxes is 0.8 s, so the rate is 60 ÷ 0.8 = 75 beats per minute. The shortcut is 300 divided by the number of large boxes between R waves, which gives 300 ÷ 4 = 75.

**Rhythm.** The R-R interval is constant across the strip, so the rhythm is regular. A regular rhythm with a rate of 75 and a P wave before every QRS is sinus rhythm. If the P waves were absent and the baseline showed sawtooth flutter waves, the rate would still be regular but the origin would be wrong.

**Interval.** If the PR interval spans 7 small boxes, that is 0.28 s — normal. At 10 small boxes it is 0.40 s, which is a prolonged PR interval and first-degree heart block, because the impulse is taking longer than normal to reach the ventricles.

**What the stem is really testing.** An ECG identifies rhythm and conduction, not contractility. A patient with a very low cardiac output can have a normal ECG, and a patient with atrial fibrillation can have an adequate output if the rate is controlled.

#### Worked example: reading a spirometry trace

The trace gives tidal volume, inspiratory and expiratory reserve volumes, and residual volume. For a typical adult male, roughly: tidal volume 500 mL, inspiratory reserve 3000 mL, expiratory reserve 1100 mL, residual volume 1200 mL. Vital capacity is therefore about 4600 mL, functional residual capacity about 2300 mL and total lung capacity about 5800 mL. These are averages for a healthy adult of average size, not constants.

**Now the flow-volume loop.** Two shapes matter.

An obstructive pattern, from asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, shows a scooped-out expiratory limb because the narrowed airways collapse during forced expiration, and the ratio of forced expiratory volume in the first second to forced vital capacity (FEV₁/FVC) falls, typically below 0.70. Residual volume rises and total lung capacity stays normal or falls only slightly.

A restrictive pattern, from pulmonary fibrosis or chest wall disease, shows a narrow loop with reduced volumes but a preserved or raised ratio, because the lungs are stiff and cannot expand, while the airways themselves are fine.

The pattern that catches people out is a reduced FVC with a normal ratio: that is restriction, not obstruction. And a patient with both has a low ratio and a low total lung capacity.

#### Worked example: a clearance calculation

Urine flow is 1.0 mL per minute. Plasma creatinine is 1.0 mg/dL and urine creatinine is 100 mg/dL.

Clearance = (100 mg/dL × 1.0 mL/min) ÷ 1.0 mg/dL = 100 mL/min.

Now interpret it. The clearance works out at 100 mL/min, below the usual GFR of about 125 mL/min. Creatinine is in fact both filtered and slightly secreted, so in a real patient a clearance that sits below GFR points to incomplete urine collection or some tubular reabsorption, and in practice clinicians use the ratio of urine to plasma creatinine to make this estimate. The examinable principle is that inulin gives a clearance equal to GFR, a substance that is both filtered and secreted (such as PAH) gives a clearance above GFR, and one that is reabsorbed (such as glucose or urea) gives a clearance below GFR.

#### Worked example: a hormone feedback diagram

A diagram shows blood glucose rising, pancreatic beta cells detecting it, insulin secretion rising, and glucose falling back down, with the falling glucose reducing insulin release.

**Trace it as a loop:** variable rises → sensor detects it → control centre responds → effector hormone rises → variable falls → sensor sees the correction → hormone falls.

**Where it fails, and what the failure looks like.** If the beta cells are destroyed, insulin never rises, the loop cannot close, and glucose stays high with ketones accumulating. If a pancreatic tumour secretes insulin autonomously, the loop is broken in the other direction, and glucose falls to a dangerously low level with hypoglycaemic symptoms. If the target cell has lost its insulin receptor, insulin rises but the target cell cannot respond, so glucose stays high despite high insulin — the picture of type 2 diabetes.

**The same diagram for the thyroid axis** works the same way: hypothalamic TRH, pituitary TSH, thyroid T4 and T3, with T3 feeding back to suppress both TRH and TSH. A pituitary tumour secreting TSH escapes the feedback loop because the tumour does not respect it, so TSH and T4 are both high. A thyroid that cannot make hormone produces low T4 with high TSH.

#### Synaptic transmission, step by step

A question asking "which step is blocked" needs the sequence in order.

1. An action potential reaches the axon terminal.
2. Voltage-gated calcium channels open and calcium enters the presynaptic terminal.
3. Calcium triggers synaptic vesicles to fuse with the membrane and release transmitter.
4. Transmitter diffuses across the synaptic cleft.
5. The transmitter binds a receptor on the postsynaptic membrane.
6. The receptor opens or closes an ion channel, giving an excitatory or inhibitory postsynaptic potential.
7. The transmitter is removed by reuptake, enzymatic degradation or diffusion.

Each step has its own familiar toxin, which is how these questions are usually phrased: botulinum toxin blocks the release step, tetrodotoxin blocks voltage-gated sodium channels, curare blocks the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor at the neuromuscular junction, and organophosphates inhibit acetylcholinesterase so the transmitter keeps firing the muscle.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (3–5d)

#### Integrating the systems in one patient

A young patient has a blood pressure of 80/50, a heart rate of 130, cold extremities, and urine output of 10 mL per hour. Work it system by system, because that is how the question is built.

The cardiovascular system is defending perfusion: the low blood pressure is detected by the baroreceptors, which fire less often, so sympathetic outflow rises and the heart rate climbs while vasoconstriction tightens the skin and kidney vessels, giving cold extremities. The respiratory system is not the problem yet; tachypnoea may appear as compensation for metabolic acidosis.

The kidney is the first casualty of that vasoconstriction, because renal blood flow is autoregulated only within a range. Once perfusion pressure falls below that range, the afferent arteriole dilates as far as it can and then GFR falls, which is why oliguria appears early.

The endocrine system reacts in parallel: the low effective arterial volume triggers renin release from the juxtaglomerular cells, renin converts angiotensinogen to angiotensin I, ACE converts that to angiotensin II, and angiotensin II constricts vessels, stimulates aldosterone for sodium and water retention, stimulates ADH for water retention, and produces thirst.

Now the second case: the same patient, but with warm extremities and distended neck veins. The volume is present and the pump is failing. The baroreceptor response still raises the heart rate, but the failing ventricle cannot increase stroke volume, and the output falls anyway. The kidneys are now congested rather than under-perfused, and the fluid that the renin-angiotensin system has retained cannot leave the circulation.

#### Common mistakes

Calling the ventricular contraction "ventricular systole" when the question is describing the period of ejection — systole is the whole contraction phase, not only the ejection phase.

Confusing the P wave with atrial contraction. The P wave is electrical depolarisation; the contraction follows it mechanically.

Assuming ventilation and perfusion mismatch lowers PaO₂ but leaves PaCO₂ alone. In a shunt, CO₂ is usually near normal because the remaining ventilated lung can compensate by over-ventilating, which is why a patient with pneumonia can have a low PaO₂ with a normal PaCO₂.

Saying the posterior pituitary makes ADH. The supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus synthesise ADH, and the posterior pituitary stores and releases it.

Assuming insulin raises blood potassium. Insulin drives potassium into cells, so insulin lowers serum potassium; that is why an insulin infusion can cause hypokalaemia and why DKA presents with hyperkalaemia despite total body potassium depletion.

Thinking nerve gases and botulinum toxin do the same thing. Organophosphates cause excess acetylcholine at the synapse, while botulinum toxin causes too little.

#### Exam traps

The absolute refractory period exists because voltage-gated sodium channels cannot reopen while inactivated. During it, no stimulus, however strong, produces another action potential.

Saltatory conduction depends on myelin and the nodes of Ranvier; the speed of conduction in a fibre depends on diameter and myelination, and this is why demyelination slows conduction and produces a conduction block.

Insulin and glucagon have opposite effects on the liver but not on muscle glycogen, because muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase and cannot release free glucose into blood.

Aldosterone increases sodium reabsorption and potassium secretion in the collecting duct, so an excess causes hypertension with hypokalaemia and metabolic alkalosis, and a deficiency causes hypotension with hyperkalaemia and metabolic acidosis.

A heart sound heard early in systole that follows a click, or a murmur that changes with position, points to valve mechanics rather than to a rhythm problem — position changes alter the geometry of the heart and change which murmurs are audible.
