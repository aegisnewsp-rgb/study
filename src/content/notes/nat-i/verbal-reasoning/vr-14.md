# Inference and Conclusion

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

An **inference** is a logical judgement that the passage *supports without saying outright*; a **conclusion** is the most defensible overall outcome that *must* follow from the stated premises. On NAT-I (NTS), Verbal Reasoning carries roughly 4% weight, and these items arrive as a 3–6 line passage followed by four or five candidate statements where exactly one is **must-be-true**.

- **Inference** = combining two or more stated facts to derive what is implied.
- **Conclusion** = the strongest, most reasonable outcome the entire argument forces.
- **Assumption** = an unstated premise the argument secretly needs to work.
- **Stem cues to watch:** *"it can be inferred that…"*, *"which conclusion follows…"*, *"the passage supports…"*

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"I-C-A = Implied, Certain, Assumed"** — Inference = Implied, Conclusion = Certain main-point, Assumption = Assumed unstated premise. Test each option against the **must-be-true ruler**: if *any* passage line can defeat it, drop it.

| Term | Stated in passage? | Strength demanded on NAT-I |
|---|---|---|
| Inference | No, but supported | Definitely or probably true |
| Conclusion | No, derived as main point | Must be true |
| Assumption | No, hidden premise | Must be true and unstated |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Definitions and the Logical Chain

An argument in NAT-I Verbal Reasoning has three layers. The **premises** are the explicit sentences the writer hands you. The **assumption** is the invisible bridge that lets those premises carry weight. The **conclusion** is the destination the writer drives you toward, while **inferences** are side-journeys the premises license even though the writer never mentions them.

The crucial move is the **must-be-true test**: read every premise, mentally negate each candidate, and reject any option that breaks. If the passage says "Most students prefer online classes", an answer claiming "All students prefer online classes" fails because *most* permits dissent.

#### Comparison Matrix: Easily Confused Pairs

| Feature | Inference | Conclusion | Assumption |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where it lives | Implied between lines | End-point of argument | Hidden behind premises |
| Direct quote? | Never | Never | Never |
| Quantity on test | 2–3 items per passage | 1 main-point item | Rare, in many papers conflated |
| Strength required | Probably or definitely true | Definitely true | Definitely true + unstated |
| Typical NAT-I stem | "it can be inferred that…" | "which of the following best concludes…" | required for the argument to hold |
| Failure mode | Choosing a restated fact | Picking a weak restatement | Picking a stated premise |

#### Qualifier Hierarchy You Must Match

Probability words are graded, and slipping one level ruins an answer.

1. **All / Every / None / Never** — absolute; match exactly.
2. **Most / In most keys / In most cases** — strong majority; about 51–99%.
3. **Some / In many papers / Many** — partial; at least one, more.
4. **May / Can / Can** — permissive; not contradicted.

A passage saying *"some employees cycle to work"* does **not** license *"most employees cycle to work"* — the jump from *some* to *most* is the classic NAT-I trap.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** *"Pakistan's textile exports rose 14% last year, yet total export revenue fell because cotton prices crashed. The government blames global demand; industry leaders blame the devaluation policy."* Which conclusion is most strongly supported?

- (A) Cotton prices will keep falling next year.
- (B) The devaluation policy is solely responsible for the revenue drop.
- (C) Falling cotton prices contributed to the revenue decline despite higher volumes.
- (D) Global demand for Pakistani textiles increased last year.
- (E) Industry leaders support the government's export policy.

#### Solution:
1. **Premise A:** textile *volumes* rose 14%.
2. **Premise B:** total *revenue* still fell.
3. **Premise C:** cotton prices crashed (a unit-price input).
4. To reconcile higher volume + lower revenue, **price-per-unit must have dropped sharply** — the crashed cotton price explains exactly that.
5. (A) projects the future → not supported. (B) overstates with "solely" → contradicts the competing blame. (D) contradicts "demand" being the official culprit. (E) misreads the stance — leaders *blame* the policy, not support it.
6. Only **(C)** stitches the stated facts together without contradiction.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students pick (B) because it sounds decisive. The word **solely** is the killer — the passage offers *two competing explanations* and never isolates one. When a quantifier like *solely, all, or none* appears, hunt for a softer passage equivalent.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Probability Boundaries

The hardest NAT-I items live at the boundary between *probably true* and * true*. A statement can be consistent with every premise yet still be a poor inference because the premises do not actively point to it. Ask: does the passage **predict** this, or merely **permit** it? Only predictions are safe inferences; permissions are in most keys too weak for the "must be true" standard.

A second boundary appears with **negative information**. A passage saying *"no evidence of fraud was found"* does **not** license *"fraud did not occur"*; it licenses *"the investigation did not surface fraud"*. Conflating an *epistemic* claim with an *ontic* one is the standard trap in conclusion items.

#### Advanced Traps and Exceptions

1. **The double-negative inversion** — "not unlikely" reads as *probably yes*, not *probably no*; strip negatives before testing.
2. **Temporal slippage** — a passage about *last year* does not support claims about *next year*; watch tense in every premise.
3. **Cause-versus-correlation** — a correlation between A and B does not let you conclude A *caused* B unless causation is stated.
4. **The extreme-option decoy** — the loudest, most dramatic answer is almost never the must-be-true one; it in most keys overreaches.
5. **Restated-premise decoy** — an option that quotes a premise verbatim is *stated*, not *concluded*; reject it as a main-point candidate.
6. **Scope expansion** — moving from a *Pakistani* example to a *global* claim, or from a *university* case to *all education*, breaks inference rules.
7. **Modality creep** — upgrading *can* in the passage to *will* in the option invalidates the answer.

#### Cross-Topic Linkages

This topic interlocks with **Critical Reasoning** (assumption vs conclusion), **Syllogisms** (deductive chains), and **Reading Comprehension main-point** questions. Practising one strengthens the others because the must-be-true test is universal.

#### Two Practice Prompts

1. *Passage:* "The library extended its hours during exams. Borrowing rose 22% in the fortnight, then returned to baseline." Which inference is strongest — that extended hours caused the rise, or that exam demand caused the rise? Defend using the must-be-true test.
2. *Passage:* "Several firms reported Q3 losses, although no firm exited the market." Which of these is a valid inference — (i) the losses were small, (ii) firms expect future profit, (iii) losses do not always force exit? Justify which survives negation by any passage line.
