# Elasticity

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your CMA Foundation Economics paper.

**Elasticity** measures the responsiveness of one economic variable to a change in another, expressed as the ratio of two percentage changes. The four must-know variants are:

- **Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)** = (% ΔQd) ÷ (% ΔP), where Qd is quantity demanded and P is price (₹ per unit).
- **Income Elasticity of Demand (YED)** = (% ΔQd) ÷ (% ΔY), where Y is consumer income (₹).
- **Cross Elasticity of Demand (XED)** = (% ΔQd of Good A) ÷ (% ΔP of Good B).
- **Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)** = (% ΔQs) ÷ (% ΔP), where Qs is quantity supplied.

#### Quick Classification Table

| Coefficient value | Demand / Supply label | Total revenue behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| PED = ∞ | Perfectly elastic | TR falls to zero if P falls |
| PED > 1 | Relatively elastic | TR rises when P falls |
| PED = 1 | Unit elastic | TR at maximum, constant |
| 0 < PED < 1 | Relatively inelastic | TR falls when P falls |
| PED = 0 | Perfectly inelastic | TR changes proportionally with P |

#### High-yield pointers for the CMA Foundation paper:
1. Remember the sign rule: substitutes give **positive XED**, complements give **negative XED**.
2. Inferior goods carry **negative YED**; luxuries show YED > 1.
3. The **Total Outlay Test** identifies elasticity by watching how total expenditure moves when price changes.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students working through the CMA Foundation Economics syllabus.

#### Definition and the General Formula

Elasticity is the ratio of the percentage change in the dependent variable to the percentage change in the independent variable. For demand, the independent variable is usually price, income, or the price of a related good. The general form is:

> **E = (% change in dependent variable) / (% change in independent variable)**

This dimensionless ratio removes units, allowing comparison across goods priced in rupees per kg, rupees per litre, or rupees per unit.

#### Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

Using the proportionate (percentage) method: **PED = (% ΔQd) / (% ΔP)**. Using the point (geometric) method on a linear demand curve: **PED = (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q)** at the initial price-quantity point. The arc method averages the two observations: **PED = (ΔQ / average Q) / (ΔP / average P)**, which avoids bias from choosing one endpoint as the base.

#### Total Outlay Method

The Total Outlay Test infers PED without computing percentages:

- If total revenue (TR) **falls** when price rises, demand is **elastic** (PED > 1).
- If TR **rises** when price rises, demand is **inelastic** (PED < 1).
- If TR is **unchanged**, demand is **unit elastic** (PED = 1).

This connects directly to managerial decisions on pricing and tax incidence.

#### Income Elasticity (YED) and Cross Elasticity (XED)

YED is **positive** for normal goods, **negative** for inferior goods, **greater than one** for luxuries, and **between zero and one** for necessities. XED is **positive** for substitutes, **negative** for complements, and **zero** for unrelated goods. These signs appear routinely in CMA Foundation MCQs.

#### Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)

PES depends heavily on the time horizon. In the very short run PES approaches 0 because producers cannot adjust output, while in the long run PES rises because firms can alter plant size, hire labour, and reorder inputs.

#### Determinants and Common Traps

Determinants include availability of substitutes, proportion of income spent, durability, number of uses, and time period. A typical trap is stating that perfectly inelastic demand means zero consumption; it actually means consumption is constant regardless of price, producing a vertical demand curve.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for deeper mastery of elasticity concepts.

#### Worked Numerical Example

A shopkeeper sells rice at ₹40 per kg, moving 500 kg per month. After a price cut to ₹36 per kg, monthly sales rise to 600 kg. Computing point PED at the initial point:

- ΔQ = 600 − 500 = 100; Q = 500; ΔP = 36 − 40 = −4; P = 40.
- PED = (100 / −4) × (40 / 500) = −25 × 0.08 = **−2.0**.
- Ignoring the sign convention used in Indian textbooks (which often report PED as a positive magnitude), demand is **relatively elastic**, so the shopkeeper's total revenue will rise after the price cut.

Using the arc method over the same change: ΔQ = 100, average Q = 550; ΔP = −4, average P = 38. PED = (100/550) ÷ (−4/38) = 0.1818 ÷ −0.1053 ≈ **−1.73**, still elastic but a different figure — illustrating why the chosen base matters.

#### Edge Cases and Examiner Traps

| Scenario | Correct reading | Why students slip |
|---|---|---|
| Vertical demand curve | PED = 0, perfectly inelastic | Confused with "quantity = 0" |
| Horizontal demand curve | PED = ∞, perfectly elastic | Often called "perfectly competitive firm" |
| Luxury goods | YED > 1 | Mixed with necessity classification |
| Inferior goods | YED < 0 | Reported as YED = 0 by rote |
| PES in very short run | ≈ 0 | Reported as ∞ for "instant adjustment" |

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics

Elasticity underpins **tax incidence analysis** — the more inelastic side of the market bears a larger share of any excise duty. It also feeds **demand forecasting** (luxury vs necessity shifts when disposable income rises) and **pricing strategy** under imperfect competition.

#### Practice Prompts

1. If price rises from ₹50 to ₹55 and quantity demanded falls from 200 units to 180 units, compute PED using both the point method and the arc method, then state total revenue behaviour.
2. Good A and Good B show XED = +1.4. If the price of Good B rises by 10%, what is the expected percentage change in quantity demanded of Good A, and what does the sign tell you about the relationship?
