# Summary, Note and Paragraph Writing

The Department of Examinations publishes English Language as a two-paper subject, and its published marking scheme is specific: Paper I is 40 marks, Paper II is 60 marks, 100 in total. That 60 is the number to plan around — the paper carrying most of the subject's marks is the writing paper, and it is worth exactly twice Paper I.

The 2026 timetable sets English Language Paper I at 08:30–09:30 and Paper II at 09:45–11:45. Two hours for the writing paper is not generous once you have read a long text and planned, which is why technique matters more here than in almost any other O/L subject.

## The three forms are not the same skill

They get taught together and they are assessed differently, so do not let one habit contaminate the others.

A summary is reduction. You remove everything except the main points, and the main points are a specific, findable subset of the passage. Adding an interpretation, a flourish or a connecting phrase that is not in the text is a fault in a summary even when it improves the prose.

A set of notes is compression with structure. Headings carry the meaning and the detail hangs underneath. Notes do not need sentences, and a candidate who writes full paragraphs in note form wastes time and, in some marking schemes, loses structure marks.

A continuous paragraph is expansion in the other direction — you are given a heading or an outline and must build an argument under it. Here the linking language is not optional, because coherence is what is being assessed.

## Summary: find the points, then stop

Read the passage once for structure before you write anything. Ask where it turns and where the writer's emphasis sits, because the main points are usually the topic sentences of the substantial paragraphs.

Then write each point in your own words, as a separate line. Only then, if the format requires it, connect them. Working in the other order — composing as you read — is how paragraphs of detail end up in a summary, and detail is what you are supposed to be removing.

The test of a good summary is that it could be written from the passage alone and no other text, and that a reader who has not seen the passage learns the argument. If your summary would also fit a different text on the same topic, you have written too generally.

The commonest fault is dropping the conclusion. Summaries routinely end at the second-last idea because the writer ran out of time. Reserve the last two minutes for the conclusion.

## Continuous paragraph: one idea, linked

Take the heading and write a topic sentence that states the idea in your own words — not a copy of the heading, which wastes a sentence that could have developed it.

Then develop. Give an example, explain it, and give a consequence or a contrast. That is a paragraph; three related sentences is not yet, because nothing has been explained.

Link explicitly. "However", "this is not the whole picture", "the consequence is", "by contrast" — these are not padding. In a continuous-paragraph question the link words are doing real work, because the examiner is checking that you can hold a line of reasoning across sentences rather than listing four separate assertions.

Close by returning to the heading. One sentence, showing that the argument has landed.

## Common mistakes

Writing note-form content as full paragraphs, or paragraph-form content as notes. The format is part of the mark, not a container.

Copying the original wording instead of summarising in your own words. Paraphrase the structure, not the words — you want the idea in your own construction.

Including the writer's opinion or an evaluation in a summary task. "This shows the author was wrong" is interpretation, and interpretation belongs in the continuous-paragraph paper, not in a reduction.

Running out of time before the conclusion, which is where a summary is most often truncated.

Forgetting topic sentences in continuous-paragraph questions and starting mid-thought. It is the cheapest available mark and the most frequently given away.

Over-using connectives to the point where the paragraph reads mechanically. Two or three well-chosen links do more work than six.
