# Logical Organization & Argument Mapping

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Logical Organization & Argument Mapping is the Reading Comprehension skill of decoding how a passage is built and tracing the author's argument from premises to conclusion. It is the engine behind **main-point**, **primary-purpose**, **function**, and **relationship** questions on LSAT India Reading-Comp. Structure signals (*however*, *therefore*, *for example*, *in contrast*, *moreover*) are the road signs that mark each argumentative move.

- **Thesis** = the author's central claim; **premises** = reasons supporting it; **counterargument** = opposing view acknowledged and rebutted.
- Map each paragraph by its **function**: introduction → background → evidence → counterpoint → conclusion.
- Distinguish the *topic* (what the passage is about) from the *thesis* (what the author argues about it).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### What the skill actually tests

LSAT India passages reward readers who can see the scaffolding beneath the prose. The four question families powered by this skill are main-point, primary-purpose, function-of-a-paragraph, and relationship-between-ideas. Each asks you to abstract one level above the surface text — to name the *role* an idea plays in the author's argument rather than to restate what it says.

#### Recognizing the seven organizational schemes

| Scheme | Structural fingerprint | Typical LSAT trigger |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cause–effect | *because, consequently, as a result* | "Function of the third paragraph" |
| Compare–contrast | *however, in contrast, unlike* | "Relationship between A and B" |
| Problem–solution | *the difficulty, the remedy* | "Primary purpose of the passage" |
| Classification | *three types, several kinds* | "Which answer best organizes the author's categories?" |
| Definition–example | *defined as, for instance* | "Function of the example in paragraph two" |
| Chronological | *first, then, subsequently* | "What shifts in the author's view over time?" |
| Claim–evidence–rebuttal | *argues, demonstrates, critics counter* | "Main point / role of counterargument" |

#### Mapping an argument in four steps

1. Locate the **thesis sentence** — usually the first or last sentence of paragraph one, or the final paragraph.
2. Tag each paragraph by **function**: introduction, background, supporting evidence, counterargument, rebuttal, or synthesis.
3. Trace **premise-to-claim links** — note which evidence supports which assertion, and flag any unstated **assumptions** bridging them.
4. Use **signal words** (*however, therefore, moreover*) as delimiters between argumentative moves; they mark where the author's role changes from evidence-giver to opponent of a view.

A common trap: a paragraph that *describes* a counterargument is still authored as a **rebuttal**, not as endorsement. Selecting "the author agrees with the criticism" is the classic wrong-choice error on function questions.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Implicit reasoning and the assumption gap

The hardest organization questions on LSAT India Reading-Comp do not ask what the author says — they ask what the author **must believe** to connect stated premises to the stated conclusion. This is the *assumption gap*: the unstated link that, if removed, collapses the argument. To find it, ask, "If this premise were the only support for the conclusion, what else would have to be true?" The answer is the assumption.

#### Pitfalls unique to mapped arguments

| Mistake | Why it costs marks | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confusing topic with thesis | Picks an answer that is merely descriptive of the subject | Search for the author's evaluative claim |
| Treating all paragraphs as equal | Misses where the central argument actually lives | Rank paragraphs by argumentative weight |
| Reading reported speech as authorial | Attributes a cited scholar's view to the author | Note attribution cues (*according to, X argues*) |
| Ignoring concession cues (*admittedly, granted*) | Misclassifies refutations as agreements | Treat concessions as setup for the rebuttal that follows |

#### Practice prompts (2 micro-drills)

1. **Mapping drill.** Take any 30-line LSAT passage. Without looking back, write one sentence per paragraph naming its function (intro, evidence, counterargument, etc.). Compare your map to the paragraph's actual role; mismatches reveal your blind spots.
2. **Signal-word sweep.** Highlight every transition word in the passage. Each one marks a boundary between argumentative moves. If you cannot name what changes at each boundary, the structure is unclear to you — re-read just those junctures.

This skill transfers directly into Logical Reasoning, where the same premise–conclusion–assumption architecture appears in shorter form. Mastering it here compounds across both sections.
