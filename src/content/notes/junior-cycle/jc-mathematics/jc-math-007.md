# Financial Mathematics — Junior Cycle Mathematics Notes

Junior Cycle treats financial mathematics as a strand outcome in its own right rather than a sub-topic of arithmetic, because the reasoning is the point and not the computation. The learning outcome asks candidates to compare financial options, judge whether a decision is financially sound, and reason about money over time — not to evaluate a compound interest formula they have been handed.

---

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### The three structures

**Simple interest.** *A* = *P*(1 + *rt*), where *r* is the rate per year and *t* the time in years. Interest is always charged on the original principal, so the amount you receive grows in a straight line. Recognisable from the words "simple interest".

**Compound interest.** *A* = *P*(1 + *r*)^(*t*). Each year's interest is added to the balance, and the next year's interest is charged on the larger balance, so growth accelerates. Recognisable from "compound", "interest compounded", or simply from a savings question where no qualifier is given — because savings conventionally compound.

**Depreciation.** The value of an asset falls. Straight-line depreciation writes off the same amount each year; reducing-balance depreciation applies a fixed percentage to the falling value. Both are assessed by asking you to build a table and then describe the pattern.


#### Deciding which one you are looking at

Read for three things in this order.

1. **Is the base growing or staying the same?** If interest is always charged on the original amount, it is simple. If it is charged on a balance that has previously included interest, it is compound.
2. **What is being asked for?** A single "how much after *t* years" usually wants the formula substituted once. A table or "show what happens each year" wants each step written out.
3. **Is the value going up or down?** A rising balance is interest; a falling one is depreciation or a loan.

Most candidates lose marks here by using the wrong structure, and the tell is nearly always in the wording rather than in the arithmetic.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Building the table

When a question says to show the working year by year, a table is required — not a formula.

| Year | Opening value | Interest or depreciation | Closing value |
|---|---|---|---|

Fill in one row completely as a worked example, then use the pattern. Round at each step if the question says to, and say that you have. Rounding only at the end, when the question required rounding each step, produces a different answer from the mark scheme.

Where the balance falls — a loan — the interest is charged on the *opening* balance and the repayment reduces the *closing* balance. Charging interest on the original amount every year is the standard error, and it produces a total interest that is too large.


#### Comparing two options

The strand outcome is comparison, so the exam often gives you two savings products, two loans, or a cash price against a hire purchase, and asks which is better.

A comparison needs more than two final numbers. Build the comparison around:

- **The final value** in each case.
- **The time** over which each runs — a larger return over a longer period is not automatically better.
- **The risk**, where the question gives it: variable rates, early-withdrawal penalties, fees.
- **Access to the money.** Money locked in for five years is worth less than money available now, and that difference is not captured by a single figure.

Where the question asks you to justify a choice, the sentence needs to reference the comparison, not just state the winner. "Option A gives more after five years but nothing can be withdrawn in the first two, so for money needed in year one Option B is the safer choice" earns the reasoning marks that "Option A is better" does not.


#### Judging a decision

Questions that ask whether a decision is financially sound require the same move: state what the decision costs over its full duration, not just at the start. A monthly payment looks small until it is multiplied by the number of payments, and a headline rate ignores fees.

The strand also expects economic reasoning in a familiar context — for example, why the effect of a percentage rise is different on a small base than on a large one. A 50% fall from €40 leaves €20, but a 50% rise from €20 gives €30, not €40. The percentages look the same and the amounts are not, and that asymmetry is a named outcome.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Common mistakes

1. **Applying compound interest where simple interest is named.** Read the wording every time.
2. **Charging interest on the original principal for a loan**, where it is charged on the falling balance.
3. **Comparing a longer-term product against a shorter-term one on final value alone.**
4. **Ignoring fees** when a question supplies them, and then concluding the wrong option is cheaper.
5. **Rounding at the wrong stage** in a table question.
6. **Saying a rise of 40% then a fall of 40% returns to the start.** It does not: 1.4 × 0.6 = 0.84.

## Self-check

1. €2,000 at 6% per year, compound. What is the balance after two years? *(Year 1: 2000 × 1.06 = 2120. Year 2: 2120 × 1.06 = 2247.20. The second year charges interest on 2120, not on 2000 — that difference from simple interest is exactly what compound means.)*
2. A loan of €5,000 at 8%, repaying €2,000 a year. What is the interest in the second year? *(€400 in year 1 on €5,000, leaving €3,400 outstanding, so the second year's interest is 3400 × 0.08 = €272. Charging €400 again gives a total interest that is too large and is the designed error.)*
3. Savings option A pays 4% and locks the money for four years; option B pays 3.5% and allows withdrawal at any time. How do you decide? *(There is no single answer without knowing whether the money might be needed. The comparison must state the time horizon and the access constraint, and justify the choice in terms of them — that reasoning is the outcome being assessed, not a final-value calculation.)*
4. A price rises 50% and then falls 50%. Where does it end up? *(At 75% of the original, because 1.5 × 0.5 = 0.75. The percentages look symmetrical and the amounts are not, because the fall is applied to a larger base than the rise was.)*
5. Why does a table rather than one formula substitution earn the marks in a year-by-year question? *(The strand outcome is reasoning about money over time. The table shows the pattern — interest rising in absolute terms on a growing balance, or falling on a shrinking one — which is what the question is assessing; the final figure alone does not demonstrate it.)*
