# Sense Organs and Effectors

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Sense organs** (eye, ear, nose, tongue, skin) contain **receptors** that detect specific **stimuli** and convert them into **nerve impulses** sent to the brain via **sensory neurones**. **Effectors** are the **muscles** and **glands** that respond to these impulses through contraction or secretion. A **reflex arc** is the shortest pathway: receptor → **sensory neurone** → **relay (intermediate) neurone** in the spinal cord → **motor neurone** → effector. The **eye** focuses light on the **retina**, where **rods** detect dim light (black-and-white vision) and **cones** detect colour (red, green, blue). The **ear** has three regions: outer (pinna, auditory canal, eardrum), middle (three ossicles: malleus, incus, stapes), and inner (cochlea for hearing, semicircular canals for balance). **NECO tip:** questions often test the ear ossicle sequence and the functions of rods vs. cones in a single MCQ.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### The Eye and Vision
Light enters through the **cornea**, passes through the **aqueous humour**, the **pupil** (whose size is regulated by circular and radial muscles of the **iris**), the **lens**, and the **vitreous humour** before striking the **retina**. The **lens** changes shape via the **ciliary muscles** and **suspensory ligaments** in a process called **accommodation**: it becomes thicker (more convex) for near objects and thinner (flatter) for distant objects. The retina houses two photoreceptor types: **rods**, which contain the pigment **rhodopsin** and function in dim light without colour discrimination, and **cones**, which require bright light and exist in three forms sensitive to red, green, and blue wavelengths. The **fovea (yellow spot)** carries the densest cone population and yields the sharpest vision.

#### The Ear, Tongue, Nose and Skin
The **ear** divides into three sections. The **outer ear** (pinna, auditory canal, tympanic membrane) funnels sound waves. The **middle ear** contains the three **ossicles** — malleus, incus and stapes — which amplify vibrations, and the **Eustachian tube**, which equalises pressure with the pharynx. The **inner ear** holds the **cochlea** (spiral organ of hearing with hair cells on the basilar membrane) and three **semicircular canals** (detecting rotational balance). The **tongue** bears **taste buds** on papillae, recognising four primary tastes: sweet, sour, bitter and salty. The **nose** uses **olfactory epithelium** in the upper nasal cavity to detect volatile chemicals. The **skin** carries **Meissner's corpuscles** (light touch), **Pacinian corpuscles** (deep pressure), temperature receptors, and free nerve endings for pain.

#### Reflex Arc and Effectors
A **reflex action** is rapid, involuntary and protective. Its pathway runs: **receptor → sensory (afferent) neurone → relay (intermediate) neurone in the spinal cord → motor (efferent) neurone → effector**. The knee-jerk and withdrawal (e.g., hand from hot object) reflexes are classic NECO examples. **Effectors** are **muscles** (contract) and **glands** (secrete). Muscles classify as **voluntary/striated/skeletal** (e.g., biceps), **involuntary/smooth/visceral** (gut wall), and **cardiac** (heart wall, striated but involuntary).

#### NECO Exam Pattern
Expect 1–2 objective questions (multiple choice) on this topic per paper, often combining a diagram of the eye or ear with a function-matching task. Theory questions may ask you to "describe a reflex arc" or "state the functions of the three ear ossicles."

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Coordination of Receptor and Effector
**Nervous coordination** uses electrical impulses travelling along neurones. A **neurone** consists of a cell body, **dendrites** (receive impulses), an **axon** (transmits impulses), and a **myelin sheath** (insulates the axon and speeds saltatory conduction). Where two neurones meet, a **synapse** allows chemical transmission: an impulse triggers release of **neurotransmitters** (e.g., acetylcholine) across the synaptic cleft, which bind to receptors on the next neurone. The **central nervous system (CNS)** comprises the brain and spinal cord (containing **grey matter** of cell bodies internally and **white matter** of myelinated tracts externally); the **peripheral nervous system (PNS)** carries sensory and motor neurones to and from the CNS.

#### Common Mistakes and Traps
- Confusing **rods** (many, dim light, no colour) with **cones** (few, bright light, colour vision).
- Reversing the order of the three ear ossicles: malleus → incus → stapes.
- Believing the **Eustachian tube** transmits sound — it only equalises air pressure.
- Forgetting that the **suspensory ligaments** are relaxed for near vision (lens is thick) and taut for distant vision (lens is thin).
- Writing "receptor" when the question asks for the **effector** in a reflex arc.

#### Adjacent Topics Worth Linking
**Hormonal coordination** complements nervous control: **endocrine glands** (e.g., pituitary, thyroid) release **hormones** into the blood, which travel to **target organs**. Unlike nerves, hormonal responses are slower but longer-lasting — a useful contrast if the paper asks you to compare the two systems.

#### Practice Prompts
1. A student touches a hot iron. Describe the complete reflex arc that causes the hand to withdraw, naming each structure and the type of neurone involved. (Expected length: 6–8 lines.)
2. State two structural differences between a rod and a cone, and explain why a person cannot see colour in a dimly lit room. (Expected length: 4–5 lines.)
