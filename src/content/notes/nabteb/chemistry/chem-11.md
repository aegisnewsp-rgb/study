# Aldehydes, Ketones and Esters

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your NABTEB Chemistry paper.

- **Aldehydes (RCHO)** carry the carbonyl group (C=O) bonded to at least one hydrogen; **ketones (RCOR′)** carry C=O bonded to two carbon groups; **esters (RCOOR′)** are acid derivatives with the –OH of a carboxylic acid replaced by an –OR′ group.
- **Carbonyl polarity** makes the carbon atom electrophilic, so aldehydes and ketones undergo **nucleophilic addition**, while esters preferentially undergo **nucleophilic acyl substitution** (hydrolysis, saponification).
- **Fischer esterification**: RCOOH + R′OH ⇌ RCOOR′ + H₂O (conc. H₂SO₄ catalyst, reflux).
- **Distinguishing tests**: 2,4-DNP gives an orange/yellow precipitate with both aldehydes and ketones; **Tollens' (silver mirror) and Fehling's (brick-red Cu₂O) tests** are positive only for aldehydes.
- **Iodoform test** detects the CH₃CO– group (methyl ketones, acetaldehyde, ethanol) by forming yellow CHI₃.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months of revision time.

#### Functional-Group Identity

The **carbonyl group C=O** is the defining feature of all three families. In **aldehydes**, the carbonyl carbon is bonded to one hydrogen and one R group, giving RCHO. In **ketones**, both substituents are carbon groups, giving RCOR′ (where R and R′ may be the same or different alkyl/aryl groups). **Esters** carry an alkoxy group (–OR′) on the carbonyl carbon, written RCOOR′. The "R" attached directly to the C=O is the acyl group; the "R′" after the second oxygen comes from the alcohol.

#### Naming Esters Correctly

Esters are named **alkyl alkanoate**: the first word is the alkyl group from the alcohol, the second is the alkanoate from the parent acid. Thus CH₃COOC₂H₅ is **ethyl ethanoate** (not "ethanoic ethyl"), and CH₃CH₂COOCH₃ is **methyl propanoate**. This naming convention is a frequent NABTEB short-answer question worth 2–3 marks.

#### Preparation and Hydrolysis

Fischer esterification reversibly combines a carboxylic acid and an alcohol under concentrated H₂SO₄:

> RCOOH + R′OH ⇌ RCOOR′ + H₂O (heat, conc. H₂SO₄)

Hydrolysis runs in reverse under dilute acid; **saponification** is the irreversible alkaline route using NaOH or KOH, yielding a soap (RCOO⁻Na⁺/K⁺) and an alcohol.

#### Distinguishing Tests Compared

| Reagent | Aldehyde | Ketone | Ester |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (Brady's) | Orange/yellow ppt | Orange/yellow ppt | No reaction |
| Tollens' (Ag(NH₃)₂OH) | Silver mirror | No reaction | No reaction |
| Fehling's (Cu²⁺/tartrate) | Brick-red Cu₂O | No reaction | No reaction |
| Iodoform (I₂/NaOH) | Positive for CH₃CHO only | Positive for CH₃CO–R | No reaction |

#### Common NABTEB Question Patterns

- Write IUPAC names from condensed structural formulas (e.g. (CH₃)₂CHCHO → 2-methylpropanal).
- State **two reagents** that distinguish an aldehyde from a ketone and write the observation.
- Balance the equation for the reaction of CH₃COOC₂H₅ with aqueous NaOH.
- Explain why esterification is written with an **equilibrium arrow**, not a single arrow.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for mastery-level understanding of the topic.

#### Reactivity Differences Between Aldehydes and Ketones

Both undergo nucleophilic addition, but **aldehydes react faster** for two reinforcing reasons. First, **steric**: the carbonyl carbon of an aldehyde carries one H and one R, leaving the carbon more accessible than a ketone's doubly substituted carbon. Second, **electronic**: the two alkyl groups of a ketone are electron-donating (+I), which slightly stabilises the carbonyl and reduces the partial positive charge on carbon. This is why Tollen's and Fehling's reagents — which require the carbonyl to be oxidised to a carboxylate — work on aldehydes but leave ordinary ketones untouched.

#### Mechanisms to Recognise

- **Nucleophilic addition to C=O**: the nucleophile (e.g. HCN, H₂O, ROH, NH₂OH) attacks the electrophilic carbon, the C=O π-bond breaks, and the oxygen is protonated. The product for HCN addition to CH₃CHO is CH₃CH(OH)CN, a cyanohydrin.
- **Nucleophilic acyl substitution in esters**: HO⁻ (or H₂O) attacks the carbonyl carbon, a tetrahedral intermediate forms, and the –OR′ group leaves, regenerating the C=O bond. This is why saponification proceeds to completion while Fischer esterification is reversible.

#### Edge Cases and Examiner Traps

- **Formaldehyde (HCHO)** is the only aldehyde without an R group; it still gives positive Tollens' and Fehling's tests.
- **Methanoic acid (HCOOH)** also reduces Tollens' reagent because the H attached to the carbonyl carbon is itself oxidised; do not list methanoic acid as a "ketone-like" exception.
- The **iodoform test** is positive for ethanol and secondary alcohols bearing CH₃CH(OH)– because they are first oxidised in situ to a methyl carbonyl. CH₃CH₂CHO (propanal) gives a negative iodoform test.
- Ketones **can** be oxidised by strong agents (hot KMnO₄, conc. HNO₃) and undergo haloform cleavage with alkaline halogens; examiners sometimes trick students with "ketones cannot be oxidised".

#### Worked Example

**Q:** Name CH₃CH₂COOCH₃ and write its saponification equation with NaOH.

**A:** **Methyl propanoate**. CH₃CH₂COOCH₃ + NaOH → CH₃CH₂COONa + CH₃OH. The sodium salt (sodium propanoate) and methanol are the products.

#### Practice Prompts

1. State **two** chemical tests to distinguish propanal from propanone, and give the observation in each case.
2. Write a balanced equation for the preparation of ethyl ethanoate from ethanoic acid, name the catalyst, and explain why the reaction is described as reversible.
