# Internal Medicine Core

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review

> Step 2 CK is not a diagnosis exam, it is a next-step exam. The stem hands you a presentation and asks what happens next.

| Presentation | Your first split | The discriminator that decides it |
|---|---|---|
| Chest pain | Cardiac vs non-cardiac | Character, radiation, timing, associated features |
| Dyspnoea | Acute vs chronic | Time course, before anything else |
| Jaundice | Pre-hepatic, hepatic, post-hepatic | Urinalysis — conjugated bilirubin darkens the urine |
| Proteinuria | Nephritic vs nephrotic | Blood and protein vs protein loss plus oedema |
| Diabetic emergency | DKA, HHS, hypoglycaemia | Rate of onset, ketones and pH — not glucose alone |

**Three rules that decide most items.** Treat the cause, not the number. A highly sensitive test rules out; a highly specific test rules in. And pre-test probability dominates everything else — even a good test performs badly against an implausible prior.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study

Step 2 CK "evaluates your clinical knowledge and ability to apply medical concepts in patient care". That second clause is the whole exam. Knowing that heart failure causes oedema is Step 1 knowledge; recognising that a patient with unilateral calf swelling is more likely to have a DVT than heart failure is Step 2 knowledge. This topic covers the internal-medicine patterns you will meet repeatedly, organised the way they are examined.

#### Working by presentation, not by diagnosis

Study internal medicine by the way patients present, not by listing diseases. The stem gives you a presentation and asks what to do next; if you have memorised a disease list, you will not recognise which one fits. Organise your revision as: presentation, then differential, then what must be excluded, then which test answers the question, then what you do while waiting for the result.

Two presentations carry disproportionate weight.

Chest pain is ischaemic disease, dissection, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, pericarditis, oesophageal spasm or musculoskeletal until shown otherwise, and the discriminator is almost always a combination of character, radiation, timing and associated features rather than any single one of them. Dyspnoea is cardiac, pulmonary, anaemic, metabolic or neuromuscular, and the first question is always whether it came on acutely or has been chronic, because the two differentials share almost nothing.

#### Patterns by system

In cardiology, palpitations narrow to arrhythmia or structural lesion, so interpret the rhythm rather than the symptom. Syncope is cardiac unless there is clear evidence otherwise, and hypertension in a young patient with an abdominal bruit warrants looking for a renal cause.

Respiratory symptoms describe infection without reliably identifying the organism — fever, cough and sputum colour will not do it. Central versus peripheral crackles, pleuritic pain and clubbing are the more useful discriminators. Long-term breathlessness with a barrel chest points towards emphysema; the same breathlessness with orthopnoea and ankle swelling points towards heart failure.

In renal disease, classify the problem first as glomerular, tubular, vascular or post-renal, because the urine findings follow from the site. Nephritic presentations carry blood and protein, while nephrotic presentations are dominated by protein loss with oedema.

For the liver and biliary tract, jaundice is pre-hepatic, hepatic or post-hepatic, and the urinalysis separates them faster than anything else: conjugated bilirubin appears in urine in the post-hepatic group and the urine turns dark. Ascites with a large cap suggests portal hypertension.

In endocrinology, the diabetic emergencies are DKA, HHS and hypoglycaemia, and the features that distinguish them are rate of onset, ketones and pH rather than blood glucose alone. Thyroid presentations shift with age, since older patients often present atypically, without tremor or heat intolerance.

Rheumatology separates inflammatory from mechanical pain by pattern. Morning stiffness over an hour with symmetrical small-joint involvement suggests inflammatory arthritis; pain that is worse on use is mechanical. Fever, rash and joint pain together point towards vasculitis or systemic disease rather than a single joint problem.

| System | Classify first by | What the urine or rhythm then tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Cardiology | Arrhythmia vs structural lesion | Interpret the rhythm, not the symptom |
| Renal | Glomerular, tubular, vascular, post-renal | Urine findings follow from the site |
| Liver and biliary | Pre-hepatic, hepatic, post-hepatic | Conjugated bilirubin in urine means post-hepatic |
| Endocrine | DKA, HHS, hypoglycaemia | Onset rate, ketones, pH separate them |
| Rheumatology | Inflammatory vs mechanical | Morning stiffness over an hour, symmetrical small joints |

#### Diagnostic testing as a decision, not a reflex

Before ordering any test you should be able to say what result would change your management. The exam rewards this because it is how clinical practice actually works.

Pre-test probability decides everything. A D-dimer ordered in a low-risk patient and the same test ordered in a high-risk one are not the same investigation, because a high pre-test probability makes a positive result almost uninformative. Sensitivity and specificity cut different ways: a highly sensitive test rules out, a highly specific one rules in, which is why a negative result on a specific test means far less than a negative result on a sensitive one. And above all the pre-test probability dominates — even a good test performs badly against an implausible prior, which is the most tested idea in the section.

Two more habits follow from the same logic. Multiple comparisons generate false positives, so a panel of twenty tests on a healthy person produces at least one abnormal result by chance alone. And sometimes the correct answer is to observe rather than investigate further.

| Test property | What it licenses you to do | Classic example |
|---|---|---|
| High sensitivity | Rule out when negative | D-dimer in a low pre-test-probability patient |
| High specificity | Rule in when positive | Troponin confirming myocardial injury |
| Neither decides alone | Interpret against the prior | Any test at an implausible pre-test probability |

#### Treatment principles that recur

Treat the cause rather than the number. Metformin is not the first-line choice in acute decompensated heart failure because of the lactic acidosis risk. In sepsis, fluids and antibiotics given early are the interventions with mortality impact; everything after that matters less. And lowering blood pressure is not always the goal — in acute stroke and acute coronary syndrome, a low pressure worsens outcomes.

### 🔴 Extended — Mastery

#### Common mistakes

Diagnosing by symptom alone, without checking duration, tempo and associated features, is the commonest failure of all. Ordering a test because it is plausible rather than because the result would change management is the second. Ignoring pre-test probability and treating sensitivity as a general property of "the test" is the third.

Beyond those: ignoring age, because atypical presentation is the normal presentation in older adults. And anchoring on the first diagnosis that fits, discarding the alternative that explains the whole picture because the first one explains most of it.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** two items look like a knowledge question and are actually a probability question. A patient with a low pre-test probability and a positive D-dimer does not start anticoagulation on that result alone — the false-positive rate of D-dimer in low-prevalence populations is what makes the item work. The stem is testing whether you know when the test is uninformative, not whether you can name the test.
