# Epidemiology and Study Designs

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Epidemiology** is the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states in specified populations, applied to the control of health problems. **Study designs** are the structural frameworks — observational vs experimental, descriptive vs analytical — that dictate subject selection, data collection, and causal inference about exposure–outcome relationships.

- **Hierarchy of evidence (top → bottom):** Systematic review/Meta-analysis → RCT → Cohort → Case-control → Cross-sectional → Case report.
- **Measure of association by design:** RR for cohort and RCT; OR for case-control; prevalence ratio for cross-sectional.
- **Bradford Hill criteria for causation:** Strength, Consistency, Specificity, **Temporality**, Biological gradient, Plausibility, Coherence, Experiment, Analogy.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"ORC-PP"** for choosing a measure — **O**dds Ratio for Case-control, **R**isk Ratio for Cohort/RCT, **P**revalence ratio for cross-sectional, **P**redictive values for screening. Plus **"TICS"** for blinding — **T**riple > **D**ouble > **S**ingle → **I**nvestigator, **C**linician, **S**ubject.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Measures of Frequency and Association

Two-by-two table values: a = exposed cases, b = exposed non-cases, c = unexposed cases, d = unexposed non-cases.

| Measure | Formula | Unit | Used in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Relative Risk (RR) | Iₑ / Iᵤ | dimensionless | Cohort, RCT |
| Odds Ratio (OR) | (a × d) / (b × c) | dimensionless | Case-control |
| Attributable Risk (AR) | Iₑ − Iᵤ | cases/person-time | Cohort |
| AR% | [(Iₑ − Iᵤ) / Iₑ] × 100 | % | Cohort |
| Population AR (PAR) | Iₜₒₜₐₗ − Iᵤ | cases/person-time | Cohort/Population |
| NNH | 1 / AR | persons per extra case | Cohort/RCT |
| Sensitivity | TP / (TP + FN) | proportion (0–1) | Screening |
| Specificity | TN / (TN + FP) | proportion (0–1) | Screening |

#### Design Selection Logic

1. Rare disease → **case-control** (only feasible design to accumulate enough cases).
2. Multiple outcomes of one exposure → **cohort** (gives incidence and RR).
3. Prevalence snapshot or resource-limited setting → **cross-sectional**.
4. Gold-standard causal inference for therapy → **RCT** with intention-to-treat analysis.
5. Population-level hypothesis generation → **ecological study** (watch for ecological fallacy).

#### Concept Comparison: Confounding vs Bias

| Feature | Confounding | Bias |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Third variable associated with both exposure and outcome, not on causal pathway | Systematic error in data collection or selection |
| Effect on estimate | Distorts magnitude/direction | Distorts magnitude/direction |
| Control methods | Restriction, matching, stratification, multivariate regression | Better design, blinding, standardisation, validation |
| Example | Smoking confounding the alcohol–lung cancer link | Recall bias in case-control studies |

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** In a cohort study of 1,000 smokers and 1,000 non-smokers followed for 5 years, 200 smokers and 20 non-smokers developed lung cancer. Calculate RR, OR, AR per 1,000 person-years (assume average 5-year follow-up), and NNH.

#### Solution:
- Table: a = 200, b = 800, c = 20, d = 980.
- **RR** = (200/1000) ÷ (20/1000) = 0.20 ÷ 0.02 = **10**.
- **OR** = (200 × 980) / (800 × 20) = 196,000 / 16,000 = **12.25**.
- **Incidence in exposed** = 200 / (1000 × 5) = 0.04 / person-year = **40 per 1,000 person-years**.
- **Incidence in unexposed** = 20 / (1000 × 5) = 0.004 / person-year = **4 per 1,000 person-years**.
- **AR** = 40 − 4 = **36 per 1,000 person-years**.
- **NNH** = 1 / 0.036 ≈ **27.8** → roughly **28 persons** to cause one extra lung cancer case over 5 years.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students report OR (12.25) as RR. Since lung cancer is not rare in heavy smokers, OR overestimates RR — always pick the measure dictated by the design (cohort → RR). Also, forgetting the person-time denominator yields AR per 1,000 people instead of per 1,000 person-years, a common calculation error in NEET PG numerical items.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Advanced Traps

1. **Rare disease assumption breaks above ~10% prevalence** — OR diverges from RR; use log-binomial or modified Poisson regression for cohort-derived risk ratios.
2. **Nested case-control** — performed inside an existing cohort: controls sampled from the at-risk set, gives OR but retains cohort temporality.
3. **Cross-over RCT** — each subject serves as own control; requires washout and stable disease; not valid for cure outcomes or irreversible events.
4. **Cluster randomisation** — unit of randomisation is a group (village, clinic); inflates required sample size due to intracluster correlation.
5. **Ecological fallacy** — country-level salt intake correlating with stroke mortality does not prove individual causation.
6. **Berkson bias** — hospital-based case-control over-represents patients with multiple admissions, distorting exposure prevalence.
7. **Intention-to-treat vs per-protocol** — ITT preserves randomisation and prevents attrition bias; per-protocol answers efficacy, ITT answers effectiveness.
8. **Bradford Hill temporality** — the only criterion that is *necessary*; cross-sectional designs cannot satisfy it.

#### Practice Prompts

1. A vaccine trial reports RR = 0.35 and AR% = 65%. If exposure prevalence in the population drops from 80% to 20%, what happens to PAR% and why? (Hint: PAR% depends on both RR and prevalence of exposure — lower prevalence lowers PAR%.)
2. A screening test has sensitivity 95% and specificity 90%. In a low-prevalence setting (1%), does a positive result more likely reflect true disease or false positive? Compute PPV: PPV = (0.95 × 0.01) / [(0.95 × 0.01) + (0.10 × 0.99)] ≈ **0.087** — only ~9% of positives are true cases.
