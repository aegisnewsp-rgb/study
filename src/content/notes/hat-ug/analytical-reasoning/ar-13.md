# Ranking and Ordering

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Ranking and Ordering questions ask you to place items (people, objects, events) into a sequence using comparative clues such as taller/shorter, older/younger, or above/below. The HAT-UG Analytical Reasoning section forms about 30% of the test for Engineering, Computer Science, Mathematics, Statistics and Physics streams, and ranking items is one of its recurring item types.

- **Decode clues into a linear chain** before drawing any diagram; every "A is taller than B" becomes `A > B`.
- **Apply transitivity**: if A > B and B > C, then A > C is forced even without a direct statement.
- **Count permutations**: n distinct items in a line give **n!** arrangements; around a circle, only **(n − 1)!** because rotations are equivalent.

| Clue type | Example | Converted form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Comparative | A is taller than B | A > B |
| Positional | D is second from the left | D = rank 2 |
| Negative | B is not adjacent to C | B, C cannot be neighbours |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Reading and converting clues

Every ranking question starts with statements that compare two or more items. The first task is to translate each sentence into a strict relation: "older than" becomes `A > B`, "ranks below" becomes `A < B`, and "in the middle of X and Y" becomes `A` sandwiched between them. HAT-UG statements often use phrases like *"third from the top"* or *"second to the left"*, which are positional indices rather than ranks.

#### Transitive inference

Transitivity is the most powerful shortcut. Once you record `A > B` and `B > C`, you can write `A > C` even when no direct clue exists. Skipping this step leaves invisible gaps in the chain and produces contradictions once a fifth item is added.

#### Anchor placement and negative clues

Anchor clues such as *"D is the tallest"* or *"E sits at the left end"* freeze one position and let the remaining items slot in around them. Negative clues like *"F is not between G and H"* are equally important: they eliminate two candidate slots, so forgetting them instantly invalidates the arrangement.

#### Counting valid orderings

For n distinct items, linear arrangements equal **n!**. On a circular table, rotations collapse, leaving **(n − 1)!**. When items repeat (for example, two identical medals), divide by the factorial of the repeat count. The sum check `1 + 2 + … + n = n(n+1)/2` verifies a complete ranking when every rank 1 to n is assigned exactly once.

| Scenario | Formula | Why it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| n distinct people in a row | n! | Each of the n positions chooses from remaining items |
| n distinct people in a circle | (n − 1)! | One rotation is fixed to remove symmetry |
| Repeated items of type k | n! / k! | Indistinguishable copies collapse permutations |

- Always draw a **position line** or circle before placing items; mental tracking hides contradictions.
- Treat **rank** (1st, 2nd) and **position-from-an-end** separately: "second from the top" in a row of 7 is rank 6, not rank 2.
- Convert every clue **before** attempting placement; partial conversion wastes the 60–90 seconds per item.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge cases and inversion logic

Two permutations of the same n items can be compared via the **inversion count I**, where 0 ≤ I ≤ n(n − 1)/2. Each pair `(i, j)` with `i < j` but `π(i) > π(j)` contributes one inversion. HAT-UG rarely asks for the count directly, but questions phrased as *"how many swaps to sort?"* lean on this idea, so practising one or two inversion drills is worthwhile.

#### Combining multiple parameters

A common trap is the **two-parameter puzzle**: five people are ranked by height *and* by marks, with only partial cross-clues. The clean method is to solve one parameter completely, then overlay the second using cross-references such as *"the tallest is not the topper"*. Solving both simultaneously usually leads to contradictions.

#### Practice prompts

1. Six students A, B, C, D, E, F sit in a row. A is taller than only B; D is taller than E but shorter than C; F is the shortest; B is not adjacent to F. Produce the full height order and state how many linear arrangements satisfy all clues.
2. Five officers sit around a circular table. The youngest sits immediately to the left of the oldest; the second-oldest is opposite the youngest; the third-ranked officer refuses to sit next to the youngest. How many valid circular arrangements exist, and where does the third-ranked officer sit?

> **Exam tip:** In HAT-UG the Analytical Reasoning block averages one ranking-or-seating item every 4–5 questions; budget about 75 seconds per item and skip a contradictory puzzle only after marking the conflicting clue — guessing blindly costs more than returning later.

#### Common traps specific to HAT-UG phrasing

- *"Second from the left"* is read as rank 2 only when the row length equals 2; otherwise it is a positional index.
- *"In the middle of"* requires an **odd** number of items; if the stem gives an even count, the phrase means *"between"* and permits two middle positions.
- Negatives phrased as *"none of"* sometimes hide a single positive constraint, so always rewrite them before counting.
