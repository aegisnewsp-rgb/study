# II. Microscopic World I — HKDSE Chemistry Notes

This topic covers Topic II of the Compulsory Part of HKDSE Chemistry. It addresses atomic structure (subatomic particles, isotopes, electron arrangement); the periodic table (periodicity across periods and down groups); chemical bonding (ionic, covalent, metallic; Lewis structures; shapes of molecules by VSEPR); and intermolecular forces.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Atomic structure**: protons (positive, in the nucleus), neutrons (neutral, in the nucleus), electrons (negative, in shells/orbitals). Atomic number Z = number of protons; mass number A = protons + neutrons. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons (e.g. ¹²C, ¹³C, ¹⁴C).
- **Electron arrangement**: electrons occupy shells (or principal quantum levels) at increasing distance from the nucleus. The first shell holds up to 2 electrons; the second up to 8; the third up to 8 (then 18 in higher periods).
- **Periodic table**: arranged by atomic number. Periods are horizontal rows; groups are vertical columns. Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties (same number of valence electrons).
- **Periodicity**: atomic radius decreases across a period (more protons, same shell); increases down a group (more shells). Ionisation energy increases across a period; decreases down a group. Electronegativity increases across a period; decreases down a group.
- **Chemical bonding**: ionic (transfer of electrons, between metal and non-metal); covalent (sharing of electrons, between non-metals); metallic (delocalised electrons in a lattice of metal cations).
- **Lewis structures**: dots representing valence electrons. Used to show covalent bonds (shared pairs) and lone pairs.
- **Shapes of molecules (VSEPR)**: linear (CO₂), bent (H₂O), trigonal planar (BF₃), tetrahedral (CH₄), trigonal pyramidal (NH₃).
- **Intermolecular forces**: van der Waals (all molecules); dipole-dipole (polar molecules); hydrogen bonding (H bonded to N, O or F).

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing atomic number with mass number — atomic number is the number of protons; mass number is protons + neutrons.
- Treating "polar" and "non-polar" bonds as interchangeable with "polar" and "non-polar" molecules — polar bonds can cancel out in a symmetric molecule (e.g. CO₂ has polar bonds but is non-polar overall).
- Forgetting that hydrogen bonding requires H bonded to N, O or F.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Atomic structure

The modern atomic model: protons and neutrons in the nucleus; electrons in shells (Bohr model) or, more precisely, in orbitals (quantum mechanical model).

- **Isotopes**: same number of protons, different number of neutrons. Chemical properties are the same; physical properties (mass, density) differ. Applications: ¹⁴C dating, ⁶⁰Co cancer therapy, ²H (deuterium) as a tracer.

- **Electron shells**: 1 (2 e⁻), 2 (8 e⁻), 3 (8 e⁻ for the first 18 elements, then 18 for higher), 4 (18-32 e⁻). Valence electrons are those in the outermost shell; they determine the chemical properties.

- **Ion**: an atom or molecule that has gained or lost electrons to become charged. Cation (positive) — formed by metal atoms losing electrons. Anion (negative) — formed by non-metal atoms gaining electrons.

#### The periodic table

The modern periodic table arranges elements by atomic number. Periods (horizontal rows) reflect the principal quantum number n; groups (vertical columns, 1-18) reflect the number of valence electrons.

**Periodicity**:

- **Atomic radius**: decreases across a period (more protons, electrons in the same shell → stronger nuclear pull); increases down a group (more shells, inner shells shield valence electrons).
- **Ionisation energy**: the energy required to remove the first electron from a gaseous atom. Increases across a period (stronger nuclear pull, smaller radius); decreases down a group (larger radius, valence electrons further from nucleus).
- **Electronegativity**: the ability of an atom to attract electrons in a covalent bond. Increases across a period; decreases down a group. Fluorine is the most electronegative element.

#### Chemical bonding

**Ionic bonding**: formed between a metal and a non-metal. The metal atom loses electrons (becoming a cation) and the non-metal atom gains electrons (becoming an anion). The resulting electrostatic attraction holds the ions in a lattice (e.g. NaCl). Properties: high melting point, soluble in water, conduct electricity when molten or dissolved.

**Covalent bonding**: formed between two non-metals. Atoms share electrons to achieve a stable electron configuration. Single, double and triple bonds exist (sharing 1, 2 or 3 electron pairs). Properties: low melting point (typically), insoluble in water (typically), do not conduct electricity.

**Metallic bonding**: formed in metals and alloys. Metal cations in a fixed lattice, surrounded by delocalised valence electrons ("sea of electrons"). Properties: high melting point (variable), malleable, ductile, conduct electricity and heat.

**Dative covalent bonding**: a special case where one atom contributes both electrons of a shared pair. Common in coordination compounds (e.g. [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺).

#### Shapes of molecules (VSEPR)

VSEPR (Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion) theory predicts the shape based on the number of electron pairs (bonding and lone) around the central atom.

| Electron pairs | Shape | Bond angle | Example |
|----------------|-------|------------|---------|
| 2 | Linear | 180° | BeCl₂, CO₂ |
| 3 | Trigonal planar | 120° | BF₃ |
| 4 | Tetrahedral | 109.5° | CH₄ |
| 3 bonding + 1 lone | Trigonal pyramidal | 107° | NH₃ |
| 2 bonding + 2 lone | Bent | 104.5° | H₂O |

Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs, so the angle between bonding pairs is slightly less than the geometric angle.

#### Intermolecular forces

Forces between molecules (weaker than covalent bonds).

- **Van der Waals (London dispersion)**: present in all molecules; weakest; arises from temporary dipoles.
- **Dipole-dipole**: present in polar molecules; arises from permanent dipoles.
- **Hydrogen bonding**: present when H is bonded to N, O or F; strongest of the three; gives water its high boiling point.

Effects: high boiling point for water, ammonia, hydrogen fluoride; solubility of polar solutes in polar solvents ("like dissolves like"); surface tension; high specific heat capacity.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Cross-topic links

- **III. Metals**: ionic and metallic bonding underpin metal extraction and properties.
- **IV. Acids and Bases**: dissociation of acids in water involves ionic bonding.
- **V. Fossil Fuels and Carbon Compounds**: covalent bonding of carbon is the basis of organic chemistry.

#### Exam technique

- **For atomic structure questions**: identify the element by atomic number and the isotope by mass number. State the number of protons, neutrons and electrons (consider any charge).
- **For bonding questions**: identify the elements; classify as metal/non-metal; determine the type of bond (ionic, covalent, metallic).
- **For shape questions**: count the electron pairs around the central atom; determine the VSEPR shape.
- **For intermolecular forces questions**: identify the molecule as polar/non-polar; determine which intermolecular forces are present.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Isotopes are radioactive." Most isotopes are stable; only some are radioactive (radioisotopes).
- "Ionic compounds conduct electricity." Solid ionic compounds do not conduct electricity; they conduct when molten or dissolved in water.
- "Hydrogen bonding is a chemical bond." Hydrogen bonding is an intermolecular force, not a chemical bond.
