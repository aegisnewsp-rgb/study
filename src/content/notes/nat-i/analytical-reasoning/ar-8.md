# Puzzles (Linear and Circular)

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A puzzle gives a fixed set of people (in most keys 4–7) and a list of positive/negative clues telling you who sits where, next to whom, or at which end. **Linear** arrangements place entities in a row with definite first/last positions; **circular** arrangements place them around a closed loop where only left/right neighbours matter.

- Anchor the diagram with any **exact-position clue** ("B is second from the left") first.
- Apply **negative clues** ("A is not next to D") to eliminate seating options.
- Resolve **either-or** clues by testing which case contradicts a definite clue.
- In **circular puzzles**, fix an absolute facing direction (e.g., facing the centre) before reading any left/right clue.
- For NAT-I (NTS), expect 2–4 MCQs of this type, ~30 seconds each.

| Arrangement type | Defining feature | Anchor clue style |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Linear | Two definite ends | "X is at the left end" |
| Circular | No ends; only neighbours | "X is to the immediate left of Y" |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### What makes a puzzle different from other reasoning items

A puzzle supplies a complete set of entities plus a closed list of constraints that together admit **one unique arrangement**. Every clue must be used at least once; if a clue is redundant, the setter rarely includes it. NAT-I items in standard papers use 5–7 entities to keep the search space manageable inside a 30-second window.

#### Building the diagram

Start by listing entities down the page. Mark any clue that names a specific seat ("A sits at the right end", "C is third from the left") and pencil that entity in. Then walk through the remaining clues in this order:

1. **Exact-position clues** — fix the seat.
2. **Neighbour clues** ("immediately left/right of") — fix the adjacent seat.
3. **Negative clues** ("is not next to", "does not sit at") — cross out impossible cells.
4. **Either-or clues** — hold two possibilities; reject the one that breaks a later definite clue.
5. **Conditional clues** ("if A is at an end, then B is next to C") — apply only after the condition is true.

#### Linear vs circular: the rules that diverge

| Feature | Linear row | Circular table |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Endpoints | Two (first, last) | None |
| "Left of" reference | Subject's left (facing the row) | Subject's left (facing the centre) |
| Absolute direction | Fixed | Must be fixed before reading clues |
| Opposite pairs | Not applicable | n/2 pairs (e.g., 3 pairs in 6 seats) |
| Equivalent diagrams | Unique reading | Two (rotated 180°) are the same |

#### Quick solving checklist

- Write down entities as **fixed-position** or **variable** before reading clues.
- Count remaining free seats after each anchor to avoid over-constraining.
- Re-read the question stem — the answer is in many papers a position, not the full chart.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge cases that break the simple model

- **Face-in vs face-out circular.** If all people face the table centre, their left is your right as you look down. If they face outwards, left and right flip. Mis-reading this is the single most common circular-puzzle error on NAT-I.
- **Opposite seats for even n greater than 6.** In an 8-person table, the person **4th** clockwise from A is opposite A, not 3rd. General rule: the k-th person clockwise is opposite A when k = n/2.
- **Combined sub-loops.** A 7-person puzzle can place 3 of them around a small inner circle while the other 4 sit in a row. Solve the inner circle first to free variables, then drop the block into the outer arrangement.
- **Either-or groups of three.** When "A is either at position 1 or at position 5" appears, treat each as a separate branch and discard the branch that later forces a contradiction.

#### Worked micro-example (5 people, circular, facing centre)

Clues: (i) B is immediately left of A. (ii) D is opposite A. (iii) C is not next to B. (iv) E is to the right of D.

Place A at seat 1, B at seat 5 (immediate left, facing centre). D opposite A → seat 4. E to the right of D → seat 3. C occupies the remaining seat 2. Check (iii): C (seat 2) is not adjacent to B (seat 5) — true. Unique arrangement confirmed.

#### Common mistake audit

| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Treating viewer's left as subject's left | Breaks every neighbour clue | Always declare facing direction first |
| Forgetting circular has no ends | Forces an impossible "first" | Use only neighbour relations |
| Locking either-or without re-checking | Misses a later contradiction | Hold both branches, discard after all clues |
| Miscounting opposite seat | Wrong n/2 calculation | n/2 rule, then verify |

#### Exam strategy for NAT-I

Puzzle items account for roughly 4% of the Analytical Reasoning MCQ block. Allocate **no more than 45 seconds per item**: draw a tiny circle, anchor one clue, and scan the options — most wrong answers fail the first negative clue you apply. If two options both survive, apply the *either-or* or *conditional* clue that the setter placed last in the list.

#### Practice prompts
1. Seven friends A–G sit around a circular table facing the centre. A is 2nd to the left of B; C is opposite G; D is immediately right of A. Who is opposite F?
2. Five people stand in a row. P is not at either end. Q is immediately to the left of R. S is at the right end. T is between P and Q. Find the person at the left end.
