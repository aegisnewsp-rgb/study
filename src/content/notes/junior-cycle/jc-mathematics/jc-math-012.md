# Algebraic Expressions, Formulae and Linear Equations — Junior Cycle Mathematics Notes

Algebra is the strand's language: an expression is a generalisation, an equation is a claim you can test, and a formula is an equation that already has one letter singled out for solving. The specification expects you to move freely between all three, and to explain why an algebraic step is valid rather than just performing it.

---

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Expressions

**Expanding** means removing the brackets. Multiply each term of the first bracket by every term of the second:

2*x*(3*x* + 5) = 6*x*² + 10*x*

**Factorising** is the reverse and it is assessed as a skill in its own right, not just as a means to solving a quadratic. Take out the common factor first, then look for a difference of two squares *a*² − *b*² = (*a* − *b*)(*a* + *b*) or *a*² + 2*ab* + *b*²* = (*a* + *b*)².

**Substitution** — replace the letter with the value and evaluate, keeping the order of operations intact. Two frequent losses: substituting in the middle of an expression rather than replacing first, and leaving brackets in when a negative value goes in, where −(*a* + *b*) ≠ −*a* + *b*.

**Identities** expand to *nothing*:

- (*a* + *b*)² = *a*² + 2*ab* + *b*²
- (*a* − *b*)² = *a*² − 2*ab* + *b*²
- (*a* + *b*)(*a* − *b*) = *a*² − *b*²

The middle term is where every error happens. Say the pattern out loud — "twice *a* times *b*" — before you write it.


#### Formulae

Rearranging a formula is solving for a required variable, and the order of operations applies in reverse: undo the last thing done.

*V* = π*r*² *h*, find *h*: divide both sides by π*r*².

Candidates typically divide by *h* instead, which rearranges in the wrong direction. The habit that fixes it is: ask *what has been done to *h* to produce *V*?* — multiply by π*r*² and by *h* — and undo those in reverse order.

Substituting into a formula then evaluating is a separate mark from rearranging it, and questions give both.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Linear equations

An equation is a balance; whatever you do to one side you do to the other. Write the operation out as you perform it.

The order that avoids almost every error:

1. Expand any brackets.
2. Collect like terms.
3. Move the *x* terms to one side and the numbers to the other.
4. Divide by the coefficient.

**Fractions in the answer are acceptable** and are not penalised — but leaving *2*x* + 8 = 3*x* as an "answer" to a question asking for *x* is not.

**Checking** is one substitution and it catches the errors above. Substitute your value back in and confirm both sides are equal. Where the question asks you to verify a solution, this substitution *is* the answer and it must be written out.


#### Simultaneous equations

Two equations, two unknowns, and the method decides itself from the coefficients.

**Substitution** when one equation is already arranged for a variable — and the exam's arrangement is a hint about which route to take.

**Elimination** when the coefficients nearly match: multiply or divide until they do, then add or subtract to remove one variable. Choose add when the coefficients have opposite signs and subtract when they have the same sign.

**Graphical** when the question wants a point read from a diagram, or as a check. It also tells you how many solutions exist: no intersection means no solution; one intersection means a unique solution; two intersections mean the equations are not linear.

**Interpreting the solution.** A pair gives a coordinate. If the question says "the pair represents the meeting point of the two lines", say so — naming what it represents is a marked outcome, not a restatement of the numbers.


#### Sequences in algebra

Generating terms from a rule, or building an expression for the *n*th term, connects here and to the Algebra strand's sequence outcomes:

- *n*th term of an arithmetic sequence: *uₙ* = *a* + (*n* − 1)*d*
- *n*th term of a geometric sequence: *uₙ* = *a r*ⁿ⁻¹*

The (*n* − 1) is the error that costs the most marks across the whole topic, because it gives a correct answer for the first term and a wrong one for every other.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Common mistakes

1. **The (*n* − 1)** in the general term of a sequence.
2. **The middle term in a squared bracket** — writing 2*ab* as 2*a* or as *ab*.
3. **Dropping the minus when a negative value is substituted** into a bracket.
4. **Rearranging in the wrong direction** — solving for the variable you were given rather than the one asked for.
5. **Not checking**, which costs two minutes and catches a sign error.
6. **Stopping at the intermediate step** on simultaneous equations, where the answer requires a coordinate pair and a sentence about what it means.

## Self-check

1. Expand and simplify (*x* + 5)(*x* − 2). *(*x*² − 2*x* + 5*x* − 10 = *x*² + 3*x* − 10. The middle term is *x*² − 2*x* + 5*x* − 10 before collection, and collecting like terms gives +3*x*.)*
2. Rearrange *F* = *ma* to find *a*. *(Divide both sides by *m*: *a* = *F*/*m*. Asking what has been done to *a* to produce *F* makes the direction unambiguous — *F* is *m* multiplied by *a*, so undo the multiplication by dividing.)*
3. Solve 3(*x* − 2) = 2*x* + 4. *(Expand to 3*x* − 6 = 2*x* + 4. Collect: *x* = 10. Check: 3(8) = 24 and 2(10) + 4 = 24. Both sides agree.)*
4. Two lines on a graph never meet. What does that tell you about the equations? *(There is no solution — the pair of lines is inconsistent, so no point (*x*, *y*) satisfies both simultaneously. Whether that shows as parallel lines or two coincident-looking lines with different gradients depends on the slopes.)*
5. A sequence starts 5, 8, 11, 14. Find *u*₅₀. *(*u*₅₀ = 5 + 49 × 3 = 152. Note 49, not 50 — the general term counts from the first term, so the (*n* − 1) offset is required.)*
