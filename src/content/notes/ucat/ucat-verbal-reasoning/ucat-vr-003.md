# Time management across 44 Verbal Reasoning questions in 22 minutes

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> The subtest is 44 items in 22 minutes. Budget per passage first; seconds per item second.

| Layer | The number to hold | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Whole subtest | 11 passages of 4 items each | The passage count is fixed, so the passage is the real unit |
| Per passage | About 1 minute 55 seconds | Eleven passages at that rate leaves a buffer inside the 22 minutes |
| Per item | Roughly 15 to 20 seconds | Only once the passage read has told you where to look |
| Never blank | There is no negative marking | An unanswered item is a mark lost with no upside |

**The habit.** Budget downwards as you go, not uniformly. Spending the same on the first passage as on the last is how candidates run out of clock on items that were still solvable.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

> The working picture — the split that fits the clock, and the three situations where you stop working on an item.

| Situation | The move | The cost |
|---|---|---|
| Stuck on a statement | Mark the best guess, flag it, move on after about 40 seconds | You will not solve it by staring |
| Keyword missing entirely | Answer Cannot Tell and move on after about 10 seconds | The passage is silent on the claim |
| Unusually dense passage | Take about 30 seconds more reading, about 5 seconds less per statement | The extra reading pays back across four items |
| Flagged item, first pass | Leave it and return with the rest of the passage fresh | Revisiting costs seconds, not minutes |

**Why the two-pass method wins.** Reading the passage once for shape, then jumping to the relevant sentence per statement, avoids re-reading the whole passage for every item. At roughly 30 seconds an item across the subtest, that saved re-read is the difference between finishing and guessing.


Verbal Reasoning gives you 44 questions in 22 minutes, with a 1 minute 30 second timed instruction section first. Working that out: roughly 30 seconds per question across the whole subtest, including the time you spend reading the passage. The UCAT is a timed test, not a comprehension test, and the candidates who score highest are usually the ones who treat time as a resource to budget rather than something to find.

> Source for the 44-item, 22-minute, 1:30 instruction section, 300–900 scale: UCAT Consortium, *Test Format*, https://www.ucat.ac.uk/about-ucat/test-format/. The UCAT verbal reasoning question tutorial is the official source for pacing advice and is best viewed on a desktop.

#### Why the budget is tight by design

The UCAT Consortium publishes 11 passages with four items each — 44 questions in total. Across 22 minutes of test time, that gives an average of 30 seconds per item, but the items are not equally timed. The first question on a passage costs more because you are still reading the passage. The third and fourth usually cost less because the passage is loaded. So the budget is uneven, and a candidate who tries to spend exactly 30 seconds on every item will run out of time on the early items and rush the later ones.

The fix is to budget the time in two layers: total minutes for the passage (including reading), and per-item seconds after that. A workable split on a four-item passage is:

- About 60 seconds reading the passage and identifying the keywords.
- About 90 seconds answering the four statements (roughly 22 seconds each).
- Total per passage: roughly 2 minutes 30 seconds.

Eleven passages at 2:30 each is 27:30 — over the 22-minute cap. So that split does not work. A tighter split is:

- About 45 seconds reading and skimming the passage.
- About 70 seconds answering the four statements.
- Total per passage: roughly 1 minute 55 seconds.

Eleven passages at 1:55 each is 21:05, which leaves a buffer of about a minute for the items that take longer. That buffer is what saves you when one passage turns out to be unusually dense.

#### The two-pass method

Most Verbal Reasoning candidates try to answer each statement in order, on the first read. That looks efficient but costs time, because you re-read the passage for almost every statement.

The two-pass method is faster:

1. **First pass — the passage read.** Skim the passage for shape: where the main claim sits, where the evidence sits, and where the qualifiers sit. Do not try to memorise exact figures. Note the two or three words you would scan for when the statements appear.
2. **Second pass — the statements.** For each statement, jump to the relevant sentence in the passage, read that sentence and the surrounding clause, decide True, False or Cannot Tell, and move on. The passage read took 45 seconds; each statement takes 15 to 20 seconds because you already know where to look.

This is faster than the alternative because you are not re-reading the whole passage for each statement. The UCAT's online interface keeps the passage on screen alongside the statements, so the jump is a quick scroll or a tab click.

#### When to skip, when to guess, when to come back

The UCAT has no negative marking, and the test is not adaptive — your performance on one item does not influence which item comes next. That means a guess is always worth as much as a confident wrong answer, and a skipped item is just a mark lost. So the rule is: never leave a Verbal Reasoning item blank.

That said, there are moments when skipping is the right call in the moment, provided you come back:

- **Skip-on-stuck.** If you have spent 40 seconds on a statement and the answer is not clear, mark your best guess, flag the item, and move on. You will not solve it by staring; you will solve it by coming back fresh after the rest of the passage.
- **Skip-on-keyword-miss.** If you cannot find the keyword in the passage at all within about 10 seconds, the answer is almost always Cannot Tell. Pick it and move on.
- **Skip-on-passage.** If the passage is unusually long or unusually technical, allow yourself 30 seconds of extra reading and 5 fewer seconds per statement. The trade-off is worth it.

The mistake is skipping without coming back. In the UCAT's on-screen interface, flagged items are easy to revisit; use that feature.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (3mo+)

> Pacing is a diagnostic tool, not just a time-saving trick. The review tells you which kind of error you actually have.

#### The arithmetic of the split is the argument for compressing
The note rules out the generous split first. About 60 seconds reading and about 90 seconds answering gives roughly 2 minutes 30 seconds a passage, and eleven of those is 27:30 — comfortably past the 22-minute cap. The tighter split of about 45 seconds reading and about 70 seconds answering gives roughly 1 minute 55 seconds, and eleven of those is 21:05, leaving a buffer of about a minute. The buffer is the point. It is not slack; it is the reserve that absorbs the one passage in a run that is denser than the rest.

#### Compressing reading time is a skill, not a shortcut
The worked drill shows the budget shrinking across four passages with 8 minutes left: 2:00, 2:00, 1:45 and 1:30, leaving a 45-second buffer. Reading time compresses because you stop reading for memorisation and start reading for shape, and answering time compresses because the keyword jump gets faster. A candidate whose reading time is over a minute is still trying to remember the passage, and the review log in this note is how that gets caught.

| Reading time per passage | What it indicates | What to change |
|---|---|---|
| Over a minute | Reading for memorisation | One pass only; note two keywords and move |
| Around 45 seconds | Reading for shape | Already at the target rate |
| Under 30 seconds | Skimming past qualifiers | Check that strength words are still being caught |

#### Flagged items are a diagnostic, not just a time-saver
The drill's review separates two outcomes for a flagged item. Getting it right on the second pass means the problem was pacing, not skill. Getting it wrong again means the problem is skill, and more time would never have helped. That distinction is worth building into every drill, because it decides whether your next fortnight of practice should be timed repetition or targeted work on quantifier slips and direction flips.

#### The last passage is worth attempting even at two minutes
The note's advice on running out of clock is not to skip the remaining passage but to compress it hard — read it flat, answer what you can, and pick the best answer for the rest. This follows from the marking scheme rather than from optimism: there is no penalty for a guess, so abandoning a passage converts a set of recoverable items into a certain zero. Panic-skipping is the one response that is strictly worse than the alternative.

#### Worked example — pacing across a 22-minute drill

Suppose you have four passages of average length left, with about 8 minutes on the clock.

- **Passage A** (4 statements): 50 seconds reading, 70 seconds answering. Total 2:00. Clock: 6:00 remaining.
- **Passage B** (4 statements): 45 seconds reading, 75 seconds answering. Total 2:00. Clock: 4:00 remaining.
- **Passage C** (4 statements): 40 seconds reading, 65 seconds answering. Total 1:45. Clock: 2:15 remaining.
- **Passage D** (4 statements): 30 seconds reading, 60 seconds answering. Total 1:30. Clock: 0:45 remaining.
- **Buffer**: 45 seconds to revisit flagged items.

The pattern that wins: spend a little less on each successive passage so that the final passage does not run out the clock. Reading time compresses as you warm up; answering time compresses as you internalise the keyword jump.

#### Common pacing traps

- **Reading the whole passage twice.** Once is enough. Reading it twice signals you did not read for shape the first time.
- **Re-reading the statement to "make it fit".** Statements are short. If the statement does not match the passage on a re-read, it was already wrong. Move on.
- **Equal time per statement.** Some statements take 5 seconds (Cannot Tell on a passage-silent claim). Some take 30 seconds (a direction or strength check). Allow the time per statement to vary.
- **Panic-skipping the last passage.** When the clock hits 2 minutes and you have a passage left, do not skip the passage. Read it in 30 seconds flat, answer the four statements in 60 seconds, and pick the best answer for the rest.
- **Not flagging.** The UCAT interface lets you flag items for review. Candidates who do not flag lose time on the items they got stuck on.

#### A 22-minute drill you can run this week

Use one of the official UCAT practice tests. Sit it under timed conditions, with the instruction section included. Then do three things in the review:

1. **Bucket every wrong answer.** Classify each wrong answer as a quantifier slip, a new-claim slip, a direction flip, or a time-pressure guess. The first three are skill errors; the last is a pacing error. Skill errors deserve more practice; pacing errors deserve more drills.
2. **Log your read time per passage.** For each passage, write down how long you spent reading and how long you spent answering. If your reading time is over a minute, you are reading for memorisation rather than for shape.
3. **Re-run the items you flagged.** If you flagged an item and got it right on the second pass, the problem was pacing, not skill. If you flagged an item and got it wrong on the second pass, the problem was skill.

#### What to do next

- Sit one timed Verbal Reasoning drill from the official UCAT practice tests with a hard 22-minute cap. Flag any item you spend more than 30 seconds on.
- After the drill, work out your average reading time per passage. If it is over 50 seconds, practise reading for shape only (one pass, with two keyword notes).
- Build a one-line pacing log per drill: read time per passage, answer time per item, flagged count, wrong count by category.
- Repeat the drill once a week for four weeks. The repetition is what turns the 30-second average into a reflex rather than a target.
