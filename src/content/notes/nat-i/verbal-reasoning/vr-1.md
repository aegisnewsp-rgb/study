# Analogies (Word Relationships)

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

An **analogy** question presents a pair of words (A:B) and asks you to find another pair (C:D) that shares the same logical relationship, formatted as *"A is to B as C is to D"* or *"A:B :: C:?"*. In the NAT-I Verbal Reasoning section, analogies usually carry 4–6 marks and appear in three formats: matching-pair (pick the option with the same relation), completion (A:B::C:?), and odd-one-out (find the pair that breaks the pattern). The fastest solving method is a four-step routine: (1) name the relation between A and B (cause-effect, part-whole, function, degree, etc.), (2) check the **direction** of that relation, (3) verify the **grammatical parallel** (noun must pair with noun, verb with verb, adjective with adjective), (4) test each option against the exact relation — not a merely similar one.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Relationship Types Tested in NAT-I
The verbal-reality of any analogy lives in its relation tag. Master these 12 categories because roughly 80% of NTS analogies use one of them:

- **Synonym / Antonym** — happy:joyful; hot:cold
- **Cause : Effect** and **Effect : Cause** — rain:flood; death:disease
- **Part : Whole** and **Whole : Part** — wheel:bicycle; library:book
- **Function / Use** and **Instrument / Action** — key:lock; pen:write
- **Worker : Tool** and **Tool : Worker** — carpenter:saw; saw:carpenter
- **Degree / Intensity** — warm:hot; whisper:shout
- **Sequence / Order** — spring:summer; dawn:dusk
- **Category : Member** and **Member : Category** — mammal:whale; whale:mammal
- **Characteristic** — lion:brave; dove:peace
- **Place** — panda:China; kangaroo:Australia
- **Material / Product** — ring:gold; furniture:wood
- **Gender / Young-One** — king:queen; cat:kitten

#### The Solving Method
**Step 1 — Decode the stem.** Read the capitalized pair A:B and write a sentence describing their link, e.g. *"A *is a part of* B"* or *"A *causes* B"*.

**Step 2 — Lock the direction.** Part:whole does **not** equal whole:part. Cause:effect does not equal effect:cause. Many wrong options flip the direction.

**Step 3 — Match grammar.** If the stem is noun:adjective (lion:brave), reject noun:noun options even if the meaning tempts you.

**Step 4 — Test options, not vibes.** Each option must satisfy the *same* relation tag, not a vaguely similar one. A multiple-relationship trap appears when two relations seem possible — pick the most specific, primary one the stem *directly* states.

#### Worked Example
Stem: **Doctor : Hospital :: Teacher : ?**
Relation = *profession:workplace*. Direction = worker-to-place. Grammar = noun:noun.
- (A) Student ✓ matches workplace only? No — student is *not* a workplace.
- (B) School ✓ profession-to-workplace — correct.
- (C) Book ✗ instrument/object, not workplace.
- (D) Class ✗ room, not the institution's name.

**Answer: (B) School.** Note that "Class" tempts students because of the *environment* relation — but Doctor:Hospital names the institution, not the room.

#### Typical NAT-I Formats
- *"Wheel is to bicycle as spoke is to ___"* (part:whole)
- *"Choose the pair that does NOT follow the same pattern."*
- *"Synonym is to antonym as analogous is to ___"*

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Traps

**1. Multiple-relationship ambiguity.** When a pair carries two plausible relations — e.g., *panda:China* could be "animal:habitat" *or* "animal:native country" — pick the relation the stem makes most explicit through surrounding words or capitalization cues. In NTS, the intended relation is always the **most specific**, not the most abstract.

**2. Grammatical drift.** A stem like *swift:swiftly* tests adverb-formation; *run:runner* tests agent-noun formation. Candidates must mirror the same derivation rule. Mixing *run:runs* (third-person verb) into a stem of *run:runner* (nominalization) is the classic decoy.

**3. Direction reversal.** Watch for stems phrased as *B is to A* — the option pair must keep the reversed direction. *Finger:hand* ≠ *hand:finger*; only the second matches if the stem is *hand:finger*.

**4. Symbol vs. literal.** *Dove:peace* is symbolic (a culturally coded symbol), not literal. Choosing *chicken:egg* would match the literal "produces" relation but break the symbolic tag.

#### Connection to Adjacent Topics
Analogies share the underlying skill of **semantic mapping** with **classification / odd-one-out** and **coding-decoding** items in the same Verbal Reasoning section. Training on analogies sharpens pattern recognition that lifts your score across all three sub-types. For students preparing for CSS or PMS alongside NAT-I, the same relation vocabulary transfers directly.

#### Common Mistakes
- Accepting a **synonymous** relation where the stem is **functional** (key:lock ≠ key:metal).
- Choosing a pair whose words are *thematic* (both about school) but whose *relation* differs from the stem.
- Ignoring the **case** of letters in the stem (capital vs. small often signals proper noun vs. common noun).
- Spending more than 45 seconds per analogy — NTS allows roughly one minute per Verbal Reasoning item.

#### Practice Prompts
1. **Stem:** *Painter : Brush :: Carpenter : ?* — Identify the relation tag, lock direction and grammar, then choose among (A) Saw (B) Wood (C) Hammer (D) Nail. *(Hint: worker:tool)*
2. **Stem:** *Whisper : Shout :: Warm : ?* — Pick the relation tag and complete with (A) Cool (B) Hot (C) Freeze (D) Lukewarm. *(Hint: degree/intensity ascending)*

Mastering the **relation tag + direction + grammar** triad will let you solve most NAT-I analogies in under 40 seconds, leaving time for the more calculation-heavy quantitative items later in the paper.
