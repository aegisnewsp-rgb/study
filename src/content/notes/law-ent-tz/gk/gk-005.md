# The Constitution of Tanzania

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

The **Constitution of the United Republic of Tanzania, 1977** is the supreme law that governs the Union of mainland Tanzania and the semi-autonomous Zanzibar. It is organised into **three tiers of government**: the Union Government, the Revolutionary Government of Zanzibar, and Local Government Authorities. The **President** is Head of State, Head of Government, and Commander-in-Chief. Fundamental rights and duties of citizens are codified in **Part III**, enforceable through the High Court and Court of Appeal. Amendments to entrenched clauses require a **two-thirds majority of all Members of Parliament**. The Constitution reflects principles of **sovereignty of the people**, the **rule of law**, and **constitutionalism**, drawing its origins from the 1965 Union Constitution of Tanganyika and Zanzibar. High-yield pointers: (1) know the three-tier structure; (2) remember the entrenched amendment threshold of two-thirds of all MPs; (3) distinguish justiciable Fundamental Rights from non-justiciable Directive Principles.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Structure of the State
The 1977 Constitution establishes a **three-tier federal-like structure**. The first tier is the **Union Government**, which holds powers over Union Matters enumerated in the Constitution, including defence, foreign affairs, and currency. The second tier is the **Revolutionary Government of Zanzibar**, exercising authority over non-Union matters in Zanzibar through its own House of Representatives. The third tier consists of **Local Government Authorities** (district, town, and city councils). Residual powers belong to the Union, and the Union Parliament may legislate on any matter for the whole territory.

#### Organs of Government and Separation of Powers
Power is distributed among three organs: the **Executive** (the President and Cabinet), the **Legislature** (Bunge, with a maximum of 393 MPs plus Zanzibar representatives), and the **Judiciary** (Court of Appeal, High Court, Resident Magistrates' Courts, District Courts, and primary courts). Each organ acts as a check on the others, embodying **separation of powers**. The **Attorney General** serves as principal legal adviser to the Government and is also the leader of the bar.

#### Citizenship and the Bill of Rights
Citizenship is acquired by **birth, descent, or registration** under the Tanzania Citizenship Act, 1995 (Cap. 357 R.E. 2002). Part III of the Constitution enumerates justiciable fundamental rights and freedoms — including the rights to life, equality, dignity, liberty, fair hearing, property, and freedom of expression — enforceable through the **High Court** under its fundamental rights jurisdiction, with appeals to the **Court of Appeal of Tanzania**.

#### Amendment Procedure and Constitutional Sources
The Constitution is a written instrument supplemented by **constitutional conventions** and historical documents such as the 1965 Articles of Union. Amendments follow either an ordinary procedure (simple majority) or an entrenched procedure requiring **two-thirds of all MPs** for specified provisions (such as those touching the Union structure, the Presidency, and the Bill of Rights).

#### Exam Pointers
Expect short factual questions on the date of enactment (1977), the three-tier structure, entrenched amendment thresholds, and the difference between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Directive Principles versus Fundamental Rights
**Directive Principles of State Policy** (Part II of the Constitution) are policy directives guiding the state in legislation and governance — for example, the obligation to pursue eradication of poverty, illiteracy, and disease. Unlike Fundamental Rights, they are **non-justiciable**, meaning a citizen cannot directly sue for their breach. Examiners commonly test this distinction; the trap answer is "Directive Principles are enforceable by courts."

#### Citizenship Edge Cases
Dual citizenship is restricted. A Tanzanian who voluntarily acquires citizenship of another country after 1995 ceases to be a Tanzanian citizen, subject to specified exceptions for citizens by birth. Citizenship by registration applies to non-citizens married to citizens for at least three years or to minors lawfully resident.

#### Judicial Independence and Constitutional Adjudication
**Security of tenure** for judges of the High Court and Court of Appeal is guaranteed — removal requires an address by the President supported by two-thirds of MPs on grounds of inability or misbehaviour. The **Court of Appeal** is the final appellate court on constitutional matters, and its interpretation is binding on all lower courts under the doctrine of stare decisis.

#### Connection to Adjacent Topics
The Constitution operates alongside the **Judicature and Application of Laws Act, Cap. 358 R.E. 2002**, which governs the residual application of received English laws in Tanzania. Local Government authorities derive powers from the **Local Government (District Authorities) Act, 1982** and the **Local Government (Urban Authorities) Act, 1982**, read together with the Constitution.

#### Common Mistakes
Candidates wrongly state that (a) the President appoints the Chief Justice without parliamentary scrutiny — in reality, the President acts on the advice of the Judicial Service Commission; (b) Directive Principles are legally binding — they are guiding principles only; (c) any law may be amended by a simple majority — entrenched provisions demand a two-thirds majority.

#### Practice Prompts
1. Examine the legal status of Directive Principles of State Policy and assess whether they should be made justiciable.
2. With reference to relevant constitutional provisions, discuss the extent to which the 1977 Constitution protects **judicial independence** in Tanzania.
