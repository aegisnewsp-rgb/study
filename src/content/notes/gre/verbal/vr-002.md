# Text Completion — GRE Verbal Reasoning Notes

Text Completion presents a short passage of one to five sentences with one, two or three words removed. Each blank carries three answer choices. The rule that decides most scores is simple and unforgiving: **every blank must be correct for the question to count**. A two-blank question with one right and one wrong scores zero, exactly like a completely blank answer. This is why Text Completion rewards a method rather than a vocabulary list.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

**Format facts worth memorising**

- Roughly **6 Text Completion questions per Verbal section**, mixed among about 10 Reading Comprehension and 4 Sentence Equivalence questions, inside a **27-minute** section.
- **One blank → 5 choices. Two blanks → 3 choices each. Three blanks → 3 choices each.**
- **No partial credit.** All blanks correct or the question scores nothing.
- Blank order is not solving order. In a three-blank question the **last blank is often the easiest**, because it usually carries the sentence's main verb.

**The single most useful habit**

Find the **pivot word** before you look at the choices. Words like *although*, *despite*, *however*, *because*, *since*, *moreover*, *paradoxically* and *rather than* tell you whether the blank must agree with or contradict the rest of the sentence. Solve the logic first; then choose the word.

**A 30-second worked pass**

> "Although the committee's report was ______ in tone, its recommendations were far-reaching."
>
> The pivot is *Although*, which sets up a contrast. The second half says the recommendations were far-reaching — ambitious, sweeping. So the first half must say the opposite of ambitious. The blank means *cautious* or *restrained*. Now read the choices and pick whichever means that; you never needed to consider the other two.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

**The four logic shapes that cover most questions**

1. **Contrast** — pivot words: although, though, despite, however, yet, nonetheless, whereas, on the other hand, rather than. The blank is the **opposite** of the clue.
2. **Support / continuation** — pivots: because, since, therefore, thus, hence, moreover, indeed, in fact, and. The blank **agrees** with the clue.
3. **Definition / restatement** — the sentence explains the blank in other words, often after a colon, dash or *that is*. The blank means the same as the explanation.
4. **Cause and effect** — one clause causes the other. Decide the direction of causation before choosing, because the same sentence read in the wrong direction produces a confidently wrong answer.

**Two-blank and three-blank strategy**

Work the **easiest blank first**, then let it constrain the rest. In two-blank questions, after fixing blank (i), ask which choice for blank (ii) is consistent with it — this eliminates most candidates in one step. In three-blank questions, read the whole sentence once for the overall argument, fix the blank you are surest of, and only then resolve the others. A choice that reads well in isolation but contradicts your solved blank is wrong by construction.

**Verdict discipline**

Every choice is a full English word doing work. Test each candidate by substituting it and re-reading the whole sentence — not just the clause around the blank. A word that fits its own clause but breaks the sentence's logic is the most common trap.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

**Where the marks actually leak**

- **Solving blanks out of order.** Candidates who fill blank (i) first in a three-blank question routinely lock in a reading that makes (iii) impossible. The sentence's main verb usually sits in the last blank — read backwards when the sentence is complex.
- **Ignoring the second blank's influence.** In a two-blank question the blanks constrain each other. A pair that is individually plausible but jointly incoherent is wrong.
- **Choosing the "fancy" word.** GRE distractors include rare words that fit grammatically but not logically. Rarity is not correctness; the logic test decides.
- **Missing a double pivot.** Sentences such as "While the theory is elegant, it is not *because* it is elegant that it was adopted" contain two pivots pulling in different directions. Parse each pivot separately rather than treating the sentence as one contrast.
- **Treating a colon as decoration.** A colon introduces an explanation. The clause after it is usually a definition of the blank, which makes colon sentences the fastest in the set.

**Vocabulary work that actually transfers**

Do not read an alphabetical word list. Build three small lists from your own wrong answers: (1) words you did not know at all, (2) words you knew approximately but not precisely — the dangerous group, (3) words you knew but whose *connotation* you misjudged. Group (2) is where scores improve fastest, because GRE distractors are usually near-synonyms distinguished by degree or by positive/negative charge. Record each word with the sentence that caught you, not in isolation.

**A four-week drill schedule**

- **Week 1 — logic only.** Take 20 questions and write the pivot word and the required direction for each blank *before* looking at the choices. No word selection at all. This isolates the skill the question is testing.
- **Week 2 — timed singles.** One-blank questions at 45 seconds each, to build speed on the easy tier.
- **Week 3 — multi-blank under time.** Two- and three-blank questions at 75–90 seconds each, with an error log recording which blank caused the failure.
- **Week 4 — full mixed sections.** 27-minute Verbal sections, so Text Completion competes with Reading Comprehension for time as it will on test day.
