# States of Matter

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**States of Matter** classifies the physical forms — solid, liquid, gas — by how closely particles pack and how strongly intermolecular forces hold them. The unifying equation for gases is the **ideal gas law**: **PV = nRT**, where P is pressure (Pa), V is volume (m³), n is moles, R = 8.314 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹, and T is temperature in **kelvin** (always convert °C to K by adding 273.15).

Key revision points:

- **Boyle's law**: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ at constant T (pressure and volume are inversely related).
- **Charles's law**: V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ at constant P (volume rises with absolute temperature).
- **Graham's law**: r₁/r2 = √(M₂/M₁) — lighter molecules diffuse faster.

Gases are highly compressible; solids are not. **Absolute zero** (0 K) is the theoretical floor where translational kinetic energy vanishes.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### The Three Common States

Solids hold a fixed shape and volume because particles vibrate about fixed positions in an ordered lattice. Liquids keep a fixed volume but take the shape of their container, since particles can slide past each other while remaining in contact. Gases fill the entire container — particles move freely with large gaps between them. The choice of state is governed by the balance of **kinetic energy** (∝ T) against **intermolecular forces** (London dispersion, dipole-dipole, hydrogen bonding).

#### Gas Laws and the Ideal Gas Equation

CUET UG usually tests these via a one-step numerical. The combined behaviour is captured by **PV = nRT**, which collapses to the individual laws when one variable is fixed.

| Law | Constant variable | Relationship |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Boyle's | Temperature | P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ |
| Charles's | Pressure | V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ |
| Gay-Lussac's | Volume | P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂ |
| Avogadro's | P and T | V ∝ n |

For mixtures, **Dalton's law** gives P_total = ΣP_i, where each P_i is the partial pressure of one component.

#### Real vs Ideal Gases

The **compressibility factor** Z = PV/nRT equals 1 for an ideal gas. At high pressure or low temperature, Z ≠ 1 because of finite molecular volume and intermolecular attractions; the **van der Waals equation** corrects this: (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT. Critical mistakes to avoid:

- Forgetting to convert °C → K (T must be absolute).
- Assuming vapour pressure depends on container volume — it depends only on the substance and temperature.
- Inverting Graham's law: heavier gases effuse **slower**, so r₁/r₂ = √(M₂/M₁).

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Beyond the Three States

CUET occasionally asks about **plasma** — an ionised gas of free electrons and nuclei, found in stars, neon signs, and lightning. At the opposite extreme, **Bose–Einstein condensates** form near 10⁻⁷ K when bosons collapse into a single quantum state. The average translational kinetic energy per molecule is **(3/2)k_BT**, with k_B = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K; this shows why **absolute zero** is a true lower limit — kinetic energy cannot go negative.

#### Worked Micro-Example

A 2.00 L flask at 27 °C contains 0.500 mol of N₂. What pressure does it exert? Convert T = 300.15 K, then P = nRT/V = (0.500)(8.314)(300.15)/(2.00 × 10⁻³) ≈ 6.24 × 10⁵ Pa ≈ 6.16 atm. This is well within ideal-gas range because 300 K is far above N₂'s critical temperature (126 K).

#### Common Mistakes and Exam Strategy

| Trap | Correction |
| --- | --- |
| Using 22.4 L at 25 °C | 22.4 L is the molar volume at **STP** (0 °C, 1 atm); at SATP it is 22.7 L |
| Saying gases have no intermolecular forces | Forces are weak but non-zero — that's why real gases deviate |
| Mixing Boyle's and Gay-Lussac's | Boyle's fixes T; Gay-Lussac's fixes V |
| Putting lighter gas on the wrong side of Graham's law | r ∝ 1/√M, so light gas has the larger rate |

**Exam strategy**: this topic carries ~3% in CUET UG Chemistry, typically one MCQ drawn directly from NCERT Class 11 Chapter 5. Practise unit conversions (kPa ↔ atm, °C ↔ K) and the combined gas law P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂, since most numericals are single-step rearrangements of PV = nRT.
