# True, False and Cannot Tell — UCAT Verbal Reasoning

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> One rule decides this subtest: the passage is the only evidence, and every load-bearing word has to match.

| Answer | Choose it when | The usual cause of a wrong pick |
|---|---|---|
| True | The passage supports every part of the statement | You filled a gap with real-world knowledge |
| False | One part of the statement is contradicted | You softened a strength the passage does not claim |
| Cannot Tell | No part is confirmed and no part is contradicted | You treated silence as agreement |

**The habit.** If you cannot point at the sentence that supports a True answer, it is not True. The subtest budget is 44 items in 22 minutes, so this check has to run in seconds rather than be deferred.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

> The working picture — the words that flip an answer, and the four trap shapes they build.

| Trap shape | What it looks like | Correct answer |
|---|---|---|
| Stronger word | Passage says usually, often or some; statement says always, all or never | False |
| New claim | Statement adds a comparison, ranking or prediction the passage never makes | Cannot Tell |
| Direction flip | Same words, reversed relationship | False |
| Half true | Partly supported, partly contradicted | False, because the whole statement has to hold |

**Where the marks actually leak.** Candidates over-use True and under-use Cannot Tell. The instinct to treat a plausible statement as a supported one is the largest single source of lost marks, and the passage tells you nothing about whether the world agrees with it.


Verbal Reasoning gives you 44 questions in 22 minutes. That works out to roughly 30 seconds per item, so the subtest measures reading speed and judgement under pressure, not comprehension in the abstract. Every statement after a passage is judged against the passage alone. Outside knowledge has to stay out, even when the topic is one you happen to know well, because the marker scores the match between statement and passage, not between statement and reality.

The format is fixed. Each question is a statement, and the three answers are:

- **True** — the passage states the statement, or the statement follows unambiguously from what the passage states.
- **False** — the passage states the opposite of the statement, or contains information that makes the statement impossible.
- **Cannot Tell** — the passage neither confirms nor denies the statement. There is not enough information in the passage to judge.

> Source for the published subtest size, time and scoring (300–900 scale): UCAT Consortium, *Test Format*, https://www.ucat.ac.uk/about-ucat/test-format/. The wording above is the operational form the UCAT tutorials use.

#### The one rule that decides most items

Read every statement word for word, and check every load-bearing word against the passage. A statement is **True** only when the passage supports every part of it. A statement is **False** as soon as one part is contradicted. A statement is **Cannot Tell** when no part is contradicted and no part is confirmed.

The reason this matters: the trap words that flip an answer from True to False, or from True to Cannot Tell, are one or two quantifiers or modals. *All*, *some*, *many*, *most*, *always*, *usually*, *never*, *none*, *only*, *may*, *will*. If the passage says "usually" and the statement says "always", the statement is False. If the passage says "some" and the statement says "most", the statement is False. If the passage says nothing about the comparison and the statement adds new information, the statement is Cannot Tell.

#### Why candidates under-use Cannot Tell

Most candidates pick True too readily, and lose marks because of it. The natural instinct is to treat any statement that sounds plausible as True. But the marker scores a True answer only when the passage backs it. Plausibility is irrelevant. If you find yourself choosing True because the statement "feels right" and you cannot point to the sentence in the passage that supports it, change the answer to Cannot Tell.

A second pattern is the reverse: choosing False whenever a statement is unverified. False is wrong if the passage simply does not mention the point. Cannot Tell is the correct answer in that case, not False.

#### Step-by-step technique

1. Read the stem and underline every quantifier, modal and comparison. Words that look small in print do most of the work in this subtest.
2. Scan the passage for the keyword in the stem. The keyword is a noun phrase that repeats across the statements, such as "the regulator" or "the pilot scheme".
3. Read the one or two sentences around the keyword in full. Read past the keyword to the end of the clause, because the qualifying phrase sits after it.
4. Match the statement against the passage sentence by sentence. For each load-bearing word in the statement, ask: does the passage use the same word, a stronger word, or a weaker word?
5. Decide True, False or Cannot Tell from that match alone. If any load-bearing word in the statement is unsupported, the answer is either False (if the passage contradicts it) or Cannot Tell (if the passage is silent on it).

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (3mo+)

> The subtest is scored on a match, not on comprehension. Knowing the topic does not help you.

#### The beach passage contains every trap at once
A 2024 review of UK coastal water quality found 27 of 50 sampled beaches above the safe-bathing threshold at least once, and covered only beaches with designated monitoring stations. Four of the five statements written against it turn on a different mechanism. The count statement is True because the passage supplies the figure. The rainfall statement is False because the passage says heavy rainfall was associated with the worst readings. The coverage statement is False because "only" in the passage is doing the work. The harbour statement is Cannot Tell because the passage says a comparable index is not published, which is a fact about reporting and not about water quality. One passage, four distinct mechanisms.

#### "May have" is a weak claim, not an absent one
The trap in that fourth statement is worth isolating, because candidates who read it correctly still answer it wrongly. A weak modal claim is still a claim. The passage is simply silent on whether unmonitored harbours have poorer water quality, and silence produces Cannot Tell however cautiously the statement is hedged. Hedging does not buy support; only a passage sentence does.

#### The trap words are a short, fixed list
The words that decide outcomes cluster into two small groups: quantity words such as all, some, many and most, and certainty words such as always, usually, never and none, alongside only, may and will. Learning the groups is worth more than memorising worked examples, because every statement here is decided by a substitution drawn from them. Underline them in the stem, find the same word in the passage, and compare strength in one motion. At a budget of roughly 30 seconds an item, that motion has to be automatic.

| What the passage says | What the statement says | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| "usually" | "always" | False — strength upgraded |
| "some" | "most" | False — strength upgraded |
| No comparison made | A new ranking added | Cannot Tell — new claim |
| "reviewed only monitored beaches" | "All UK beaches were included" | False — the qualifier was dropped |
| A figure is given outright | The same figure restated | True |

#### Three answers, three different questions about the passage
True asks whether the passage supports the statement. False asks whether the passage contradicts it. Cannot Tell asks whether the passage is silent. Treating them as one question with two settings is what produces the under-use of Cannot Tell, because a silence feels like neither support nor contradiction until you notice it is a third case. Naming which question you are asking is the fastest route to a consistent answer.

#### Worked example

Passage:

> A 2024 review of UK coastal water quality found that 27 of 50 sampled beaches exceeded the Environment Agency's safe-bathing threshold for enterococci at least once during the summer season. The review covered only beaches with designated monitoring stations. Heavy rainfall in June was associated with the worst readings. The Environment Agency publishes weekly bathing-water classifications for monitored beaches, but does not currently publish a comparable index for harbours, marinas or estuarine sites.

Statements:

1. "More than half of the monitored beaches in the review exceeded the safe-bathing threshold at least once." — **True**. 27 of 50 is more than half, and the passage says "27 of 50".
2. "Heavy rainfall improves coastal water quality." — **False**. The passage says heavy rainfall was associated with the worst readings.
3. "All UK beaches were included in the 2024 review." — **False**. The review covered only beaches with designated monitoring stations, so beaches without monitoring stations were excluded.
4. "Unmonitored harbours may have poorer water quality than monitored beaches." — **Cannot Tell**. The passage notes that comparable data for harbours is not published, but says nothing about the actual water quality of those harbours.
5. "The Environment Agency publishes weekly water-quality data for monitored beaches." — **True**. The passage says the agency publishes weekly bathing-water classifications for monitored beaches.

The trap in statement 4 is the one candidates miss most. The passage tells you a comparable index is not published for harbours; it does not tell you anything about what the readings would be if it were. "May have poorer" is a weak claim, but the passage is silent on it, so the strict answer is Cannot Tell.

#### Examiner traps to look out for

- **Stronger-word statements.** A statement that swaps a softer word from the passage (usually, often, some) for a stronger word (always, all, never) is False.
- **New-claim statements.** A statement that introduces a comparison, ranking or prediction that the passage does not make is Cannot Tell.
- **Direction-flip statements.** A statement that keeps the words but inverts the direction of the passage's claim is False.
- **Half-true statements.** A statement that is partly supported and partly contradicted is False, not True. The whole statement has to hold.

#### Common mistakes to correct now

- Bringing in real-world knowledge. If the passage is about a topic you have studied elsewhere, ignore that knowledge and answer from the passage.
- Treating "True" as a vote for plausibility. The marker scores the match, not your confidence.
- Skipping Cannot Tell because it feels like a cop-out. Cannot Tell is the right answer when the passage is silent.
- Reading only the keyword sentence. The qualifying phrase sits later in the same clause.

#### Practice drill for the next study hour

Take any four-paragraph press release or summary of a research paper, write six statements against it, and label each one yourself before you check. Force at least one statement per passage to be Cannot Tell. The point of the drill is to train the instinct that an unmentioned claim is Cannot Tell, not True.

#### What to do next

- Sit one Verbal Reasoning drill from the official UCAT practice tests, timed, with a hard 22-minute cut-off.
- Review every wrong answer and write one line on whether the mistake was a quantifier slip, a new-claim slip, or a direction flip.
- Re-read the stem of each True answer and confirm, in writing, the exact passage sentence that supports it.
- Build a short "trap word" list (always, all, only, never, some, may, will) and re-check the list against every statement before you commit the answer.
