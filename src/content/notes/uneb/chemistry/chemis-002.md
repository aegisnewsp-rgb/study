# The Atom Contains Three Sub-atomic Particles Protons Charge

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

The atom contains three sub-atomic particles: **protons** (charge +1, found in the nucleus), **neutrons** (no charge, also in the nucleus), and **electrons** (charge −1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, orbiting the nucleus in shells). Atomic number **Z** counts the protons and defines the element, while mass number **A** sums protons plus neutrons.

- **A = protons + neutrons**, therefore **N = A − Z**
- Isotopes share the same Z but differ in A, giving nearly identical chemistry but different masses
- Bohr shells fill as **2, 8, 8, 18** (then 32 for higher shells) following the Aufbau order

**Exam pointers:** expect MCQs on identifying particle counts from nuclide symbols, drawing electron arrangements for Z = 1–20, and stating one periodic trend (e.g. ionization energy across Period 3).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Sub-atomic particles and nuclear structure

The modern atom is mostly empty space with a tiny, dense nucleus. J.J. Thomson's cathode-ray experiment identified the electron, Rutherford's gold-foil experiment (1911) revealed the nucleus by deflecting α-particles, and Chadwick confirmed the neutron in 1932. A nuclide is written as $^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$, where X is the element symbol. The number of neutrons is found by subtraction: **N = A − Z**.

| Particle | Charge (C) | Mass (u) | Location |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Proton | +1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ | ≈ 1 | Nucleus |
| Neutron | 0 | ≈ 1 | Nucleus |
| Electron | −1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ | ≈ 1/1836 | Shells |

#### Isotopes and relative atomic mass

Isotopes of an element have the same Z but different neutron counts. They occupy the same position in the periodic table and undergo the same reactions; only physical properties such as density and diffusion rate differ. Chlorine, for instance, is a 75 : 25 mixture of $^{35}\text{Cl}$ and $^{37}\text{Cl}$, producing an **A_r** of 35.5.

#### Electronic configuration rules

Electrons fill shells in the order **1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d**, governed by the Aufbau principle. For elements Z = 1–20, the simpler **2, 8, 8, 2** rule applies: K holds 2, L holds 8, M holds 8 (or 18 once d-orbitals appear).

#### Periodic trends

| Property | Across a period (left → right) | Down a group (top → bottom) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Atomic radius | Decreases | Increases |
| Ionization energy | Increases (with dips at Group 2 → 13 and Group 15 → 16) | Decreases |
| Electronegativity | Increases | Decreases |
| Metallic character | Decreases | Increases |

Common errors to avoid:

- Treating A as if measured in grams — it is a pure count
- Writing calcium as 2,8,8,2 instead of 2,8,8,8,2 (Ca, Z = 20)
- Assuming isotopes behave differently in reactions

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Worked example — neutrons and electrons in an ion

For $^{27}_{13}\text{Al}^{3+}$:
1. Protons = Z = 13
2. Neutrons = A − Z = 27 − 13 = 14
3. Electrons in neutral Al = 13; the 3+ charge removes 3, leaving **10 electrons** arranged **2, 8**
4. Outer shell electrons (for bonding reasoning) come from neutral Al: 2,8,3 → **3 valence electrons**

This single problem tests four skills in one UACE Paper 2 question: nuclear arithmetic, ion formation, shell filling, and valency.

#### Edge cases and trap questions

- **Group numbers vs valence electrons:** for main-group elements the group number equals the valence count, but for transition metals (Group 3–12) and noble gases the relationship is indirect — always read the actual electron arrangement.
- **A_r is not the mass of one atom:** it is a weighted average of isotopes, often non-integer (e.g. Mg = 24.3).
- **First ionization energy anomalies:** the drop from Be to B and from N to O occurs because the new electron enters a higher-energy p-orbital or pairs in an orbital, increasing electron–electron repulsion.

#### Connections to later topics

The shell structure taught here becomes the basis for ionic and covalent bonding (Topic 3), redox electron transfer, and the shapes of s-, p-, and d-orbitals relevant to transition-metal chemistry in Paper 2. Periodic trends in ionization energy and electronegativity reappear whenever reaction feasibility is discussed.

#### Practice prompts

1. The element X has $^{70}_{31}\text{X}$ and $^{72}_{31}\text{X}$ as its only naturally occurring isotopes, with abundances 60 % and 40 %. Calculate A_r to one decimal place and state the number of neutrons in each isotope.
2. Write the electron arrangement of sulfur (Z = 16) and explain, using your arrangement, why the first ionization energy of sulfur is lower than that of phosphorus.
