# Amines, Amino Acids and Proteins

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Amines** are organic derivatives of ammonia (NH₃) where one or more H atoms are replaced by alkyl (R) or aryl (Ar) groups. **Amino acids** carry both an –NH₂ and a –COOH on the same α-carbon and link via peptide bonds into **proteins**.

| Class | General formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Primary amine (1°) | R–NH₂ | Methanamine, CH₃NH₂ |
| Secondary amine (2°) | R–NH–R | Dimethylamine, (CH₃)₂NH |
| Tertiary amine (3°) | R₃N | Trimethylamine, (CH₃)₃N |
| α-Amino acid | H₂N–CHR–COOH | Glycine, H₂N–CH₂–COOH |

- Peptide bond: –CO–NH– linkage formed by condensation; each bond loses H₂O (Mᵣ = 18 g/mol).
- Water released when **n** amino acids polymerise = **(n − 1)** molecules.
- Biuret test = violet colour for polypeptides; ninhydrin test = blue-purple for α-amino acids.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"1°, 2°, 3° = number of carbons on N"** (one, two, three R groups); **"Bi-U-RET needs U (two) peptide bonds"** — dipeptides stay negative, proteins turn violet.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Classes and Basicity of Amines
Aliphatic amines are bases because the nitrogen lone pair accepts a proton: R–NH₂ + HCl → R–NH₃⁺Cl⁻. In aqueous solution the observed basicity order is 2° > 1° > 3° > NH₃, set by inductive donation versus steric shielding of the lone pair. Aromatic amines (e.g. phenylamine/C₆H₅NH₂) are weaker bases because the lone pair delocalises into the benzene ring.

The base dissociation constant is Kb = [R–NH₃⁺][OH⁻]/[R–NH₂] (dimensionless at 25 °C/298 K). For the conjugate acid, pKa + pKb = 14 at 298 K.

#### Preparation of Primary Amines
- **Reduction of nitro compounds:** R–NO₂ + 6[H] → R–NH₂ + 2H₂O (Sn/HCl, Fe/HCl, or H₂/Ni).
- **Hofmann degradation:** R–CONH₂ + Br₂ + 4NaOH → R–NH₂ + Na₂CO₃ + 2NaBr + 2H₂O. The product has **one fewer carbon** than the amide.

#### Amino Acid Structure and the Zwitterion
An α-amino acid has the form H₂N–CHR–COOH. In aqueous solution the –COOH donates a proton to the –NH₂, producing a **zwitterion** (dipolar ion): H₃N⁺–CHR–COO⁻. At the **isoelectric point (pI)** the molecule has zero net charge and will not migrate in electrophoresis. Glycine pI ≈ 6.0; aspartic acid pI ≈ 2.8; lysine pI ≈ 9.7.

#### Peptide Bond and Polypeptides
Condensation of two amino acids:

H₂N–CHR¹–COOH + H₂N–CHR²–COOH → H₂N–CHR¹–CO–NH–CHR²–COOH + H₂O

The amide linkage –CO–NH– is the **peptide bond**. With n amino acid residues, the polypeptide mass ≈ Σ(residue masses) − 18(n − 1) g/mol.

#### Protein Structure Levels
1. **Primary** — sequence of residues held by peptide bonds.
2. **Secondary** — α-helix or β-pleated sheet, H-bonded between backbone C=O and N–H.
3. **Tertiary** — 3-D folding by R-group interactions (H-bonds, ionic, disulphide bridges –S–S–, hydrophobic).
4. **Quaternary** — association of polypeptide subunits (e.g. haemoglobin, four chains).

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Feature | Amine (R–NH₂) | Amide (R–CO–NH₂) | Amino acid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functional group | –NH₂ on alkyl/aryl | –CO–NH₂ (acyl-amine) | Both –NH₂ and –COOH |
| Basicity | Strong base (aliphatic) | Very weak (lone pair delocalised into C=O) | Amphoteric — exists as zwitterion |
| Reaction with HCl | Forms ammonium salt | Generally no salt formation | Protonates –COO⁻ |
| Typical test | Carbylamine for 1°; HNO₂ for 1°/2°/3° | Hydrolysed by acid/alkali to acid + amine | Ninhydrin → blue-purple |

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** A tripeptide was formed from three different α-amino acids of average residue mass 110 g/mol. Calculate (a) the number of peptide bonds present, (b) the number of water molecules released during its formation, and (c) the relative molecular mass of the tripeptide. **[NECO SSCE, 4 marks]**

#### Solution:

(a) Number of peptide bonds in n amino acids = (n − 1) = 3 − 1 = **2 bonds**.

(b) Water molecules released = (n − 1) = **2 molecules** (each peptide bond forms by losing one H₂O of Mᵣ 18 g/mol).

(c) Mᵣ(tripeptide) = 3 × 110 − 18 × (3 − 1) = 330 − 36 = **294 g/mol**.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Many candidates write "(n − 1)" but then apply it as "(n − 2)" when subtracting water mass. The two formulas share the **same index** (n − 1) — number of bonds **equals** number of waters lost.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases in Amine Chemistry
- **Quaternary ammonium ions** (R₄N⁺) carry a permanent positive charge and have no N–H bond; they cannot donate H-bonds and act as phase-transfer catalysts.
- **Direct alkylation of ammonia** with R–X produces a mixture of 1°, 2°, 3° amines and quaternary salt — never a clean single product, which is why industrial routes prefer the Hofmann degradation.
- **Aromatic diazonium salts** (Ar–N₂⁺Cl⁻) are stable below 5 °C and couple with phenols/naphthols to form azo dyes; aliphatic diazonium salts decompose immediately, releasing N₂ and a carbocation.

#### Advanced Protein Traps and Exceptions

| Trap | Reality | Memory cue |
|---|---|---|
| Denaturation breaks peptide bonds | False — only 2°, 3°, 4° structure disrupted; peptide bonds remain intact | "Heat unknots, it does not cut" |
| Biuret positive for all amino acids | False — needs ≥ 2 peptide bonds (tripeptides onward) | "Bi-U-RET = bi = two" |
| Acid hydrolysis recovers every residue | Tryptophan is destroyed; asparagine/glutamine lose amide side chains | "Acid hates tryptophan" |
| Hofmann keeps the carbon count | False — amide carbonyl carbon leaves as carbonate | "Hofmann eats one C" |
| Isoelectric point = titration endpoint | False — pI is intrinsic; endpoint depends on indicator/assistance used | "pI is a property, not a volume" |

#### Reactions Distinguishing 1°, 2° and 3° Amines
- With **HNO₂ (NaNO₂/HCl, < 5 °C):** 1° aliphatic → N₂ + alcohol + carbocation (vigorous); 1° aromatic → stable Ar–N₂⁺Cl⁻; 2° → N-nitrosamine (yellow oil); 3° → generally no useful product.
- With **ethanoyl chloride (CH₃COCl):** 1° and 2° form N-substituted amides; 3° does not react (no N–H to substitute).
- **Carbylamine test:** 1° only, on warming with CHCl₃ + alcoholic KOH, gives a foul-smelling isocyanide R–NC.

#### Extended Worked Hook
A polypeptide contains 75 amino acid residues of average residue mass 120 g/mol. (i) State the number of peptide bonds. (ii) Calculate the molar mass of the polypeptide. (iii) If 0.01 mol of this protein is completely hydrolysed, what mass of water is consumed?
- (i) Bonds = 75 − 1 = 74.
- (ii) Mᵣ = 75 × 120 − 18 × 74 = 9000 − 1332 = 7668 g/mol.
- (iii) Moles of H₂O = moles of bonds = 74 × 0.01 = 0.74 mol; mass = 0.74 × 18 = **13.32 g**.

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics
- Links to **Carboxylic Acids** (carboxyl reactivity), **Amides** (peptide bond is an internal amide), **Isomerism** (α-carbon chirality, L/D isomers), and **Hydrolysis** (acid/alkaline hydrolysis of esters parallels protein hydrolysis).
- NECO frequently combines this topic with **soap/detergent chemistry** under organic short-answer questions; expect an essay part asking for differences between hydrolytic change and denaturation.
