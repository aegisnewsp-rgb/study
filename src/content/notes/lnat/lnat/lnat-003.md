# Section A — Eliminating Wrong Options — LNAT Notes

Each Section A question offers four possible answers, and the most reliable technique on a speed test is elimination rather than selection. Most wrong options fail in one of four recognisable ways: they overstate a hedge the passage deliberately left open, they swap a cause for an effect, they swap a comparison for an absolute, or they import a fact from outside the passage. Train on the official practice test until you can name which of those failures you are looking at, because naming the failure is what stops you re-reading the same two options for a minute and a half.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### The four named failure modes

- **Overstated hedge.** The passage uses an open word; the option uses a closed one. The hedge is the part the author chose to leave open. An option that closes the hedge is wrong even when the underlying claim is roughly right.
- **Swapped cause and effect.** The passage says A leads to B; the option says B leads to A, or that B causes A. The two claims are not equivalent. Section A will frequently present the swapped version as the wrong option.
- **Swapped comparison for absolute.** The passage says "more than X"; the option says "the most". The comparison survives only inside the comparison the passage actually made.
- **Imported outside fact.** The option is true in the world but the passage never gave it. Section A is marked against the printed words, not against general knowledge.

#### Why elimination beats selection

Selection asks: which of these four is right? You have to defend the right one against three plausible alternatives — which is what makes selection slow.

Elimination asks: which of these four is wrong? You have to find one named failure in three of the four. The fourth option is right by elimination, and you do not need to defend it directly. Under the 95-minute wall across 42 questions, the difference in time per question is the difference between finishing and not finishing.

#### The two-second check

Before you commit to an option, run this check in two seconds:

1. **Does the option say more than the passage?** If yes, it is overstating. Look for the strongest word in the option and the strongest word in the passage — they should match.
2. **Does the option swap a direction?** If yes, it is swapped cause/effect or swapped comparison. Find the direction in the passage and check the option is the same direction.
3. **Does the option need outside knowledge?** If yes, it is importing. Mark it out unless the passage itself supplies the fact.

If three of the four options fail one of those checks, you have your answer.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### A table of named failure modes

The four modes cover the majority of Section A wrong options. Use the table as a checklist while you are reading the options.

| Failure mode | What the passage does | What the option does | Diagnostic word pair |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overstated hedge | "often", "may", "tends to", "in many cases" | "always", "is", "all", "every" | often ↔ always |
| Swapped cause and effect | A causes B | B causes A | "because of" / "leads to" reversed |
| Swapped comparison for absolute | "more than X", "less than Y" | "the most", "the least", "the only" | more than ↔ the most |
| Imported outside fact | Silent on the point | States a fact in the world | Option needs knowledge outside the passage |

#### Worked example: spotting the failure mode

**Passage C.** *Most of the recorded decline in UK high-street bookshop numbers over the last twenty years happened in the first half of that period, before the rise of the e-reader. The remaining decline has been smaller, and many independent bookshops that survived the first wave have continued to trade. The pattern is consistent with a one-off structural shock rather than a steady downward trend, and the recent data suggests the population of independent bookshops has stabilised.*

**Q6.** Which of the following is the best summary of the passage?

A. The number of UK high-street bookshops is now falling steadily.
B. The decline in UK high-street bookshops has slowed and may have stopped.
C. Independent bookshops are now growing in number.
D. The decline of UK high-street bookshops was caused by e-readers.

**Reasoning.** Run the table against each option.

- **A.** Says the number is "now falling steadily." The passage says the recent data "suggests the population… has stabilised." That is a swapped comparison for absolute — "steady decline" replaces "one-off shock followed by stabilisation." Also overstated hedge: "steady" contradicts "stabilised". Mark out.
- **B.** The passage says the remaining decline has been smaller and the recent data suggests stabilisation; option B restates that with an open word of its own, matching the passage's open word. No swapped direction. No imported fact. Keep.
- **C.** Says bookshops "are now growing in number." The passage says "stabilised" — flat, not growing. Overstated hedge in the opposite direction. Mark out.
- **D.** Says the decline "was caused by e-readers." The passage says the first half of the decline happened *before* the rise of the e-reader. Imported outside fact (and a factual contradiction). Mark out.

By elimination, **B** is the only option that survives. Notice that the table did not pick B; it eliminated A, C and D. B is right by elimination, not by direct defence.

#### Worked example: when two options both look defensible

**Passage D.** *A recent study found that students who took a short break between secondary school and university performed slightly better in first-year assessments than students who went straight through. The author of the study was careful to point out that the gap was small and that students who took a gap year were a self-selected group, so the result cannot be read as evidence that a gap year causes better performance.*

**Q7.** Which of the following is the author most likely to agree with?

A. Students who take a gap year tend to do better at university.
B. The study shows that taking a gap year improves university performance.
C. The study does not show that taking a gap year causes better performance.
D. Students should be encouraged to take a gap year before university.

**Reasoning.** Run the table.

- **A.** The author says the gap was small and the group was self-selected. "Tends to do better" overstates the hedge the author placed around the result. Mark out.
- **B.** The author explicitly says the result "cannot be read as evidence" of causation. Option B says the study shows causation. Direct contradiction. Mark out.
- **C.** The author says the result cannot be read as evidence of causation. Option C restates that. Keep.
- **D.** The author does not make a recommendation. Imported outside fact (a value claim the author did not make). Mark out.

Answer: **C**. Note again that B is right by elimination, not by direct defence — C is the option that does not overstate, swap or import.

#### Worked example: the import trap

**Passage E.** *The Human Rights Act 1998 requires UK courts to read legislation, so far as possible, in a way that is compatible with the rights defined in the European Convention on Human Rights. Courts cannot strike down an Act of Parliament; they can only issue a declaration of incompatibility, which Parliament may then choose to address.*

**Q8.** According to the passage, when UK courts find legislation incompatible with the Convention rights, they

A. can strike down the Act of Parliament containing the legislation.
B. must issue a declaration of incompatibility.
C. may issue a declaration of incompatibility, which Parliament may then address.
D. will refer the legislation to the European Court of Human Rights.

**Reasoning.** Run the table.

- **A.** The passage says courts "cannot strike down an Act of Parliament". Direct contradiction. Mark out.
- **B.** The passage says they "can only issue a declaration of incompatibility". "Must" is stronger than "can only". The hedge is wrong. Mark out.
- **C.** Matches the passage word for word on the declaration of incompatibility and on who decides next. Keep.
- **D.** The passage says nothing about the European Court of Human Rights. Imported outside fact. Mark out.

Answer: **C**. Notice B vs C: the diagnostic pair is a strong modal ("must") against a weak one — the passage keeps the decision open, option B closes it. Option B is the overstated hedge.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Why naming the failure stops the re-read trap

The single most expensive failure on Section A is the 90-second re-read loop. You have narrowed it to two options, you read the passage again, you narrow again, you read again. By the third loop you have spent more time on one question than the budget for two passages.

The reason the loop starts is that the two options both look defensible. The fix is to stop asking "which is right?" and start asking "which of these is wrong, and what is it doing wrong?". Each of the four failure modes gives you a question that resolves the loop in under five seconds:

- "Is this option's strongest word the same strength as the strongest word in the passage?" (overstated hedge)
- "Is the direction of the claim the same as the direction in the passage?" (swapped cause/effect)
- "Is this option a comparison or an absolute, and is it the same kind the passage makes?" (swapped comparison for absolute)
- "Does the passage ever say this?" (imported outside fact)

Each of those questions takes five seconds. Total time to resolve a two-option standoff: ten seconds. Total time you save across 42 questions is the difference between finishing and not finishing.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- **"Elimination is what you do when you do not know the answer."** Elimination is the technique on every question, including the ones where you think you know the answer. Selection is the slower path even when it is possible.
- **"If three options look wrong, the fourth is right by default."** Sometimes the passage is ambiguous and you have marked all four out. In that case, the right move is to commit to your best option and use the 95-minute review window to come back to it. Do not leave the question blank.
- **"The diagnostic words are the only thing to check."** The diagnostic word pair (weak ↔ strong, more than ↔ the most) is a fast filter, but the underlying check is the move the option makes: does it say more, swap a direction, swap a comparison, or import? Run the move check first; use the word pair as a confirmation.
- **"If I can defend an option against the passage, it must be right."** You can defend two options. The question is which one survives the named failure modes, not which one you can argue for.
- **"Imported facts are obvious."** They are not. The import trap presents an option that is true in the world — e.g. that the European Court of Human Rights exists — and uses that truth to make the option look defensible. The test is not whether the option is true; it is whether the passage supplied it.

#### Practice routine that builds elimination

For one week, do elimination-only practice. Take a passage from the official practice test and read the question stem and the four options. For each of the four options, write down which of the four failure modes it commits (or "no failure mode identified"). If more than one option has no failure mode, you have misread something — go back to the passage. If zero options have no failure mode, you have misread the stem or the passage.

The discipline of writing the failure mode down is what stops the eye from sliding over the overstated hedge. Under timed conditions the eye accepts an open word and a closed one as the same word; on paper they do not look the same.

After one week of elimination-only practice, switch back to full timed passages and use the failure modes as the first check on every question.
