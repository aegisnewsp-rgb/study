# Situational Judgement — UCAT ANZ Notes

Situational Judgement is the fourth subtest of the UCAT ANZ. It measures the candidate's capacity to understand real world situations and to identify critical factors and appropriate behaviour in dealing with them. Tested through scenarios drawn from healthcare and professional settings; measures integrity, perspective taking, team involvement and resilience.

> Re-check the live test format and any updates on https://www.ucat.edu.au/about-ucat-anz/test-format/ before planning revision around these figures.

---

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Subtest structure.** Scenarios followed by questions about appropriate behaviour. Some items ask for the most appropriate action; others for the most important consideration.
- **Scoring.** Items are scored against a consensus key. There is no penalty for guessing.
- **Time pressure.** Approximately 22 seconds per item.

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing "most important" with "most appropriate" — these are different questions.
- Applying personal opinion rather than the consensus answer.

---

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Reading the scenario

Identify the key actors, the conflict or issue, and the underlying professional values (integrity, perspective taking, team involvement, resilience).

#### Answering

For "most appropriate action" questions, choose the response that addresses the issue professionally and ethically. For "most important consideration" questions, choose the principle that the response embodies.

#### SJT principles

Integrity: honesty, transparency, accountability.
Perspective taking: empathy, respect for patients and colleagues.
Team involvement: collaboration, communication, conflict resolution.
Resilience: handling pressure, learning from mistakes.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Why SJT rewards principled reasoning

- SJT tests professional judgement, not domain knowledge. The consensus key is based on what healthcare professionals agree is appropriate.

#### Practice pattern

- One SJT drill per day in the 6-8 weeks before the test.
- Use the official UCAT practice tests.
- Review each question carefully — understand the principle.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Personal opinion matters." No — SJT scores against a consensus key.
- "All options are correct." No — only the most appropriate option earns marks.

#### Specification reference

#### Worked example — most-appropriate-action reasoning

Scenario: a junior doctor overhears a colleague raising their voice at a patient who is upset about a long wait. The junior doctor is alone with the colleague at the time.

```
  A. Step in immediately and tell the colleague to stop.
  B. Wait until the colleague is no longer with the patient, then
     raise the concern privately.
  C. Ignore it — the colleague probably had a stressful day.
  D. Report the colleague to a senior before speaking to them.
```

The best answer is B. It addresses the issue (the patient's experience and the colleague's behaviour) without escalating in front of the patient, who would feel humiliated by an on-the-spot confrontation. A addresses the issue but does so publicly. C abandons the patient and the colleague. D escalates beyond what the situation requires.

The principle is: address concerns at the lowest appropriate level first, but do not delay when patient safety or dignity is at stake.

#### Worked example — most-important-consideration reasoning

Scenario: a medical student sees a peer copying answers from a textbook during a closed-book exam. The student has not yet finished their own paper.

```
  A. Mind your own paper — the rules will catch cheaters.
  B. Tell the invigilator after the exam.
  C. Confront the peer during the exam and ask them to stop.
  D. Ask to be moved to a different seat so you cannot be accused
     of involvement.
```

The best answer is B. The most important consideration is academic integrity — cheating undermines the assessment for everyone. A leaves the integrity breach in place; C creates a confrontation that is not the student's role; D protects the student but does not address the cheating. B preserves integrity while keeping the student's own paper intact.

#### Worked example — distinguishing "most appropriate" from "most important"

"Most appropriate action" asks: what should the person in the scenario do? The answer is a behaviour.

"Most important consideration" asks: what principle is most at stake? The answer is a value or professional duty.

```
  Scenario: a nurse realises she has given a patient the wrong dose.

  Most appropriate action: inform the patient, document the error,
  notify the prescribing doctor, and follow the local incident-report
  procedure.

  Most important consideration: patient safety, which requires
  immediate disclosure and clinical assessment rather than concealment.
```

In an exam, the stem makes the question type explicit. If the stem asks for an action, pick a behaviour. If the stem asks for a consideration, pick a principle.

#### Worked example — recognising the four SJT domains

```
  Integrity         — honesty about errors; refusal to cut corners;
                      reporting unsafe practice.
  Perspective taking — empathy with patient distress; treating
                      colleagues with respect; understanding cultural
                      and personal context.
  Team involvement  — offering help; raising concerns through the
                      right channel; shared decision-making.
  Resilience        — coping with pressure; seeking support after a
                      difficult case; reflecting on mistakes.
```

Each scenario in SJT tests one or more of these domains. Identifying which domain is in play narrows the option set quickly.

#### Common marking-scheme (or scoring) mistakes

- Confusing "most appropriate" with "most important". Read the stem.
- Picking the option that addresses the issue but does so publicly, when a private approach is more appropriate.
- Picking the option that protects the actor's interest at the expense of the patient or team.
- Reaching for the most dramatic option. SJT rewards proportionate, principled responses, not heroic ones.
- Applying personal opinion instead of the consensus key. The key reflects what healthcare professionals broadly agree on.

#### 20-minute recap before you walk in

- Read the stem carefully. Note whether it asks for an action or a principle.
- Identify the four SJT domains at play: integrity, perspective taking, team involvement, resilience.
- Prefer the lowest-level intervention that still addresses the issue.
- Patient safety and dignity come first; the actor's comfort comes second.
- There is no negative marking. A guess is always better than a blank.
