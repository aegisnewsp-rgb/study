# Creating — Leaving Certificate (Established) English Notes

Creating is the composition strand of the NCCA Leaving Certificate English specification: the skill of writing original pieces across a range of genres — personal, discursive, argumentative, imaginative, critical and literary — with deliberate attention to register, audience and rhetorical purpose. At Higher level the strand tests whether a candidate can sustain an argument over several paragraphs, control tone and voice across a longer piece, and make deliberate stylistic choices that serve the writer's intent. At Ordinary level the same range of genres is assessed but the mark scheme rewards clarity and coherence over stylistic ambition. The Foundation level composition stream narrows further to short functional pieces.

The strand carries a weight rating of 4 out of 5 in the topic catalogue because the composition paper is the single largest discrete block of marks on the SEC English paper — and the one section where a candidate's marks are entirely under their own control. Paper 1 of the established Leaving Certificate English examination offers candidates a choice of composition prompts and assesses creating as a standalone skill, separate from the comprehension and language questions on the same paper. A candidate who can write a coherent, well-structured piece in their strongest genre will reliably pull marks that are otherwise hard to recover from comprehension and comparative sections.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Strand weight & spread**: Weight 4/5 in the topic catalogue. Composition is the largest single recoverable block on Paper 1 of the SEC examination; under-preparing it forfeits the section a candidate can most directly control.
- **Genre range**: Personal, discursive, argumentative, imaginative, critical and literary writing are all in the syllabus. The live composition prompt on any given year offers a choice — confirm the current list on examinations.ie.
- **Register and audience**: Every piece must consciously match *who is speaking* to *who is listening*. A formal argumentative essay aimed at an adult reader uses a different vocabulary from a personal essay addressed to a peer.
- **First ten minutes**: Plan, do not write. Two minutes of planning buys five marks back at marking time. Candidates who start writing immediately almost always run out of structure.
- **PESTO principle for openings**: *Position, Example, Statement, Topic sentence, Orientation*. A strong opening states the argument and signals the structure. Skip straight to the thesis; never start with "In today's society" or a question.
- **Paragraph architecture**: One idea per paragraph. The first sentence states the idea; the middle sentences evidence it; the final sentence links to the next paragraph. A paragraph that does all three is reliably a band 3 paragraph on the mark scheme.
- **Rhetorical purpose**: Argue to convince, explain to clarify, narrate to engage, describe to evoke. Pick the verb that matches the prompt *before* you start writing, and let the whole piece serve that verb.
- **Editing pass**: Always leave five minutes at the end to read the piece aloud. Sentences that stumble on the tongue are sentences that stumble on the mark scheme too.

#### Examiner traps

- Writing a personal essay when the prompt asked for an argumentative one — or vice versa. The genre mismatch forfeits the *purpose* marks before the content is even read.
- Running out of time and submitting a piece that ends mid-paragraph. A clean, shorter piece that *finishes* outscores a longer piece that breaks down in the final quarter.
- Repeating the same word three times in a paragraph ("very", "really", "good"). The mark scheme reads for variety of vocabulary; the easiest mark to recover is the thesaurus mark.
- Confusing the personal essay with the personal *diary*. Personal essays need a controlling idea; a diary recounts events. Mark scheme rewards the former.
- Using an outline plan that mentions a point and then forgets to make it. Every point in the plan must appear in the piece, signposted explicitly.

#### 30-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Have you written at least one timed piece in each of the six genres in the past month?
- [ ] Can you state the rhetorical purpose (convince / clarify / engage / evoke) for any prompt in under 30 seconds?
- [ ] Do you always plan two minutes before writing?
- [ ] Can you read a finished paragraph aloud and tell whether it has one idea or three?

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### Genre by genre

The Leaving Certificate composition strand is genre-diverse by design — the SEC wants to test whether a candidate can adapt their writing to the prompt rather than relying on a single voice. A four-week pass before mock examinations should hit each genre at least once:

- **Personal essay**: Reflective, first-person, anchored in a specific moment or relationship. The mark scheme rewards *insight* over event-recount. A strong personal essay arrives at a sentence the candidate could not have written at the start — that is the H1-grade pay-off.
- **Discursive essay**: Balanced, weighing two or more sides of an issue. The mark scheme rewards the candidate who can *concede* a counter-argument honestly before defending their own position. Pure advocacy reads as immature.
- **Argumentative essay**: One-sided, persuasive, evidence-driven. The mark scheme rewards the candidate who can *marshal* three or four distinct arguments in support of a position and *sequence* them from strongest to least strong.
- **Imaginative writing**: Story, scene, monologue, vivid moment. The mark scheme rewards the candidate who can sustain atmosphere without lapsing into melodrama. Concrete sensory detail beats abstract description.
- **Critical / literary writing**: An essay on a studied text that uses the literary vocabulary of analysis. The mark scheme rewards the candidate who treats the literary writing as a *critical* essay, not a personal response.
- **Discursive essay with a personal element**: A hybrid genre that the new specification encourages. The mark scheme rewards the candidate who knows when to switch between the personal voice and the discursive voice without losing either.

#### Register, audience and rhetorical purpose

Three levers control a piece of writing:

1. **Register** — formal, semi-formal, informal, intimate. Determined by audience and purpose. A piece addressed to a teacher is formal; a piece addressed to a peer is informal; a piece addressed to oneself is intimate.
2. **Audience** — the imagined reader. A piece aimed at an examiner (who has read a thousand essays on the same prompt) demands more originality than a piece aimed at a peer. Pick the audience *before* you pick the register.
3. **Rhetorical purpose** — what the writing is *for*. Convince, clarify, engage, evoke, reflect. The purpose is the answer to the prompt's verb; the entire piece must serve that purpose.

The mark scheme reads for register consistency. A piece that opens informally and pivots to formal mid-paragraph — without a deliberate signal — forfeits the cohesion marks. Pick the register in the plan and stay there.

#### Building a planning template

A planning template that fits in two minutes on the exam page:

- **Line 1**: The prompt's verb + noun = my rhetorical purpose. (e.g. *Argue* that *technology in schools* is a net positive.)
- **Line 2**: My controlling argument in one sentence.
- **Line 3-5**: Three points I will make, in order from strongest to weakest.
- **Line 6**: A quotation, statistic or example I will open with (one short, specific anchor).
- **Line 7**: The conclusion sentence I want to land on.

Two minutes of planning buys five marks back at marking time. Candidates who skip the plan consistently lose the *structure* mark and the *register* mark on the mark scheme.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Genre-specific mastery at Higher level

At Higher level the mark scheme rewards stylistic ambition. A Higher-grade piece is one where the writer makes deliberate choices that another writer would not — short sentences for tension, longer sentences for reflection; the deliberate fragment; the rhetorical question; the shift to second person. These choices must be *intentional*. A piece that uses a stylistic trick because the candidate remembers it from a model essay reads as pastiche; a piece that uses the same trick because the writer is consciously shaping the reader's response reads as craft.

The H1-grade candidate has at least one *signature* technique — a stylistic move they have practised so often it has become part of their natural writing voice. The most common signature moves are: the opening sentence that names the controlling idea as a paradox ("The day I learned to lie was the day I started telling the truth"), the paragraph that opens with a single short sentence then unpacks it, the closing sentence that returns to the opening image with a shifted meaning. None of these are tricks if they earn their place; all of them are tricks if they appear because the writer was told to insert one.

#### Critical writing as a composition genre

The critical essay genre — sometimes called the literary essay — is the composition genre candidates most consistently under-prepare. It looks like analysis, but the mark scheme treats it as composition: the candidate is composing an *argument* about a text, using literary analysis as evidence. A strong critical essay opens with a thesis about the text, marshals three pieces of evidence, and closes with a sentence that places the text in a larger context. It does *not* open with biographical detail about the writer, and it does *not* narrate the plot.

A useful Higher-level drill: take one of the comparative texts, write a 400-word critical essay arguing a single thesis about it, time-bound yourself to 30 minutes. Do this once a fortnight. After three months the critical essay becomes a recoverable genre rather than a feared one.

#### Common mistakes that depress the grade

- **The "topic-sentence" trap** — opening every paragraph with "Firstly", "Secondly", "Thirdly" reads as a list, not an argument. Use signposting instead: "The strongest evidence for this is...", "A more controversial claim is...".
- **The "concession sandwich" gone wrong** — a discursive essay should concede the opposing view *briefly and honestly*, then move on. A concession that takes up a third of the essay is no longer a concession.
- **The "model essay" pastiche** — opening with a striking quotation from a poet the candidate has not studied, and whose relevance to the prompt is unclear. Examiners read past this; the marks do not follow.
- **The "vocabulary inflation" trap** — replacing common words with thesaurus alternatives that don't quite fit. "Sanguine" for "hopeful" is fine; "pusillanimous" for "cautious" reads as the candidate showing off, not writing clearly.
- **The "rushed ending" trap** — running out of time and submitting a piece that ends mid-thought. A clean conclusion, even a short one, outscores a longer piece that breaks down. Leave five minutes for the ending; if the ending is not landing, write a one-sentence summary and stop.
