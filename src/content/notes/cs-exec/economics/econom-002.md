# Demand and Supply Analysis

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your CS Executive exam.

**Demand and Supply Analysis** explains how buyers' **quantity demanded (QD)** and sellers' **quantity supplied (QS)** interact to set the **equilibrium price (Pe)** and **equilibrium quantity (Qe)** in a market.

- **Law of Demand** (ceteris paribus): QD is inversely related to price → downward-sloping demand curve.
- **Law of Supply** (ceteris paribus): QS is directly related to price → upward-sloping supply curve.
- **Equilibrium condition:** QD = QS at (Pe, Qe).
- **Price Elasticity of Demand:** Epd = (%ΔQD) / (%ΔP); report as absolute value; |Epd| > 1 elastic, = 1 unit elastic, < 1 inelastic.

> **Exam pointer:** In CS Executive Economics (Paper 3, ~3% weight), 5-mark short notes on elasticity interpretation and 8-mark graphical questions on tax incidence appear most often.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Laws and Curves

The **Law of Demand** states that, holding income, tastes, and prices of related goods constant, consumers buy more of a good at lower prices and less at higher prices. The **Law of Supply** states that, holding input costs and technology constant, producers offer more for sale at higher prices and less at lower prices. Together they produce the standard market diagram: a downward-sloping **demand curve (D)** intersecting an upward-sloping **supply curve (S)** at the equilibrium point (Pe, Qe).

#### Shifts vs. Movements

A **movement along** the curve is caused only by a change in the good's own price. A **shift of the entire curve** is caused by non-price determinants.

| Curve | Rightward shift (increase) | Leftward shift (decrease) |
|---|---|---|
| Demand | ↑ income (normal good), ↑ price of substitute, ↓ price of complement, favourable tastes | ↓ income, ↓ price of substitute, ↑ price of complement, unfavourable tastes |
| Supply | ↓ input cost, better technology, subsidy, ↑ number of sellers | ↑ input cost, specific tax, regulation, ↓ number of sellers |

#### Elasticity and Revenue

**Price Elasticity of Demand (Epd)** measures buyers' responsiveness. Because ΔQD/ΔP is normally negative, Epd is reported as an absolute value. Total revenue (TR = P × Q) rises when price falls if demand is elastic (|Epd| > 1) and rises when price rises if demand is inelastic (|Epd| < 1).

#### Government Intervention

A **price ceiling** below Pe causes **excess demand (shortage)**; a **price floor** above Pe causes **excess supply (surplus)**. A specific tax of *t* per unit shifts the supply curve leftward by *t*, raising Pe, lowering Qe, and creating **deadweight loss** equal to the triangle between the old and new quantities under the demand curve.

> **Scoring tip:** Always label the axes (P on Y, Q on X) and shade the consumer-surplus and producer-surplus triangles — examiners award partial credit for a clean, annotated diagram even when the numerical answer is off.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Surplus Measurement and Welfare

**Consumer Surplus (CS)** is the triangular area between the demand curve and the equilibrium price line, bounded on the right by Qe. **Producer Surplus (PS)** is the triangle between the equilibrium price line and the supply curve. Together CS + PS = **total surplus**, which is maximised at the free-market equilibrium. Any deviation — a tax, price control, or monopoly restriction — shrinks total surplus by an amount equal to the **deadweight loss** triangle.

#### Worked Micro-Example

Suppose QD = 200 − 2P and QS = 2P − 40. Setting QD = QS gives 200 − 2P = 2P − 40 → 240 = 4P → **Pe = ₹60**, **Qe = 80 units**. Consumer Surplus = ½ × (₹100 − ₹60) × 80 = **₹1,600**. A specific tax of ₹10 per unit shifts supply to QS′ = 2P − 50. New equilibrium: 200 − 2P = 2P − 50 → P = ₹62.50 paid by buyer, seller receives ₹52.50; new Qe = 75. Deadweight loss = ½ × ₹10 × (80 − 75) = **₹25**.

#### Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Confusing a **change in demand** (shift) with a **change in quantity demanded** (movement).
- Dropping the negative sign on Epd without stating the absolute-value convention.
- Drawing CS as a rectangle instead of a triangle under the demand curve.
- Treating a price ceiling as a permanent shortage rather than a temporary disequilibrium that rationing mechanisms (queues, black markets) often resolve.

> **Exam strategy:** In CS Executive, expect an 8-mark question linking elasticity to a firm's pricing decision or a 10-mark case study on tax incidence. Budget 4–5 minutes sketching the diagram first, then annotate CS, PS, and the deadweight-loss triangle before writing the explanation.

#### Practice Prompts

1. If demand is given by QD = 500 − 5P and supply by QS = 10P − 100, find Pe, Qe, CS, and PS, then evaluate the welfare effect of a ₹6 specific tax.
2. Explain, with a diagram, why a price floor on agricultural produce in India typically generates excess supply and how the government absorbs it through procurement at the Minimum Support Price.
