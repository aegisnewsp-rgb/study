# Cell Division and Protein Synthesis — Leaving Certificate (Established) Biology Notes

Unit 2.3 carries weight 4 and contains more separate examinable facts than any other unit in Strand 2: a cell cycle with four named stages, two types of division compared on one specific axis, DNA replication, transcription, translation, four types of RNA, the genetic code, two classes of mutation and cancer. It sounds like a lot until you see the structure — every one of those items is downstream of a single idea, that **genetic information must be copied exactly for a cell to keep working, and modified deliberately when a cell needs to become something new**.

Two questions carry most of the marks. "Compare the roles of mitosis and meiosis in transmitting genetic information" is the classic comparison — same stages, different outcome, and the outcome is the whole point. "Model how DNA is replicated and the flow of information through mRNA to protein" is the classic modelling question — three molecules, three stages, one sequence.

---

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **The cell cycle**: **interphase** (cell growth and DNA replication) → **nuclear division** (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase) → **cytokinesis**. It is the sequence in which genetic information is transmitted from one cell to two.
- **Prophase** — chromosomes condense and become visible, each with two chromatids joined at the centromere; the nuclear envelope breaks down; spindle microtubules form from centrioles.
- **Metaphase** — chromosomes line up along the equator of the cell, attached to spindle microtubules at their centromeres. This is the stage where karyotyping is done, because it is when chromosomes are at their largest and most distinct.
- **Anaphase** — centromeres divide, sister chromatids separate and are pulled to opposite poles.
- **Telophase** — chromosomes arrive at the poles and decondense; nuclear envelopes re-form around each set.
- **Cytokinesis** — division of the cytoplasm. In animal cells this happens by constriction of the cell surface; in plant cells a **cell plate** of new wall material forms in the middle because a rigid wall prevents the cell from pinching in.
- **DNA replication** happens in interphase, before any division. The double strand separates into two single strands, each of which is a **template** for the synthesis of a complementary strand. The result is two identical DNA molecules, each with one old strand and one new strand — semiconservative replication.
- **Haploid (n)** — one complete set of chromosomes, as in a gamete. **Diploid (2n)** — two complete sets, as in a somatic cell. Humans have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs); gametes have 23.
- **Mitosis produces two genetically identical daughter cells** — the genetic information remains the same. **Meiosis produces four haploid genetically different cells** — the genetic information varies. That single sentence is the answer to the comparison question.
- **The four types of molecule**: **DNA** stores the information; **mRNA** carries a copy of a gene's base sequence out of the nucleus to the ribosome; **tRNA** carries a specific amino acid and has an **anticodon** that pairs with a complementary **codon** on the mRNA; **rRNA** is a structural and catalytic component of the ribosome itself.
- **Transcription** — RNA **polymerase** builds an mRNA strand complementary to one strand of DNA, using free nucleotides. It happens in the nucleus in eukaryotes. The genetic code is read in **triplets (codons)**.
- **Translation** — at the ribosome, tRNA anticodons pair with mRNA codons in sequence, and the corresponding amino acids are joined by **peptide bonds** into a polypeptide, which folds into its functional 3D shape.
- **The genetic code is universal** — the same codon specifies the same amino acid in almost all organisms — which is why a human gene can be expressed in a bacterium, and the foundation of all genetic engineering.
- **Point mutation** — incorrect base pairing during replication produces a change in the nucleotide sequence by **substitution, insertion or deletion**. **Chromosomal mutation** — a change in genes within a chromosome or in the chromosomes themselves, by **deletion, duplication, inversion or translocation**.
- **Cancer** arises when mitotic division becomes **uncontrolled** — the rate of division increases and the normal regulation is lost. Contributing factors the specification names: infectious agents, environmental factors and genetic susceptibility.

#### Examiner traps

- **Writing "interphase is when the cell divides".** Interphase is growth and DNA replication; division is nuclear division plus cytokinesis.
- **Describing anaphase as "chromosomes move apart".** It is the **sister chromatids** that separate, and once they do, each is an individual chromosome.
- **Saying meiosis produces four cells and stopping.** The specification asks you to say what happens to the genetic information, which is the whole point of the comparison.
- **Swapping DNA and RNA functions.** DNA stores; mRNA carries the message; tRNA brings amino acids; rRNA is part of the ribosome.
- **Saying tRNA carries the genetic code.** The **codon** is on the mRNA; the **anticodon** is on the tRNA.
- **Treating point and chromosome mutations as the same thing.** A point mutation changes bases; a chromosomal mutation changes large segments or whole chromosome sets.
- **Giving a cause for cancer that is not on the specification list.** Infectious agents, environmental factors and genetic susceptibility are the three named.

#### 30-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Can you put prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase in order and name what changes in each?
- [ ] Can you explain why a cell plate forms in plants but a constriction forms in animals?
- [ ] Can you state what mitosis and meiosis each do to the genetic information in one sentence each?
- [ ] Can you trace a base from DNA through mRNA to an amino acid, naming polymerase and ribosome?
- [ ] Can you give one example of each of the four chromosomal mutations?

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### The mitosis/meiosis comparison, written the way the mark scheme reads it

| Feature | Mitosis | Meiosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of divisions | One | Two (I and II) |
| Daughter cells produced | Two | Four |
| Chromosome number | Same as parent (diploid → diploid) | Half of parent (diploid → haploid) |
| Genetic identity | **Genetically identical to parent and to each other** | **Genetically different from parent and from each other** |
| Where it happens | Somatic cells, growth and repair, unicellular asexual reproduction | Germ cells to make gametes; spores in plants and fungi |
| Source of variation | Only the rare mutation arising during replication | Independent assortment and recombination between homologous chromosomes |
| Function | Growth, tissue repair, asexual reproduction | Produces gametes for sexual reproduction, which mixes parental genes |

Note that the specification says crossing over knowledge is not required elsewhere in the course, but here the point that meiosis *produces variation* is explicitly required. State it as an outcome: the four cells are not genetically identical, so any two offspring that receive different cells will differ.

#### The central dogma as a process

*Worked question.* A gene on a nuclear chromosome codes for a 180-amino-acid protein. Trace the information from DNA to a functioning protein.
*A.* The gene's base sequence is transcribed by RNA polymerase, which builds a strand of **mRNA** complementary to one strand of DNA — this is **transcription**, and it happens in the nucleus. Because amino acids are specified in triplets, the 180-amino-acid sequence is specified by 180 codons, so the mRNA is at least 540 nucleotides long (longer if introns have been removed). The mRNA leaves the nucleus through a nuclear pore and attaches to a **ribosome** in the cytoplasm. **tRNA** molecules, each carrying one specific amino acid and bearing a complementary **anticodon**, pair with the mRNA codons in sequence. As each tRNA leaves, the ribosome joins the amino acid it carried to the growing chain by a **peptide bond** — this is **translation**. When a stop codon is reached the chain is released and folds into its functional 3D shape, which is what gives the protein its activity. Any error in the DNA sequence propagates through to a different amino acid, and a different shape, and a different protein function.

#### Classifying mutations

*Worked question.* Classify each of these as a point mutation or a chromosomal mutation: (a) C replaced by G in a gene; (b) a segment of a chromosome inserted into a non-homologous chromosome; (c) one base lost from a coding sequence; (d) a whole set of chromosomes duplicated after fertilisation.
*A.* (a) **Point (substitution)**, because a single base is changed. (b) **Chromosomal (translocation)**, because a segment moved to a non-homologous chromosome. (c) **Point (deletion)**, because a single base is lost — and note this shifts the whole reading frame from that point onward, so it usually changes every subsequent amino acid. (d) **Chromosomal**, because the number of whole chromosome sets changed; this is polyploidy, which is common in plants and which produces sterile individuals in animals because meiosis cannot pair the sets evenly.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Worked Higher-level questions with answers

**Q1.** A cell has 12 chromosomes. After one division it produces two daughter cells. State, with reasons, whether the division was mitotic or meiotic.
*A.* Each daughter cell has 12 chromosomes, the same as the parent. Mitosis conserves chromosome number, so each daughter is 2n = 12 with the same genetic information as the parent. If the division had been meiotic I, each daughter would have 6 chromosomes (n) and would not be genetically identical to the parent. Producing only two cells rather than four confirms it is not meiosis. Therefore the division was mitotic.

**Q2.** Explain why crossing over during meiosis increases variation but mitosis does not.
*A.* Crossing over occurs between homologous chromosomes in meiosis I, when non-sister chromatids exchange corresponding segments. A chromosome pair that carried alleles A-B and a-b can afterwards carry A-b and a-B, so the number of *new allele combinations* on single chromosomes increases beyond anything independent assortment alone can produce. In mitosis, sister chromatids are identical because they were produced by replication from the same template, so exchanging material between them produces no new combinations. Mitosis therefore conserves the genotype exactly; meiosis reshuffles it. That is why meiosis, not mitosis, is the source of variation in sexual reproduction.

**Q3.** A patient has a gene with a 12-base insertion. Predict the likely consequence and explain why a single-base substitution might be less serious.
*A.* Insertions and deletions of a number of bases that is not a multiple of three cause a **frameshift**: the triplets are read from the insertion point onward in a completely different frame, so every amino acid downstream is changed and usually a stop codon appears prematurely, giving a truncated, non-functional protein. A **substitution** changes only the codon it falls in. Because the code is degenerate — most amino acids are specified by more than one codon — a substitution is often *silent*, leaving the protein unchanged, and even where it does change an amino acid, only one position is altered. So the same number of bases changed can have very different outcomes depending on which type of mutation occurred.

**Q4.** Explain why meiosis is described as producing genetically different cells, and name the two mechanisms.
*A.* The specification's requirement is that meiosis produces *variation of the genetic information*, and there are two mechanisms. First, **independent assortment**: the random orientation of each homologous pair on the metaphase plate in meiosis I means each gamete receives a random selection of which maternal or paternal chromosome it gets from each pair, giving many combinations. Second, **recombination (crossing over)**: exchange of segments between non-sister chromatids in meiosis I creates chromosomes carrying allele combinations that neither parent possessed. Together these give four cells that differ from each other and from the parent. This is the biological basis of the variation that natural selection acts on in unit 1.5 — without meiosis, a sexually reproducing population would have far less variation on which selection could work.

#### Cancer: mechanism and evidence

The specification asks you to outline how uncontrolled cell proliferation leads to cancer, examine the role of infectious agents, environmental factors and genetic susceptibility, and evaluate factors that impact its development. In outline terms: cancer cells divide uncontrollably because the genes regulating the cell cycle have been damaged, so the checkpoints that normally pause or stop division no longer respond. Three named contributors:

- **Infectious agents** — viruses such as human papillomavirus, hepatitis B virus and Epstein–Barr virus have oncogenes in their genomes, or integrate into host DNA and disrupt cell-cycle regulation.
- **Environmental factors** — tobacco smoke, certain chemicals, ionising radiation and UV light damage DNA directly and raise mutation rate.
- **Genetic susceptibility** — inherited mutations in tumour-suppressor genes mean fewer further mutations are needed before cancer develops, so the process starts earlier and with fewer exposures.

A good evaluation point is that these interact rather than act alone: inherited predisposition raises susceptibility, environment raises mutation rate, and infection may supply the additional damage. And the specification's list of emerging evidence — **early detection, treatments, vaccination and lifestyle changes** — is where the prevention argument lives.

#### How this unit pays elsewhere

- **Unit 1.2** supplies the nucleotides, the genetic code and the protein structure that translation produces.
- **Unit 1.4** supplies the consequences of segregation and independent assortment, which happen in the meiotic stages you draw here.
- **Unit 2.4** returns to cells dividing uncontrollably in the context of disease.
- **Unit 3.3** applies everything: PCR replicates DNA, restriction enzymes cut it, and expression of an inserted gene produces a protein by exactly this transcription/translation pathway.
- **Biology in Practice Investigation** (40% of the grade) — onion root tip or broad bean root tip mitosis slides give quantitative primary data (count cells at each stage, use a mitotic index), which is exactly the evidence-based investigation the descriptors reward.

#### Mistakes students actually make

1. **Leaving out cytokinesis.** Four marks for "division" and the cytoplasm has to be divided too, differently in plants and animals.
2. **Confusing interphase with prophase.** Interphase is longest and is when DNA replication happens; it is also when the chromosome is invisible because it is decondensed chromatin.
3. **Saying meiosis gives "four identical cells".** Identical is the opposite of the truth.
4. **Losing the genetic code's universality.** One sentence — the same codon means the same amino acid in all organisms — underwrites all of genetic engineering.
5. **Describing translation but not transcription.** The question asks you to model both.
6. **Confusing anticodon and codon**, or forgetting that the ribosome (with rRNA) is the site of translation.
7. **Writing a plausible environmental cause for cancer that is not on the list**, or asserting a single cause where the specification wants you to evaluate interacting factors.
