# Grammar: Agreement and Usage

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Subject-verb agreement** demands that the verb form match the **subject** in number (singular/plural) and person (1st/2nd/3rd) — not the nearest noun. **Pronoun-antecedent agreement** requires a pronoun to match its antecedent in number and gender. Key rules to memorise:
- **Indefinite pronouns** (each, every, anyone, somebody, neither, either) are always **singular**: *Everyone **is** ready.*
- **Collective nouns** (team, jury, family) take a **singular verb** when acting as a unit, **plural** when members act individually.
- **Compound subjects** joined by *and* → plural verb; joined by *or/nor* → verb agrees with the **nearest** subject.
- **Prepositional phrases** between subject and verb do not affect agreement.
- **Tense consistency** must hold within a sentence unless time shifts logically.
- **Modifiers** must sit next to the word they modify; **parallelism** requires matched grammatical forms in lists.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Definitions
**Subject-verb agreement** is the grammatical rule that a verb's number (singular or plural) must reflect its subject's number. The **person** (first, second, third) of the verb must also match the subject. **Pronoun-antecedent agreement** extends this to pronouns, which must match the noun they replace (antecedent) in number, gender, and person.

#### Subject Identification Rules
The grammatical subject is the doer or topic of the sentence — not the noun closest to the verb. In *The bag of apples is on the floor*, the subject is *bag* (singular), not *apples*. Prepositional phrases (*of apples*) and relative clauses never alter agreement.

#### Indefinite Pronouns and Number
Pronouns such as **each, every, either, neither, anyone, everybody, someone, nobody, anything** are grammatically singular, regardless of meaning: *Each of the students **has** submitted the form.* Common errors occur when writers mentally translate these words as plurals.

#### Collective Nouns
**Team, family, committee, group, jury** take singular verbs when the group acts as one unit (*The team **wins** often*) and plural verbs when members act separately (*The team **are** arguing among themselves*). British NAT-I passages often test this distinction.

#### Compound Subjects
- Joined by **and** → **plural** verb: *Bread and butter **are** his staples.*
- Joined by **or / nor** → verb agrees with the **nearest** subject: *Neither the manager nor the assistants **were** present.* → *Neither the assistants nor the manager **was** present.*

#### The Number / A Number Distinction
- **The number of** (refers to a single quantity) → singular: *The number of seats **is** limited.*
- **A number of** (means "many") → plural: *A number of seats **were** added.*

#### Tense Consistency
Tense must remain stable within a sentence unless a time shift is justified: *She opened the door and **greets** the guests* is wrong; both verbs need the same tense or a logical past-to-present transition.

#### Modifier Placement
A **misplaced modifier** is too far from its intended word; a **dangling modifier** has no clear subject. *Running quickly, the finish line was crossed by John* misplaces the participle. Correct: *Running quickly, John crossed the finish line.*

#### Parallelism
Items in a list or comparison must share grammatical form: *She likes reading, swimming, and **to bike*** is broken. Correct: *reading, swimming, and **biking***.

#### NAT-I Question Pattern
NAT-I Verbal Reasoning tests these rules through **sentence-correction** items: a sentence with one underlined segment, four alternatives, choose the grammatically correct version. Expect 1–2 such items per paper (3% weight).

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases in Subject-Verb Agreement
- **Subjects joined by "as well as," "along with," "together with," "in addition to"** take a singular verb when the first subject is singular: *The principal, along with the teachers, **is** attending.* The phrase is parenthetical, not compounding.
- **Titles, company names, and words referred to as words** take singular verbs: *Great Expectations **is** a novel; "Data" **is** a plural-looking singular noun.*
- **Units of measurement, money, and time** treated as a single sum take singular verbs: *Fifty rupees **is** the fee.*
- **Each / Every + singular noun + and + singular noun** remains singular: *Each boy and girl **is** expected to attend.*
- **Fractional subjects** depend on the noun's number: *Half of the pie **is** gone* vs. *Half of the apples **are** gone.*

#### Pronoun-Antecedent Edge Cases
- **Generic singular antecedents** traditionally took *his*; modern formal English prefers *his or her* or *their* (singular they, now widely accepted): *Each student must bring **their** book.* NAT-I may still treat singular *their* as informal.
- **Indefinite antecedents** of uncertain gender require rephrasing when possible: *Every applicant must submit **his or her** form* → *All applicants must submit **their** forms.*

#### Modifier Pitfalls
- **Squinting modifiers** sit between two possible targets and create ambiguity: *Students who practise regularly** will improve** clearly.* (Unclear: does *clearly* modify *improve* or *will improve*?)
- **Limiting modifiers** (only, even, almost, just) belong immediately before the word they restrict: *She only eats vegetables* suggests she does nothing else with vegetables; *She eats only vegetables* is the intended meaning.

#### Parallelism — Advanced Patterns
Parallelism applies to **paired conjunctions** (either…or, neither…nor, not only…but also, both…and): *The job requires not only skills **but also experience**.* Mismatched forms (gerund vs. infinitive vs. noun) lose marks. Correlative comparisons also demand parallel form: *The test was harder **than I expected***, not *harder **than I had thought it would be*** (avoid unequal clause shapes).

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics
This topic intersects with **punctuation** (commas before coordinating conjunctions in compound sentences), **sentence structure** (subject-predicate recognition), and **idiom** (correct verb-preposition collocations such as *interested in* vs. *interested to*). NAT-I bundles such items under Verbal Reasoning.

#### Common Mistakes
- Treating the **nearest noun** as the subject in long sentences.
- Using **plural verbs** after *each, every, anyone* because of plural meaning.
- Forgetting **number shifts** when subjects are joined by *or/nor*.
- Allowing **tense drift** across long sentences.
- Placing **adverbs** so they modify the wrong verb.
- Mixing **gerunds and infinitives** in a parallel series.

#### Practice Prompts
1. *Neither the captain nor the players (was / were) available for comment.* — Correct: **were** (verb agrees with the nearer plural subject *players*).
2. *The committee has / have decided to publish its / their findings.* — Acceptable: **has…its** (unit action, singular pronoun).
