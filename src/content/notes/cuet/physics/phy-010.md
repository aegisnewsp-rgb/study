# Thermal Properties

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Thermal properties** describe how matter responds to temperature change, covering expansion, heat capacity, latent heat, and the three modes of heat transfer. The single most-tested formula is **Q = mcΔT** for sensible heat, paired with **Q = mL** during phase change at constant temperature.

- **Thermal expansion (solids):** ΔL = L₀αΔT, ΔA = A₀·2α·ΔT, ΔV = V₀γΔT, with γ ≈ 3α.
- **Calorimetry:** heat lost by the hotter body equals heat gained by the colder body when isolated.
- **Radiation:** P = σAT⁴ — emitted power scales with the *fourth* power of absolute temperature (Kelvin).
- **Anomalous fact:** water is densest at 4 °C, not 0 °C — the only common liquid that contracts on heating between 0–4 °C.

> **Exam tip:** CUET UG Section-II (Physics) asks 1–2 MCQs from this cluster, usually direct-formula application on latent heat or a calorimetry mix.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Temperature, Heat, and Expansion

Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of particles, while **heat** is energy in transit between bodies at different temperatures. When a solid is heated, atomic vibrations widen the interatomic spacing, producing **linear expansion** ΔL = L₀αΔT. The coefficient α has units of K⁻¹ (or °C⁻¹). For an isotropic solid the area coefficient is 2α and the volume coefficient is γ ≈ 3α; a frequent error is treating γ as equal to α.

#### Heat Capacity and Latent Heat

The heat required to raise 1 kg of a substance by 1 K is its **specific heat capacity c** (J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹), giving Q = mcΔT. During a phase change — melting, boiling, sublimation — temperature stays constant and the energy goes into breaking intermolecular bonds. That energy per kilogram is the **specific latent heat L**, used as Q = mL. Water's L_fusion = 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹ and L_vaporisation = 2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹, a ~6.8× ratio that numericals love to test.

#### Three Modes of Heat Transfer

| Mode | Mechanism | Requires medium? | Governing law |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conduction | Molecular vibration / free-electron drift | Yes (solid best) | H = kA(ΔT/L) |
| Convection | Bulk fluid motion | Yes (fluids) | Newton's cooling of fluids |
| Radiation | EM waves | No | P = σAT⁴ |

**Newton's law of cooling** states that the rate of temperature fall is proportional to (T_body − T_surroundings); it holds only for small temperature differences where radiation losses approximate linearity.

#### Worked Relationship

For an ideal gas, PV = nRT combines pressure, volume, and absolute temperature. Holding n constant, a doubling of Kelvin temperature at fixed P doubles V — examiners often disguise this as a mercury-thermometer or piston numerical.

> **Trap to avoid:** Always convert °C to K before plugging into PV = nRT or Stefan's law. A 27 °C reading must become 300 K.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Calorimetry and the Principle of Mixtures

When a hot solid (mass m₁, specific heat c₁, initial T₁) is dropped into a liquid (m₂, c₂, T₂) in an insulated calorimeter of water equivalent w, equilibrium temperature T_f satisfies:

> m₁c₁(T₁ − T_f) = (m₂c₂ + w)(T_f − T₂)

Sign discipline is the make-or-break skill: heat *out* of the hot body must equal heat *into* the cold body. Mixing units (calories with joules, or grams with kilograms) is the single most common numerical error.

#### Anomalous Expansion of Water

Between 0 °C and 4 °C water *contracts* on heating, reaching maximum density of 1000 kg m⁻³ at 4 °C. This inversion drives aquatic-life survival in winter — ice floats, insulating the water below at 4 °C. No other common liquid behaves this way; it arises from hydrogen-bonded hexagonal clustering that breaks down as temperature rises from 0 to 4 °C.

#### Radiation: Kirchhoff and the Greenhouse Effect

**Kirchhoff's law** states that a good absorber is a good emitter at every wavelength. Earth's atmosphere is largely transparent to incoming visible solar radiation but absorbs outgoing infrared, because CO₂, H₂O, and CH₄ have IR-active vibrational modes. This selective trapping is the **greenhouse effect**, distinct from the actual "greenhouse" mechanism (which is mainly convection suppression).

#### Common Mistakes in CUET-Style MCQs

- Selecting *latent heat of fusion* instead of vaporisation when a steam/condensation problem is set at 100 °C.
- Forgetting that ΔT = 0 during phase change, so Q = mcΔT does not apply at the transition instant.
- Treating α, β, γ as independent — for a solid they obey β ≈ 2α and γ ≈ 3α.
- Confusing specific heat capacity (J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) with molar heat capacity C = Mc (J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹).

#### Two Practice Prompts

1. A 0.5 kg copper block (c = 390 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) at 200 °C is dropped into 1 kg of water at 20 °C in a calorimeter of water equivalent 0.05 kg. Find the equilibrium temperature. *(Answer: ≈ 31.6 °C)*
2. A black sphere of radius 5 cm sits at 727 °C in surroundings at 27 °C. Using σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴ and applying Stefan's net-exchange form, compute the net radiated power. *(Answer: P_net = σA(T⁴ − T₀⁴) ≈ 1.52 kW)*
