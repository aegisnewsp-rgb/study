# Kohlberg's Stages of Moral Development

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> One-line anchor for the last-hour revision pass.

Lawrence Kohlberg's theory lays out **six invariant stages** of moral reasoning, grouped into **three levels**. Reasoning, not behaviour, is what changes.

| Level | Age band (approx.) | Core question driving judgement |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-conventional** (Stages 1–2) | ~0–9 years | "What will happen to me?" |
| **Conventional** (Stages 3–4) | Adolescence onward | "What do others expect of me?" |
| **Post-conventional / Principled** (Stages 5–6) | Adulthood, not all reach | "What principle justifies this act?" |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook (Stage Order Mnemonic):** **"OPIGS-LU"** — **O**bedience-and-punishment → **P**ersonal exchange (self-interest) → **I**nterpersonal "good boy" → **G**overnment/law-and-order → **L**aws as social contract → **U**niversal ethical principles. Read top to bottom: reasoning moves from *fear → fair trade → approval → duty → democratic law → self-chosen justice*.

Most UPTET MCQs hinge on remembering the count (3 levels, 6 stages), the order, and the researcher. Kohlberg tested reasoning with the **Heinz dilemma** — a husband steals an overpriced drug to save his dying wife; the *justification* the respondent gives, not the verdict, places them in a stage.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

> Working-level understanding for candidates with two weeks to two months.

#### Levels, stages, and what each one asks

| Stage | Label | Reasoning focus | Sample justification style |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Obedience and Punishment Orientation | Avoid punishment; obey power | "He'll go to jail, so it's wrong." |
| **2** | Individualism and Exchange | Self-interest, fair trades | "The druggist should split the profit." |
| **3** | Good Interpersonal Relationships | Live up to social expectations; "good boy/nice girl" | "A good husband protects his wife." |
| **4** | Maintaining Social Order | Law, duty, social system | "Stealing breaks the law; laws exist for order." |
| **5** | Social Contract and Individual Rights | Democratic law, majority welfare, human rights | "Life outweighs property; laws can be reformed." |
| **6** | Universal Ethical Principles | Self-chosen principles — justice, equality, dignity | "Right is right even if the law is wrong." |

Levels I and II together constitute **heteronomous morality** in the Piagetian sense — moral truth is taken from outside (authority, convention). Level III marks **autonomous morality**: principles are self-chosen and critically examined.

#### Key principles the examiner can probe

- **Invariance and sequence.** A child does not skip stages and does not regress backwards under normal development. Stages are *universal* in order, though not all cultures or persons reach the highest two.
- **Reasoning ≠ behaviour.** A child at Stage 2 can still give money to a poor friend; the *reason* (social approval, reciprocity) places the act, not the act itself.
- **Method.** Kohlberg's **dissonance method** uses longitudinal interviews built around dilemmas like the **Heinz dilemma**, where two moral claims (steal/don't steal) collide and force the respondent to justify a choice.
- **Three levels, six stages.** A common 1-mark trap asks you to call the levels "stages" — they are not; each level contains two stages.

#### Worked problem — matching a response to a stage

A respondent is asked the Heinz dilemma. She argues: *"If Heinz loves his wife, he should steal the drug. A good marriage means protecting each other."*

- Step 1 — Identify the moral claim: care for a loved one overrides property.
- Step 2 — Map the justification to a stage. The framing "good marriage", "protecting each other" appeals to **social expectations and relationships**, not to law or principle.
- Step 3 — Place the stage: **Stage 3 — Good Interpersonal Relationships** (Conventional level).
- Cross-check: a Stage 4 answer would cite *law and order*; a Stage 5 answer would weigh *majority welfare / democratic law*; a Stage 6 answer would invoke *universal human rights*. None of those cues appear here.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Candidates often select Stage 4 because the answer *sounds* mature. The decisive cue is the word "marriage" and the appeal to relational approval — that places reasoning in Stage 3, not Stage 4.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

> For candidates with three or more months, or for re-readers who want the edge cases.

#### Comparison matrix — Kohlberg vs Piaget on morality

| Dimension | Piaget's moral theory | Kohlberg's extension |
|---|---|---|
| Number of stages | Two (Heteronomous, Autonomous) | Six, in three levels |
| Basis of judgement | Rules as fixed / rules as cooperative (peer-based) | Expanding circle: self → others → law → principles |
| Age of final stage | About 10–11 years | Not all adults reach Stage 5/6 |
| Method | Clinical interview, game rules | Dissonance method with dilemmas (Heinz) |
| Domain of change | Moral *thinking* about rules | Moral *reasoning* about dilemmas |

Kohlberg built directly on Piaget's two stages and argued that Piaget stopped too early. CDP examiners love paired items where you must attribute "heteronomous vs autonomous" to Piaget and "Stage 3 vs Stage 4" to Kohlberg — keep the names straight.

#### Edge cases and high-difficulty traps

1. **Stage 5 vs Stage 6 inversion.** Stage 5 still accepts democratic law as legitimate but allows it to be reformed for majority welfare. Stage 6 holds principles *above* law — it can justify civil disobedience. Answer keys sometimes swap them; rely on the principle-vs-law distinction.
2. **Universal attainment myth.** Kohlberg's original samples were male, urban, Western. Snarey's 1985 cross-cultural reanalysis found Stage 5/6 reasoning rarer outside those samples. State the theory's universality claim as *sequence-universal*, not as *stage-attainment-universal*.
3. **Behaviour confound.** Longitudinal data (e.g., the Colby–Kohlberg follow-up) shows Stage 6 reasoners occasionally act unethically, and Stage 2 reasoners occasionally act heroically. The theory explains reasoning, not virtue.
4. **Dilemma scoring.** The Heinz dilemma is scored on the *strongest* justification across several probes, not on the first answer. Examiners may quote a one-line response; treat it as suggestive, not diagnostic.
5. **Common 1-mark wrong answers.** "Four stages" (it is six), "Kohlberg studied behaviour" (he studied reasoning), "all cultures reach Post-Conventional" (they do not), "Piaget's six stages" (Piaget's two, Kohlberg's six).

#### Advanced practice prompts

1. A teacher presents the Heinz dilemma to a class of 12-year-olds. Three students respond: (a) "It's bad because it's stealing"; (b) "The druggist should charge less"; (c) "Human life is more important than property, but laws should change." Assign each response to the most likely Kohlberg stage and justify your placement in two sentences each.
2. A UPTET assertion–reason item states: *"Reason: Kohlberg's stages are universal."* Evaluate whether the assertion follows, and write a one-paragraph note distinguishing **sequence-universality** from **attainment-universality** — the distinction the examiner is actually testing.
