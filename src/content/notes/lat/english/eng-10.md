# Summary Writing

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Summary writing condenses a 400–600-word passage into roughly one-third of its length while preserving the author's core argument in your own words. LAT candidates are scored on three skills: identifying the **gist**, **paraphrasing** without copying, and obeying the **one-third rule**.

- Open with the title, author, and central thesis in a single introductory sentence.
- Keep tense in the past, perspective in the third person, and voice neutral — no "I think" or evaluative adjectives.
- Drop examples, statistics, anecdotes, and minor sub-points; keep only the main idea plus 2–3 essential supports.

| Element | What to include |
| --- | --- |
| Intro line | Title, author, main thesis |
| Body | Main idea + key supports only |
| Closing | Restate thesis, no new information |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### What a Summary Must Do

A summary reproduces the source's **central idea** and its most essential supporting points in compressed form. The writer's task is selection plus paraphrasing: decide which sentences of the original carry the argument, then re-express them. Skim first for the topic sentence of each paragraph; that usually reveals the spine of the passage. Cross out illustrations, dates, quotations, and repetitions, then rewrite what survives.

#### Conventions LAT Examiners Expect

The LAT English paper marks summary answers against a fixed rubric, so deviations cost marks. The response must be in the **third person** and the **past tense**, even when the source uses present tense. Attribution belongs in the opening sentence — *"In X's essay 'Y', the author argues that…"* — not scattered through the body. Length is targeted at **about one-third** of the original passage; a summary of 180 words from a 500-word source is typical.

#### High-Yield Distinctions

| Feature | Summary | Paraphrase | Précis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Length | ~1/3 of source | Same as source | 1/3 to 1/4 of source |
| Focus | Whole passage | One section | Whole passage |
| Style | Neutral, third person | May match source tone | Formal, telegraphic |

- Drop **all** examples, statistics, anecdotes, and rhetorical questions.
- Preserve **chronological or logical order** of the source.
- Never quote directly; every line must be your own wording.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Traps

Many candidates lose marks by treating summary as paraphrasing the first paragraph. The thesis often appears mid-passage, especially in argumentative texts where the opening sets scene rather than argument. Read the **final paragraph** as well; conclusions frequently restate the thesis in sharper form. Another trap is the **inverted ratio**: writing a summary longer than the source, usually because the writer includes examples "to show understanding". Examiners read for compression, not coverage.

When the passage mixes narrative and argument, summarise the argument and reduce the narrative to its function (*"The author illustrates the point with…"*) rather than retelling events. If two paragraphs make the same point, merge them into one sentence.

#### Worked Micro-Example

Source (42 words): *"In his 1848 essay, John Stuart Mill argues that free speech protects not only the truth but the very possibility of discovering it. Silencing dissent, he warns, assumes we already know everything, which no honest thinker can claim."*

Acceptable summary sentence: *"Mill (1848) contends that free speech safeguards truth itself and the process of inquiry, warning that censorship presupposes a completeness of knowledge no thinker honestly possesses."*

Notice the causal link between truth and inquiry is preserved, "argues" becomes "contends", and the warning is condensed rather than copied.

#### Common Mistakes and Exam Strategy

- Using first-person pronouns ("I believe the author means…").
- Quoting phrases longer than four words from the source.
- Introducing a fresh point in the closing line.
- Writing under 100 words or over 220 words from a 500-word source.

| Mistake | Marks lost | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Verbatim phrases | High | Rephrase every clause |
| Wrong tense/voice | Medium | Default past tense, third person |
| Missing intro line | High | Add title + author + thesis first |
| New closing idea | Medium | Closing must echo the thesis |

Two practice prompts: (1) Summarise a 500-word editorial on judicial independence into ~165 words, preserving the argument's sequence. (2) Condense a 480-word narrative about a landmark case into ~155 words, converting descriptive events into their argumentative function.
