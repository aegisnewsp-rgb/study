# Some Basic Concepts

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Some Basic Concepts of Chemistry** is the opening unit of JEE Main Chemistry, carrying roughly **3% weightage** (typically 1–2 questions per shift) and forms the language used in every later unit — Physical, Inorganic, and Organic.

- **Mole concept**: $n = \dfrac{\text{given mass}}{\text{molar mass}}$; number of particles $= n \times N_A$, where $N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$.
- **Molarity** $M = \dfrac{n_{\text{solute}}}{V_{\text{solution (L)}}}$ (temperature-dependent); **Molality** $m = \dfrac{n_{\text{solute}}}{w_{\text{solvent (kg)}}}$ (temperature-independent).
- **Mass %** $= \dfrac{w_{\text{solute}}}{w_{\text{solution}}} \times 100$; **mole fraction** $x_A = \dfrac{n_A}{n_A + n_B}$, with $\sum x_i = 1$.
- **High-yield traps**: confuse *molarity* with *molality*, forget $1000$ factor in molality, or treat limiting reagent as "excess reagent" in yield problems.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Matter, Classification and Laws
Chemistry begins with **matter** (anything with mass and volume) classified into **pure substances** (elements, compounds) and **mixtures** (homogeneous like salt water; heterogeneous like sand + iron filings). Five experimental laws govern chemical combination: **conservation of mass** (Lavoisier), **definite proportions** (Proust), **multiple proportions** (Dalton), **reciprocal proportions** (Richter), and **combining volumes** (Gay-Lussac — only for gases).

#### Mole and Stoichiometry
The **mole** ($6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles) bridges the microscopic and macroscopic. For a reaction $aA + bB \rightarrow cC + dD$, stoichiometric ratios $\dfrac{n_A}{a} = \dfrac{n_B}{b} = \dfrac{n_C}{c} = \dfrac{n_D}{d}$ identify the **limiting reagent** (LR) — the one consumed fully, controlling theoretical yield.

#### Concentration Units
| Term | Formula | Depends on T? |
|---|---|---|
| Mass % | $\dfrac{w_{solute}}{w_{soln}} \times 100$ | No |
| Mole fraction $x_A$ | $\dfrac{n_A}{n_A + n_B}$ | No |
| Molarity $M$ | $\dfrac{n_{solute}}{V_{soln}\,(\text{L})}$ | Yes |
| Molality $m$ | $\dfrac{n_{solute}}{w_{solvent}\,(\text{kg})}$ | No |

Interconversion with density $d$ (g/mL): $M = \dfrac{m \times d \times 1000}{1000 + m \times M_{\text{solute}}}$.

#### Empirical vs Molecular Formula
**Empirical formula** = simplest integer ratio of atoms (e.g., CH for benzene); **molecular formula** = $n \times$ empirical, where $n = \dfrac{M_{\text{molecular}}}{M_{\text{empirical}}}$.

#### Exam Pattern
JEE Main usually frames this unit as a single-choice numerical: stoichiometry with LR, mole–mass–particle conversion, or molarity–molality interchange with a given density.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Dimensional Analysis and Significant Figures
SI base units used across chemistry: metre (m), kilogram (kg), mole (mol), kelvin (K), ampere (A). **Dimensional analysis** converts units by multiplying by unity ratios (e.g., 1 atm = 101325 Pa). **Significant figures** propagate the precision of the least-certain measurement: addition/subtraction follows decimal places; multiplication/division follows sig-fig count. Exact conversion factors (e.g., 12 in $N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}$) carry infinite sig-figs.

#### Equivalent Weight and Normality
**Equivalent weight** $E = \dfrac{\text{Molar mass}}{n\text{-factor}}$, where $n$-factor = acidity/basicity for acids/bases, total charge for salts, and electrons transferred for redox. **Normality** $N = \dfrac{\text{equivalents}}{V\,(\text{L})}$, and at equivalence point $N_1 V_1 = N_2 V_2$. Hardness of water (ppm CaCO₃) and H₂O₂ assays (volume strength = molarity × 11.2) are direct JEE applications.

#### Gas Stoichiometry
Combining $PV = nRT$ with stoichiometric ratios solves problems like *"what volume of CO₂ at STP is produced from 5 g CaCO₃?"* — answer: $n = 5/100 = 0.05$ mol, $V = 0.05 \times 22.4 = 1.12$ L.

#### Common Mistakes
- Treating **w/w%** as **w/v%** when density is omitted.
- Computing $n$ of solvent instead of solute for molality.
- Ignoring **temperature dependence** of molarity in heating/cooling problems.
- Forgetting that 1 mol of an ideal gas at **STP** = 22.4 L, but at **SATP** (25 °C, 1 bar) = 24.8 L.

#### Practice Prompts
1. 10 mL of 0.1 M H₂SO₄ neutralises 25 mL of NaOH. Find NaOH molarity and the mass of Na₂SO₄ formed. *(Expected: 0.08 M; 0.142 g.)*
2. A compound contains C = 40%, H = 6.67%, O = 53.33% with molar mass 180 g/mol. Determine empirical and molecular formulae. *(Expected: empirical CH₂O; molecular C₆H₁₂O₆.)*
