# Data Sufficiency Problems

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your MAT slot.

A **Data Sufficiency** item presents one question followed by two statements labelled **Statement I** and **Statement II**. Your job is to judge whether the information is *enough* to answer the question — not to compute the answer itself. The standard MAT response scheme uses five options covering individual sufficiency, joint sufficiency, and insufficiency.

> **Memory anchor:** Sufficiency = uniqueness. If two different valid answers fit the data, that data is NOT sufficient.

- **Statement I alone sufficient, II not** — pick when I pins down one answer.
- **Statement II alone sufficient, I not** — pick when only II does the job.
- **Both together sufficient, neither alone** — the most common correct choice in MAT.
- **Each alone sufficient** — rare; happens only with redundant statements.
- **Both together insufficient** — additional data is required.

Target 1–2 items per Data Analysis section in the IBT/CBT format and aim for 100% accuracy, since computation is minimal.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### What "Sufficient" Actually Means

A statement is **sufficient** if, and only if, it forces *exactly one* correct response to the question. You do not need to find that response in full detail; you only need to confirm that no alternative valid response exists. This definition drives every decision in MAT's Data Sufficiency items and separates them from ordinary quantitative problems.

#### The Five-Option Decision Scheme

| Option | Meaning | Decision rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | I alone sufficient, II not | I gives unique answer; II gives 2+ possible answers |
| 2 | II alone sufficient, I not | II gives unique answer; I gives 2+ possible answers |
| 3 | Both together sufficient, neither alone | Each alone fails; combined they pin one answer |
| 4 | Each alone sufficient | Both statements independently give the unique answer |
| 5 | Both together insufficient | Even combined data leaves 2+ valid answers |

#### Working Procedure

1. Read the question carefully and note any hidden constraints (integers, positivity, distinctness).
2. Test **Statement I alone**: try to construct two different valid answers. If you can, I is insufficient.
3. Test **Statement II alone** using the same contradiction method.
4. If both fail alone, **combine them** and re-test for uniqueness.
5. If combination still leaves multiple answers, the correct choice is option 5.

#### Common Question Types in MAT

- Arithmetic computation involving age, profit, or distance.
- Algebraic equation solving with one or two unknowns.
- Geometry measurement (area, perimeter, angle).
- Percentage, ratio, and average reasoning.
- Counting or data-comparison problems.

> **Tip:** Always ask "Can I produce two different correct answers from this data alone?" If yes, sufficiency fails — even if the data looks plentiful.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases That Decide Close Items

Hidden constraints silently flip sufficiency. A statement that seems ambiguous may actually force a unique answer once you assume **integers**, **positive values**, or **distinct elements**. Conversely, a statement that looks decisive can collapse into a range when negative or zero values are allowed. Always list the assumptions of the question before judging sufficiency.

#### Mechanism: The Contradiction Test

The fastest mental check is the **contradiction test**. Suppose a question asks for *x* and Statement I says *x² = 25*. Mentally generate two valid responses — say *x = 5* and *x = −5*. Because two distinct answers satisfy Statement I, it is insufficient. If Statement II later forces *x > 0*, the pair together becomes sufficient. This method replaces slow algebra with rapid logical elimination.

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics

Data Sufficiency overlaps with **Quantitative Comparison**, **Logical Reasoning** (assumption-heavy syllogisms), and **Data Interpretation** (where the two "statements" may be two charts). Practising it sharpens the conditional thinking needed for Reading Comprehension inference questions as well.

#### Classic Traps in MAT

| Trap | Why candidates fall for it | Correct handling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Computing, not judging | You find *an* answer and mark "sufficient" | Confirm no second answer exists |
| Skipping the joint check | Each statement fails alone, but you stop | Combine and retest before choosing option 3 |
| Forgetting hidden constraints | "x is an integer" or "ages are distinct" ignored | Reapply constraints at each step |
| Overlooking negative roots | Quadratics give ± values | Both roots count as separate answers |

#### Practice Prompts

1. *What is the value of n?* Statement I: n² − 7n + 12 = 0. Statement II: n > 3. Decide sufficiency and pick the correct option.
2. *Is triangle ABC isosceles?* Statement I: AB = 5. Statement II: AC = 5. Apply the contradiction test to both statements and to their combination.
