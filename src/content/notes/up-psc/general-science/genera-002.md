# Chemical Reactions — Acids, Bases and Salts

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

- **Arrhenius** defined acids as substances producing **H⁺ in water** and bases as those producing **OH⁻**.
- **Brønsted–Lowry** extends this: acids are **proton (H⁺) donors**, bases are **proton acceptors** — NH₃ qualifies without an OH⁻.
- **Lewis** gives the broadest definition: acids accept an **electron pair**, bases donate one (e.g., BF₃ + NH₃).
- **pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]**, **pOH = −log₁₀[OH⁻]**, and **pH + pOH = 14 at 25 °C** because **[H⁺][OH⁻] = Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴**.
- **Neutralisation:** Acid + Base → Salt + Water (e.g., HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O).
- High-yield pointers for UPPSC: salt hydrolysis (Na₂CO₃ basic, NH₄Cl acidic), Plaster of Paris = **CaSO₄·½H₂O**, washing soda = **Na₂CO₃·10H₂O**, baking soda = **NaHCO₃**.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### The three definitions side by side

| Concept | Arrhenius | Brønsted–Lowry | Lewis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acid | Releases H⁺ in water | Proton donor | Electron-pair acceptor |
| Base | Releases OH⁻ in water | Proton acceptor | Electron-pair donor |
| Limitation | Only aqueous | Needs proton transfer | Includes reactions without H⁺ |
| Example | HCl, NaOH | NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ | BF₃ + NH₃ → H₃N–BF₃ |

The Brønsted–Lowry view introduces **conjugate acid–base pairs**: when NH₃ accepts a proton it becomes NH₄⁺, while H₂O that donated it becomes OH⁻.

#### Strong versus weak electrolytes
**Strong acids** (HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HClO₄, HBr, HI) **fully ionise** in water. **Weak acids** (CH₃COOH, H₂CO₃, H₃BO₃, HCN) only **partially ionise**, giving a lower [H⁺] than their concentration suggests. Strength refers to the **degree of ionisation**, not how concentrated the solution is — concentrated acetic acid is still weak.

#### The pH scale and Kw

| Quantity | Value at 25 °C |
|---|---|
| Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] | 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² L⁻² |
| pH + pOH | 14 |
| Acidic | pH < 7 |
| Neutral | pH = 7 |
| Basic | pH > 7 |

A 0.01 M HCl solution gives [H⁺] = 10⁻² → pH = 2. A 0.01 M NaOH gives pOH = 2 → pH = 12. Kw rises with temperature, so the neutral point shifts below pH 7 above 25 °C.

#### Indicators and their colour changes

| Indicator | Acid → Base colour |
|---|---|
| Litmus | Red → Blue |
| Phenolphthalein | Colourless → Pink |
| Methyl orange | Red → Yellow |

#### Salts and hydrolysis
Salts formed from a **strong acid + strong base** (NaCl, KNO₃) are **neutral**. Salts from a **strong acid + weak base** (NH₄Cl) hydrolyse to give an **acidic solution**, while those from a **weak acid + strong base** (Na₂CO₃, CH₃COONa) give a **basic solution**.

#### Common salts of daily life

| Salt | Formula | Use / Note |
|---|---|---|
| Baking soda | NaHCO₃ | Mild antacid, leavening agent |
| Washing soda | Na₂CO₃·10H₂O | Water softening, glass-making |
| Plaster of Paris | CaSO₄·½H₂O | Setting into CaSO₄·2H₂O with water |
| Bleaching powder | CaOCl₂ | Disinfectant, water sterilisation |
| Blue vitriol | CuSO₄·5H₂O | Fungicide, electroplating |

#### UPPSC question patterns
Prelims often test one-line facts: "Plaster of Paris is obtained by heating…" or "Which salt is used to remove permanent hardness of water?" (answer: **washing soda, Na₂CO₃**, via ion-exchange). Assertion–reason items on litmus turning blue with NH₃ (a Brønsted base) appear regularly.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Water of crystallisation and anhydrous salts
Many salts crystallise with a fixed number of water molecules built into the lattice — called **water of crystallisation**. Examples: **CuSO₄·5H₂O** (blue vitriol), **Na₂CO₃·10H₂O** (washing soda), **FeSO₄·7H₂O** (green vitriol), **CaSO₄·2H₂O** (gypsum). Heating drives off this water to give the **anhydrous** form — CuSO₄·5H₂O turns white as CuSO₄. Plaster of Paris is a half-hydrate: gypsum heated to about 120 °C loses three-quarters of its water to form **CaSO₄·½H₂O**, which sets back to gypsum when mixed with water (exothermic, releasing heat used in fracture casts).

#### Hardness of water — a frequent Mains hook

| Type | Cause | Removed by |
|---|---|---|
| Temporary | Ca(HCO₃)₂, Mg(HCO₃)₂ | Boiling: bicarbonates decompose to insoluble carbonates |
| Permanent | CaCl₂, MgSO₄, CaSO₄ | Washing soda, ion-exchange resins, Calgon method |

Boiling converts Ca(HCO₃)₂ → CaCO₃↓ + H₂O + CO₂. Permanent hardness cannot be removed by boiling and needs a chemical method.

#### Buffers and biological relevance
A **buffer** resists pH change on adding small amounts of acid or base. The classic example is **CH₃COOH / CH₃COONa**. Blood maintains pH ≈ 7.4 using the **H₂CO₃ / HCO₃⁻** buffer; deviations cause acidosis or alkalosis. UPPSC descriptive items sometimes ask why lemon juice tastes sour despite containing a weak acid (high [H⁺] due to low pH, not strong ionisation).

#### Common mistakes flagged in UPPSC answer keys

- Confusing **alkali** (water-soluble base) with **base**: Cu(OH)₂ is a base but **not** an alkali.
- Writing **Na₂CO₃** when the question means **NaHCO₃** for baking/cake-making.
- Forgetting that **pH + pOH = 14 only at 25 °C**; at body temperature (37 °C) Kw ≈ 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁴ and neutral pH ≈ 6.8.
- Assuming salt hydrolysis leaves a solution neutral — NH₄Cl turns litmus **red**, Na₂CO₃ turns it **blue**.

#### Worked numeric example
A solution of HCl has pH = 3. Find the pOH and [OH⁻].
pOH = 14 − 3 = **11**; [OH⁻] = 10⁻¹¹ M. Quick check: [H⁺][OH⁻] = 10⁻³ × 10⁻¹¹ = 10⁻¹⁴ ✓.

#### Practice prompts
1. Why is an aqueous solution of ammonium chloride acidic even though it contains no H⁺ in its formula?
2. Plaster of Paris is stored in airtight containers. Predict what happens if it is left exposed to humid air and justify using the setting reaction.
