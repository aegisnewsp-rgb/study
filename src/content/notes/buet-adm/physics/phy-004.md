# Electrostatics

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Electrostatics deals with **electric charges at rest**, the forces they exert on each other, and the electric fields and potentials they produce. For BUET Admission, the recurring items are Coulomb's law, the point-charge field/potential formulas, Gauss's law for symmetric charge distributions, and capacitor networks.

| Quantity | Formula | SI Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Coulomb force | F = k·q₁·q₂ / r², with k ≈ 8.99×10⁹ N·m²/C² | newton (N) |
| Electric field of a point charge | E = kq / r² | N/C or V/m |
| Electric potential of a point charge | V = kq / r | volt (V = J/C) |
| Parallel-plate capacitance | C = ε₀εᵣA / d | farad (F) |
| Energy in a capacitor | U = ½CV² | joule (J) |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"Capacitors series sum inversely, capacitors parallel sum directly"** — the mirror image of resistors. Also remember the field/potential link: **E points from High V to Low V**, and field lines always cross equipotentials at 90°.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Laws and Their Meaning

**Coulomb's law** states that two point charges q₁ and q₂, separated by a distance r, repel or attract each other with a force whose magnitude is F = k·q₁·q₂ / r², where k = 1/(4πε₀) ≈ 8.99×10⁹ N·m²/C². The direction lies along the line joining them. The law is an inverse-square law and is strictly valid only for point charges at rest; extended bodies require integration.

**Electric field E** is defined as the force per unit positive test charge, E = F/q₀, with SI unit N/C (identical to V/m). For a point charge, E = kq/r², directed radially outward from a positive charge and inward toward a negative charge. **Electric potential V** is a scalar quantity measured in volts (J/C), representing the work done per unit positive charge to bring a test charge from infinity to a point. For a point charge, V = kq/r.

#### Field, Potential, and Conductors

- Field lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges; their density reflects field strength.
- Equipotential surfaces are always perpendicular to field lines.
- Inside a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium, E = 0 and V is constant throughout.
- Excess charge on a conductor resides entirely on the outer surface.

#### Gauss's Law

Gauss's law states that the total electric flux through any closed surface equals the enclosed charge divided by ε₀: Φ = ∮ E·dA = Q_enclosed / ε₀. Use it only when the charge distribution has high symmetry — spherical, cylindrical, or planar — so that E has constant magnitude and known direction over the Gaussian surface. Examples:

- Charged conducting sphere of radius R: outside, E = kq/r²; inside, E = 0.
- Infinite uniformly charged sheet: E = σ / (2ε₀), independent of distance.
- Uniformly charged solid sphere (non-conducting): outside behaves like a point charge; inside E = kqr / R³.

#### Capacitors and Dielectrics

A capacitor stores energy in an electric field. For a parallel-plate capacitor with plate area A and separation d, filled with a dielectric of relative permittivity εᵣ, C = ε₀εᵣA / d. Inserting a dielectric increases C by the factor εᵣ because the induced polarisation charges partially cancel the applied field. Energy stored is U = ½CV² = Q²/(2C) = ½QV.

#### Comparison Matrix — Capacitor Combinations

| Feature | Series Combination | Parallel Combination |
|---|---|---|
| Equivalent capacitance | 1/Cₛ = Σ(1/Cᵢ) — always smaller than the smallest C | Cₚ = ΣCᵢ — always larger than the largest C |
| Charge on each capacitor | Same (Q equal on all) | Different (Qᵢ = CᵢV) |
| Voltage across each | Different (Vᵢ = Q/Cᵢ) | Same (V identical on all) |
| Analogy to resistors | Direct (opposite of capacitors) | Inverse (opposite of capacitors) |

> 📌 **Formula Check:** ε₀ = 8.854×10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²); the permittivity of a medium is ε = ε₀εᵣ, NOT ε₀ alone.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** Three capacitors of 2 μF, 4 μF and 6 μF are connected: the 2 μF and 4 μF are in series, and their combination is in parallel with the 6 μF. The network is connected to a 12 V battery. Find (i) the equivalent capacitance, (ii) the charge stored on the 6 μF capacitor, and (iii) the energy stored in the 4 μF capacitor.

#### Solution:

(i) Series combination of 2 μF and 4 μF:
1/Cₛ = 1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4 (μF)⁻¹ → Cₛ = 4/3 μF ≈ 1.333 μF.

Parallel with 6 μF:
C_eq = 4/3 + 6 = 22/3 μF ≈ 7.333 μF.

(ii) Voltage across the parallel branch equals the battery voltage = 12 V. So Q₆ = C₆·V = 6 μF × 12 V = **72 μC**.

(iii) Voltage across the series branch is also 12 V. Charge on the series branch:
Qₛ = Cₛ × V = (4/3 μF) × 12 V = 16 μC.

Voltage across the 4 μF capacitor alone:
V₄ = Qₛ / C₄ = 16 μC / 4 μF = 4 V.

Energy stored:
U₄ = ½ C₄ V₄² = ½ × 4×10⁻⁶ × (4)² = ½ × 4×10⁻⁶ × 16 = **3.2×10⁻⁵ J = 32 μJ**.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students frequently give the series branch only 12 V across each capacitor, forgetting that in series the same CHARGE sits on each plate while the 12 V splits between them. Always find Qₛ first, then divide by each Cᵢ to get Vᵢ.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Boundary Conditions and Edge Cases

At the surface of a conductor, the electric field just outside is perpendicular to the surface and has magnitude E = σ/ε₀, where σ is the local surface charge density (this is a direct consequence of Gauss's law applied to a thin pillbox straddling the surface). Just inside, E = 0, so the field is discontinuous by exactly σ/ε₀ across the boundary.

For a dielectric, the field inside is reduced to E = E₀/εᵣ, and the displacement field D = ε₀E + P = ε₀εᵣE (SI units: C/m²). Breakdown occurs when E exceeds the dielectric strength (≈ 3×10⁶ V/m for air at STP), causing the material to ionise and conduct.

#### Mathematical Limits

- As r → 0, E = kq/r² diverges; the point-charge model breaks down once r approaches the charge's physical size, where continuous charge distributions must be used.
- For a uniformly charged sphere of total charge Q and radius R: outside (r ≥ R), E = kQ/r²; inside (r < R), E = kQr/R³, which is zero at the centre and maximum at r = R where it equals kQ/R² — matching the outside value, so E is continuous at the surface (no surface charge density on a non-conducting uniform sphere).
- The potential at infinity is defined as zero, so V on a conducting sphere of radius R carrying charge Q is V = kQ/R, identical at every point on and inside the sphere.

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics

- **Current electricity**: the capacitor relations link to RC time constants (τ = RC) when capacitors discharge through resistors.
- **Magnetic effects**: a moving charge produces a magnetic field, so electrostatic force qE is the stationary-charge limit of the Lorentz force F = q(E + v×B).
- **Modern physics**: Millikan's oil-drop experiment determines electron charge using electric fields balancing gravity, a direct application of qE = mg.
- **Van de Graaff generator**: a practical electrostatic device that transfers charge to a hollow conductor, where charge accumulates on the outer surface until breakdown voltage is reached — typically 10⁵–10⁶ V on small laboratory models.

#### Advanced Traps and Exceptions

1. **Direction of force on a negative charge**: F = qE gives a force opposite to E when q < 0, but the field itself still points away from positive sources.
2. **ε₀ vs ε**: a capacitor formula with ε (no subscript r) already includes the dielectric, so do not multiply by εᵣ again.
3. **Spherical vs uniform**: a conducting sphere has surface charge only at r = R with E = 0 inside; a non-conducting uniformly charged sphere has volume charge density ρ, giving a non-zero field at r < R.
4. **Gauss's law symmetry**: applying ∮ E·dA to an off-centre charge inside a sphere still gives Q/ε₀ — the integral does not require E to be constant, only flux.
5. **Capacitor energy**: U = ½CV² uses V across that capacitor, not the battery EMF if multiple capacitors are in series.

#### Practice Prompts

1. A point charge +5 μC is placed at the origin and another −2 μC at (3 m, 0). Find the point on the x-axis where the electric field is zero, and compute the potential there in volts.
2. A spherical conductor of radius 10 cm carries +8 nC. A Gaussian sphere of radius 15 cm is drawn concentrically. Compute the flux through it, the field at r = 15 cm, and the potential at r = 5 cm (inside the conductor).
