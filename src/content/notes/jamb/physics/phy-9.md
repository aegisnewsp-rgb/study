# Wave Optics (Interference and Diffraction)

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

- **Wave optics** treats light as a transverse electromagnetic wave to explain phenomena that geometric (ray) optics cannot, namely **interference** and **diffraction**.
- **Interference** requires **coherent sources** — two waves with a constant phase difference. Bright fringes appear where path difference = nλ; dark fringes where path difference = (n + ½)λ.
- In **Young's double-slit experiment**, the fringe spacing on the screen is **Δy = λD / d**, with λ the wavelength, D the slit-to-screen distance, and d the slit separation.
- For a **diffraction grating**, sharp maxima obey **d sin θ = nλ**, where d is the grating spacing and n = 0, ±1, ±2, …
- The **resolving power** of a grating is **R = λ/Δλ = nN**, growing with both the diffraction order (n) and the total number of slits (N).

| Quantity | Formula | Variable meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Fringe spacing | Δy = λD / d | λ wavelength, D slit-to-screen, d slit separation |
| Grating maxima | d sin θ = nλ | d grating spacing, n order, θ angle |
| Resolving power | R = nN | n order, N total slits |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Conditions for observable interference
Two sources must be **coherent**, meaning they emit waves of the same frequency with a constant phase difference. Independent sources such as two separate bulbs constantly shift their relative phase, so the interference pattern averages to uniform brightness. Thomas Young solved this in 1801 by using a single beam split at two narrow slits, producing the classic **Young's double-slit experiment** with alternating bright and dark bands.

#### Constructive versus destructive interference
Superposition gives two outcomes. **Constructive interference** occurs when waves arrive in phase, giving path difference Δx = nλ (n = 0, 1, 2 …) and a bright fringe. **Destructive interference** gives a dark fringe when Δx = (n + ½)λ. The path difference in a grating geometry is Δx = d sin θ, so maxima follow d sin θ = nλ.

#### Fringe spacing in Young's experiment
For small angles, sin θ ≈ tan θ ≈ y/D. Combining with d sin θ = nλ yields a linear fringe pattern on the screen with spacing

> **Δy = λD / d**

#### JAMB question patterns
- Direct substitution into Δy = λD/d with given λ, D, d.
- Identification of bright/dark fringe conditions given a path difference.
- Reading the grating equation d sin θ = nλ to find the order or wavelength.

A common trap is mixing **path difference** (Δx = d sin θ, in metres) with **fringe spacing** (Δy = λD/d, also in metres) and then plugging the wrong value into the wrong equation.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Single-slit diffraction versus double-slit interference
**Diffraction** is the spreading of a single wave as it passes through one aperture or around an obstacle; minima occur at a sin θ = mλ (m = ±1, ±2 …). **Interference** is the superposition of two or more coherent waves; maxima occur at d sin θ = nλ. In double-slit experiments both effects occur simultaneously: each slit diffracts, and the two resulting wavefronts interfere to produce fringes whose envelope is the single-slit diffraction pattern.

#### Diffraction grating resolution
A grating with many slits (typically 10³–10⁶ lines per metre) produces much sharper maxima than two slits because the principal maxima are reinforced by N coherent contributions. **Resolving power R = nN** quantifies the ability to distinguish two wavelengths λ and λ + Δλ. To resolve the sodium D-line doublet (λ ≈ 589 nm, Δλ ≈ 0.6 nm) in second order (n = 2), a grating needs N ≥ 589/(2 × 0.6) ≈ 491 slits illuminated.

#### Edge cases and adjacent links
- When λ ≪ aperture size, wave effects vanish and **geometric optics** dominates — the regime of mirrors and lenses.
- The **Huygens–Fresnel principle** unifies both phenomena: every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets.
- JAMB occasionally links this topic to **polarisation** (transverse wave evidence) and to the **photoelectric effect**, where the photon picture replaces the wave picture at very low intensity.

#### Common mistakes
- Writing d sin θ = (n + ½)λ for bright fringes instead of dark.
- Omitting the factor n in R = nN, halving the calculated resolving power.
- Treating interference and diffraction as the same mechanism.

#### Practice prompts
1. Monochromatic light of wavelength 600 nm falls on two slits 0.20 mm apart, with the screen 2.0 m away. Compute Δy and the distance from the central maximum to the third bright fringe.
2. A grating with 5 × 10⁵ lines per metre is illuminated at normal incidence by light containing 500 nm and 510 nm. In the second order, will these two lines be resolved?
