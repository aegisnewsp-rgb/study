# Constitutional Supremacy and Sources of Law

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Uganda's legal system rests on the **1995 Constitution** (supreme under **Article 2**), Acts of Parliament, common law, doctrines of equity, and customary law (recognised under **Article 246**). The court hierarchy ascends: **Magistrates Courts → High Court → Court of Appeal → Supreme Court** — the Supreme Court is final, not the High Court. The **Law Council of Uganda**, established under the **Advocates Act**, regulates admission to the Bar, supervises the Bar Course, and disciplines advocates; it is **not** the same body as the **Uganda Law Society (ULS)**, which is the voluntary bar association. Before enrolment, a candidate must complete the **Bar Course** and a period of **pupillage** under a practising advocate. The **Judicial Service Commission (JSC)** appoints and disciplines judicial officers. Expect short MCQs asking which body performs which function — memorising the four-tier court structure and the Law Council's mandate yields quick marks in the 3%-weighted GK paper.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Constitutional Supremacy and Sources of Law
The **Constitution of Uganda 1995** is the supreme law; any law inconsistent with it is void to the extent of the inconsistency (**Article 2**). Other sources include Acts of Parliament, subsidiary legislation, common law (received from England), doctrines of equity, and customary law. Customary law is expressly preserved under **Article 246** but cannot operate where it conflicts with the Constitution or written statute.

#### The Court Hierarchy
Uganda's courts are structured in ascending order of jurisdiction:

| Court | Key Jurisdiction |
|---|---|
| Magistrates Courts | Summary criminal matters; small civil claims (limits set by the Magistrates Courts Act) |
| High Court | Unlimited original civil and criminal jurisdiction; constitutional matters under Article 139 |
| Court of Appeal | Appeals from the High Court |
| Supreme Court | Final appellate court; constitutional interpretation |

#### The Law Council of Uganda
Established under the **Advocates Act (Cap. 267)**, the Law Council admits advocates to the Roll, sets and administers the Bar Course, and handles disciplinary proceedings. Every intending advocate must complete pupillage and pass the Bar Course before enrolment.

#### The Uganda Law Society
The ULS is a membership bar association that promotes ethics, runs continuing legal education, and protects advocates' interests — it does **not** regulate admission; that is the Law Council's exclusive role.

#### Exam Pattern
GK questions typically appear as MCQs testing identification of: the highest court, the body responsible for admission, the constitutional article establishing supremacy, and which institution handles which function.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Distinguishing the Regulatory Bodies
The single most-tested trap in this topic is conflating the **Law Council** with the **Uganda Law Society (ULS)**. The Law Council is a statutory body created by the Advocates Act with mandatory regulatory powers — admission, pupillage supervision, Bar Course administration, and disciplinary control. The ULS is a voluntary professional association funded by subscriptions; it advocates for members, runs CPD, and contributes to law reform but has no statutory power to admit or discipline advocates. In a typical MCQ, "Which body admits advocates to the Bar?" the correct answer is the **Law Council**, never the ULS.

#### Limits of Court Jurisdiction
Do not assume the High Court has unlimited reach in every category — its criminal jurisdiction is bounded by the **Penal Code** and the **Magistrates Courts Act**, and certain specialised matters fall to tribunals. Conversely, Magistrates Courts have **statutory limits** on sentence length and pecuniary jurisdiction; not every civil suit can begin there.

#### Common Mistakes
- Treating customary law as automatically inferior — it is constitutionally recognised unless repugnant to natural justice or inconsistent with the Constitution.
- Naming the High Court as Uganda's highest court.
- Confusing pupillage (practical training) with the Bar Course (academic).
