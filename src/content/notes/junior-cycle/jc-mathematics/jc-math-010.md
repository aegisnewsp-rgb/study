# Circle Theorems and Coordinate Geometry — Junior Cycle Mathematics Notes

These two areas sit in the same topic because the exam combines them: a diagram of a circle with labelled points is used to prove an angle result, and a graph on an axis grid is used to read off a slope. Both require you to recognise which named fact applies rather than to compute.

---

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Circle theorems

Four results carry most of the marks. Learn them as statements, not as pictures.

**The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference standing on the same arc.** The converse holds: if the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference, the point is on the circle. This is the workhorse of nearly every angle-in-a-circle question.

**Angles in the same segment are equal.** Any two angles subtended by the same chord from the same side of the chord are equal. Practically: mark the chord, then any angle standing on it from the same segment has that value.

**Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral sum to 180°.** A quadrilateral whose four vertices lie on a circle is *cyclic*, and its opposite angles are supplementary.

**The angle between a tangent and a chord equals the angle in the alternate segment.** The tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact, which gives a second route to most of these angles.

#### Writing the proof

Name the theorem, name the subject, state the conclusion. For example: *angle AOB = 2 × angle ACB, by the theorem that the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference standing on arc AB.*

Candidates lose marks by drawing an accurate diagram and writing nothing — the diagram is not the proof. Equally, candidates who write "by the circle theorem" without naming which one fail, because the marking scheme specifies the theorem.

#### Common set-ups

- **Triangle inscribed in a circle, centre O.** Draw radii to the two vertices not in the angle, and the isosceles triangles give you base angles. This is the standard route for finding an angle when a diameter is involved.
- **Diameter as a chord.** The angle in a semicircle is a right angle — an application of the cyclic quadrilateral rule on the circle's own centre point, or of Thales' theorem. This converts a "hard" circle question into a Pythagoras question.
- **Two tangents from an external point.** They are equal, so the triangle they form is isosceles; combined with the tangent–radius perpendicular, this gives a right angle at each point of contact and a fixed angle at the centre of 180° minus the central angle.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Coordinate geometry

**Gradient** = (*y*₂ − *y*₁) ÷ (*x*₂ − *x*₁), equivalent to rise over run. Read a graph's gradient from a grid rather than from a drawing when the question supplies one.

**Lines are parallel** if their gradients are equal; **perpendicular** if the product of the gradients is −1. The same rule does not cover both cases, and confusing the two is the standard error.

**Midpoint** of a segment is the average of the endpoint coordinates.

**Equation of a line**, either point–slope or *Ax* + *By* + *C* = 0. Rearranged form *y* = *mx* + *c* is the one to use when you need to substitute from a graph.

**Distance** = √[(*x*₂ − *x*₁*)² + (*y*₂ − *y*₁*)²]. Name the two points and the formula before substituting.

**Reflections and translations on a grid.** Reflecting in a horizontal or vertical line changes one coordinate's sign and leaves the other; reflecting in the line *y* = *x* swaps the coordinates. This connects directly to the transformations topic and is often the quickest route through a diagram question.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Combining the two

The exam's hardest items in this block place a circle on a grid, give the centre and a point, and ask for another point — for example the point diametrically opposite a given one, which is found by doubling the centre's coordinates and subtracting the given point's. Or they give two intersection points of a line and a circle and ask for the third, which uses the fact that the perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord.

The habit that resolves these: **write down the known quantities before you look for anything**. The centre, the radius, the two points, and whether the line is a chord or a tangent determine which route is available.

## Self-check

1. Points A, B, C lie on a circle with centre O, and the angle AOB is 80°. What is the angle ACB? *(40°, by the theorem that the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference standing on the same arc. Note that this requires C to be on the major arc; if C is on the minor arc the angle is 180° − 40° = 140°, which the diagram must resolve.)*
2. Two lines have gradients ½ and −2. What is their relationship? *(Perpendicular, because the product is −1. Equal gradients would mean parallel; here the product test is what applies.)*
3. A circle's centre is (2, −3). What is the point diametrically opposite (5, 1)? *(Diameter endpoints satisfy centre = midpoint. So the opposite point is (2 × 2 − 5, 2 × (−3) − 1) = (−1, −7). Watch the sign on the *y* term — it comes from doubling −3.)*
4. Why is a drawing not a proof in a circle question? *(The diagram shows one case; the theorem is what establishes it for all cases. The mark scheme wants the named theorem with the named subject and the conclusion, and an unlabelled accurate drawing earns none of it.)*
5. A chord passes through a point, and the perpendicular from the centre meets it there. What does that tell you about the two halves of the chord? *(They are equal. The perpendicular from the centre bisects any chord, which is the fact that makes a third intersection point computable once two are known.)*
