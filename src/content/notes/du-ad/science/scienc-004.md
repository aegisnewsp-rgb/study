# Kinetic Energy (KE)

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Kinetic energy is the energy a body carries because it is moving. A particle of mass *m* moving at speed *v* holds translational kinetic energy KE = ½mv², a scalar measured in joules (J). Doubling the speed quadruples the KE, because velocity enters as a square.

| Quantity | Symbol | Meaning | SI unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mass | m | Inertia of the body | kg |
| Speed | v | Magnitude of velocity | m·s⁻¹ |
| Kinetic Energy | KE | Energy of motion | J = kg·m²·s⁻² |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"Half Mass Vee-Squared"** — KE = ½ m v². Recall the *½* first; the most common slip on the DU Admission paper is to drop the half and write mv², which is double the correct answer.

---

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Definition and Formula

Translational kinetic energy for a rigid body of mass *m* moving at speed *v* is KE = ½mv², where *m* is in kilogram (kg), *v* in metre per second (m·s⁻¹), giving units of [M L² T⁻²], equivalent to the joule (1 J = 1 kg·m²·s⁻²). Because the formula contains v², the sign of *v* is irrelevant: KE is non-negative and direction-free.

#### Work–Energy Theorem

The net work done on a body equals its change in kinetic energy:

W_net = ΔKE = ½ m v_f² − ½ m v_i²

with W_net in joules, v_f the final speed, and v_i the initial speed. A force that accelerates a body from rest (v_i = 0) to speed *v* deposits exactly ½mv² of work into the system.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

Students routinely confuse related quantities. The table below pins down the differences most often exploited by MCQ distractors.

| Feature | Kinetic Energy (½mv²) | Momentum (mv) | Rotational KE (½Iω²) | Angular Momentum (Iω) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Type | Scalar | Vector | Scalar | Vector |
| Dependence on motion | ∝ v² | ∝ v | ∝ ω² | ∝ ω |
| SI unit | J | kg·m·s⁻¹ | J | kg·m²·s⁻¹ |
| Frame dependent? | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Conserved when? | Only if W_net = 0 | When net external force = 0 | When net torque = 0 | When net external torque = 0 |

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** A 2 kg block slides across a frictionless surface. A constant horizontal force accelerates it from 3 m·s⁻¹ to 5 m·s⁻¹. What is the net work done on the block?

(A) 8 J
(B) 16 J
(C) 25 J
(D) 50 J

#### Solution:
1. Identify initial and final kinetic energies using KE = ½mv², with m = 2 kg.
2. KE_i = ½ × 2 × (3)² = 9 J.
3. KE_f = ½ × 2 × (5)² = 25 J.
4. Apply the Work–Energy Theorem: W_net = KE_f − KE_i = 25 − 9 = 16 J.
5. Answer: **(B) 16 J**.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Choice (C) 25 J tempts students who take only the final KE and forget to subtract the initial KE. Choice (D) 50 J targets the slip of writing KE = mv² (missing the ½) or doubling 25 J by mistake.

#### Standard Problem Types on DU Admission

- Numerical: compute KE given m and v; compute v given m and KE.
- Conceptual: identify KE as a scalar or note that doubling v quadruples KE.
- Application: use W_net = ΔKE to find work or change in speed.
- Discrimination: pick KE from a list that also contains momentum and force.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Derivation from Kinematics

Start from W = F·s with constant force. Substituting F = ma and s = ½(v_f² − v_i²)/a gives W = ma × ½(v_f² − v_i²)/a = ½ m v_f² − ½ m v_i² = ΔKE. This shows the work–energy theorem is not an extra rule but a direct consequence of Newton's second law under constant force.

#### Rotational and Relativistic Extensions

For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis, the analogue is KE_rot = ½ I ω², where I is the moment of inertia (kg·m²) and ω the angular speed (rad·s⁻¹). At speeds approaching the speed of light, the classical formula fails; the relativistic form KE = (γ − 1)mc² applies, with γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²). For v ≪ c, γ − 1 ≈ ½ v²/c², recovering the classical ½mv².

#### Edge Cases and Traps

1. **Momentum–KE conversion.** From p = mv and KE = ½mv², one obtains KE = p²/(2m) and p = √(2m·KE). Confusing these is a frequent MCQ trap.
2. **Frame dependence.** Two observers in relative motion assign different v, hence different KE to the same body; this is not an error, it is built into the definition.
3. **Thermal KE of gases.** The average translational KE per molecule of an ideal gas is ½m⟨v²⟩ = (3/2) k_B T, where k_B = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J·K⁻¹, linking kinetic theory to absolute temperature in kelvin.
4. **Unit slips.** Substituting mass in grams or velocity in km/h without conversion inflates or deflates KE by factors of 1000 or 3.6² = 12.96.
5. **Sign convention.** KE is always ≥ 0; a negative value signals a wrong formula, not a physical state.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. A neutron (m = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg) moves at 2.2 × 10⁴ m·s⁻¹. Compute its KE in joules and its de Broglie wavelength hint using p = √(2m·KE).
2. A flywheel has I = 0.5 kg·m² and spins at 1200 rpm. Find its rotational KE and the constant braking torque that brings it to rest in 10 s.
