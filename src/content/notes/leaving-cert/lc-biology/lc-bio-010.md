# Enzymes — Leaving Certificate (Established) Biology Notes

Enzymes are the first numbered unit of Strand 2 and they sit underneath almost everything that follows: photosynthesis, respiration, digestion, protein synthesis and fermentation are all enzyme-catalysed. The weight is only 3, but the learning outcome says *investigate factors affecting the rate of enzyme-catalysed reactions, use primary and secondary data to support conclusions*. That means the unit is examined through graphs and datasets, not through definitions, and it is one of the easiest places in the paper to pick up a full question's marks if you can read a rate curve properly.

Two ideas do most of the work. First, an enzyme **lowers activation energy** without being used up, which is what makes a reaction fast enough to happen at body temperature. Second, enzyme activity depends on **3D shape**, so anything that changes that shape changes the rate. Temperature and pH act through shape; substrate and enzyme concentration act through collision frequency. Once you can sort every factor into one of those two categories, the graphs write themselves.

---

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Enzymes are biological catalysts**: they speed up reactions without being consumed, they are not used up in the reaction, and they lower the **activation energy** — the minimum energy needed for reactants to reach the transition state and react.
- **Most enzymes are proteins** (a minority are RNA, called ribozymes). They are large and globular, and they have an **active site** — a region whose shape and charge distribution match a specific substrate.
- **Lock-and-key vs induced fit.** The older lock-and-key model says the active site is rigidly complementary. The specification asks for the **induced fit model**: the substrate binds and *causes the active site to change shape slightly* so that it closes around the substrate and catalyses the reaction. This is why the enzyme must be flexible, and why changing pH — which alters the charges holding the tertiary structure — can stop binding altogether.
- **Enzyme specificity.** Each enzyme catalyses one type of reaction on one type of substrate, because of the shape and chemistry of its active site. This is why a protease made in the pancreas cannot digest carbohydrate.
- **Enzymes are affected by four factors**, and the specification names all four: **substrate concentration, enzyme concentration, pH and temperature**. Each has an optimum value.
- **Temperature.** Rate rises with temperature as molecules move faster and collide more often, up to an **optimum** (about 37 °C for human enzymes, higher for thermophilic bacteria). Beyond the optimum the rate falls sharply because the enzyme **denatures** — its tertiary structure changes irreversibly, the active site no longer fits, and it can no longer be reactivated on cooling.
- **pH.** Each enzyme has an optimum pH. Pepsin works at about pH 2 in the stomach; trypsin at about pH 8 in the small intestine. Departures from the optimum alter the charges in the active site and reduce binding; extreme pH denatures.
- **Substrate concentration.** Rate rises as substrate is added because more enzyme-substrate complexes form — until every enzyme molecule is saturated with substrate, at which point rate plateaus. **Adding more substrate beyond that point does nothing.**
- **Enzyme concentration.** Rate rises in direct proportion to enzyme concentration at constant substrate, because there are more active sites available — until substrate becomes limiting.
- **Competitive inhibition.** The inhibitor has a shape similar to the substrate and binds to the active site. It can be overcome by raising substrate concentration. **Non-competitive inhibition** binds at a different site and changes the active site's shape; raising substrate does not overcome it.
- **Industrial uses** the specification names: food and beverages, biofuels, medicine and pharmaceuticals. **Immobilised enzymes** — enzymes fixed to a solid support or membrane — are reusable, separate from the product, and can be used at higher temperature and pH without denaturing.

#### Examiner traps

- **Saying enzymes are used up.** They are not. Each enzyme molecule can catalyse many reaction cycles.
- **Writing "the enzyme denatures at high temperature" without saying why.** Denaturation changes the tertiary structure so the active site no longer complements the substrate — the shape argument is the mark.
- **Extending the temperature curve.** The fall after the optimum is steep, not gradual; a gentle decline suggests you are not thinking about denaturation.
- **Claiming more substrate always increases rate.** It plateaus at saturation. Saying "rate increases indefinitely" is the classic error.
- **Confusing competitive and non-competitive inhibition on a graph.** Competitive lines converge at high substrate; non-competitive lines plateau below.
- **Listing industries without naming an enzyme.** Naming the enzyme or the product — amylase in bread making, rennin in cheese, lipase in detergents, Taq polymerase in PCR — is what earns the mark.

#### 30-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Can you define activation energy in one sentence?
- [ ] Can you describe induced fit in two sentences without using the phrase "lock and key"?
- [ ] Can you sketch a temperature curve with the optimum labelled and the denaturation region marked?
- [ ] Can you state the four factors the specification names?
- [ ] Can you distinguish competitive from non-competitive inhibition from a pair of curves?

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### Reading the rate curves

**Temperature.** Rate increases to an optimum, then drops almost vertically to zero. The vertical drop is the evidence for denaturation. On a question that asks you to *explain* the shape, you need both halves: below the optimum, more kinetic energy means more successful collisions; above it, denaturation removes functional enzyme faster than the collision benefit.

*Worked question.* An enzyme solution is heated from 10 °C to 80 °C and the rate is measured every 5 °C. The rate rises until 55 °C and then falls to zero by 70 °C. Explain the results.
*A.* From 10 °C to 55 °C the molecules gain kinetic energy and collide with the enzyme more often and more energetically, so more enzyme-substrate complexes form and the rate rises. Above 55 °C the rise in collision rate is outweighed by denaturation: the protein's tertiary structure is disrupted, the active site loses its complementary shape, and enzyme activity falls. By 70 °C essentially all the enzyme is denatured and the rate is zero. The fall is steep rather than gradual because denaturation is progressive — once the hydrogen and ionic bonds holding the tertiary structure are broken, the active site cannot be reformed on cooling.

**pH.** Bell-shaped curves. The optimum reflects the ionisation state of the amino acid side chains that form the active site and hold the tertiary structure. If the question names a specific enzyme, use the real optimum — pepsin at pH 2, amylase at about pH 7, trypsin at about pH 8 — because naming it shows you understand the mechanism rather than memorising a shape.

**Substrate concentration.** Hyperbolic, then a plateau at Vmax. Vmax occurs when every enzyme molecule is saturated. The practical consequence is that substrate is rarely the limiting factor in a well-run industrial process; the enzyme cost is.

**Enzyme concentration.** Linear increase at constant substrate, up to the point where substrate becomes limiting.

#### Inhibition read from a graph

Give two enzyme concentrations under two conditions and the shape tells you the mechanism.

- **Competitive.** Both curves rise with substrate and **converge at high substrate concentration**. The high-concentration curve can reach the same Vmax. The inhibitor occupied the active site, so raising substrate out-competes it.
- **Non-competitive.** Both curves plateau, and the inhibited curve reaches a **lower Vmax** than the uninhibited one, and the two lines stay parallel or never meet. The inhibitor binds elsewhere and reduces the amount of functional enzyme, so adding more substrate cannot help.

A single curve that plateaus lower than the control is non-competitive. Two curves that meet at high substrate are competitive. That is the whole rule.

#### Designing the investigation the outcome asks for

The specification wants you to investigate factors affecting the rate of enzyme-catalysed reactions using primary and secondary data. A workable practical is catalase in liver or potato, or amylase and starch.

1. **Independent variable** — the factor you change (temperature, pH, substrate concentration).
2. **Dependent variable** — what you measure. Choose something you can actually time or count: the time for a fixed volume of gas, the time for a colour change, or the mass of product formed in a fixed time.
3. **Controlled variables** — everything else: enzyme concentration, substrate concentration (if not the variable), volume of reaction mixture, pH (if not the variable), and the apparatus.
4. **Controls** — a tube with enzyme replaced by distilled water, and/or boiled enzyme to show that the reaction requires a functional protein.
5. **Repeats** — at least three per condition, and use the mean. A single reading is not evidence.
6. **Graph it** — rate on the y-axis, factor on the x-axis. Because rate is inversely related to time, plot **1/time** as the rate proxy, or convert your time readings to rate and say so; plotting raw time against temperature produces a curve of the wrong shape and is a frequent reason for losing the analysis mark.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Worked Higher-level questions with answers

**Q1.** Explain why an enzyme is not used up but is eventually lost from a cell.
*A.* An enzyme is a catalyst, so it lowers activation energy and returns to its original form at the end of each catalytic cycle — it is not consumed. However, cells continuously break down and replace proteins, including enzymes, through proteolysis in the proteasome and lysosomes, and enzymes are lost if the cell is damaged or dies. Turnover, not use-up, is why the concentration falls. An immobilised enzyme, by contrast, is physically recovered after each batch, which is exactly why it is preferred industrially.

**Q2.** A student digests starch and protein together in the same tube at 37 °C and pH 7, then finds only partial digestion of both.
*A.* The pH is the problem. Salivary and pancreatic amylase works best at about pH 7, but the protease pepsin requires about pH 2, so at pH 7 it is largely inactive. Even if the enzymes are individually functional, optimal conditions differ between them, and a mixture cannot be optimised for both. The control comparison would be two separate tubes, one at pH 7 for starch and one at pH 2 for protein, each with the appropriate substrate and enzyme. This is a strong answer because it identifies the variable and then states the fix.

**Q3.** Explain two commercial advantages of immobilising an enzyme.
*A.* First, the enzyme can be recovered and reused, which lowers the cost per unit of product over many batches. Second, because the enzyme is held in a fixed form it is more resistant to temperature and pH changes, so the process can run at conditions that would denalyse a free enzyme, raising yield. Third — and this is often the mark that separates a top-band answer — immobilising the enzyme separates it from the product, so the product stream is uncontaminated and needs no downstream removal step, which matters enormously in pharmaceuticals and in the food industry.

**Q4.** An enzyme has an optimum temperature of 60 °C. A student argues the enzyme from a thermophilic bacterium would be identical to a human enzyme because both are proteins. Evaluate.
*A.* The student's reasoning is wrong because enzymes are specified by the shape of their active site, and shape is determined by the amino acid sequence, which is determined by the gene sequence and differs between species. An enzyme with an optimum of 60 °C has a tertiary structure that stays intact at that temperature; a human enzyme with an optimum of 37 °C does not. Both are proteins and both use the same chemical mechanisms, but the specific interactions in the active site differ. Selection under different temperature conditions is what produced the difference, which links this unit straight back to unit 1.5.

#### Where enzymes appear again

- **Unit 2.2** — the light-independent reactions of photosynthesis are catalysed by **RuBisCO**, the most abundant enzyme on Earth; glycolysis and the citric acid cycle are enzyme-catalysed throughout.
- **Unit 2.3** — enzymes are required for DNA replication, transcription and translation, including **polymerase**.
- **Unit 2.6** — amylase, protease and lipase in digestion, with the pH at each named location.
- **Unit 3.2** — enzymes in microbial growth and in industry, including the immobilised enzymes used in food production.
- **Unit 3.3** — **Taq polymerase**, a thermostable enzyme from *Thermus aquaticus*, is what makes PCR possible because it survives the 95 °C denaturation step.
- **Biology in Practice Investigation** (40% of the grade) — an enzyme investigation supplies primary data, a hypothesis and a justified conclusion directly, and the descriptors reward exactly that.

#### Mistakes students actually make

1. **Omitting the shape argument.** "It denatures" is half a mark; "the tertiary structure changes so the active site no longer complements the substrate" is the mark.
2. **Saying the enzyme is used up**, or that a small amount of catalyst "produces" a large amount of energy.
3. **Plotting reaction time instead of rate** and then describing the trend as though it were the rate curve.
4. **No control.** A tube with boiled enzyme, or with water replacing the enzyme, is what shows the effect is due to the enzyme rather than the substrate breaking down on its own.
5. **Ignoring repeats**, or quoting a single reading where a mean is expected.
6. **Naming an industry without a named enzyme or product.** "In the food industry" is a half answer; "rennin in cheese manufacture" is a full one.
7. **Asserting a sharp fall is measurement error.** It is not — it is denaturation, and dismissing it as experimental artefact is the wrong conclusion.
