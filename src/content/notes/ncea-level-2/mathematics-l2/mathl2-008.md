# Design a questionnaire (91263) — NCEA Level 2 Mathematics Notes

Achievement Standard 91263 is an internal standard (3 credits) that tests the design of a questionnaire to investigate a research question. It is part of the Level 2 statistics strand and connects to 91264 (statistical inference) and 91265 (conducting an experiment). The standard emphasises question design, sampling, ethical considerations, and pilot testing.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **3 credits**, internally assessed by the school, NZQA moderated.
- Topics: question types (closed vs open, structured vs unstructured), sampling methods (random, stratified, systematic, cluster, convenience), wording bias, layout, ethical considerations, pilot testing.
- **Merit** requires relational thinking — designing a questionnaire that fits the research question and population.
- **Excellence** requires extended abstract thinking — justifying design choices, reflecting on limitations, generalising the design principles.

#### Examiner traps

- Using only open-ended questions — closed questions are easier to analyse but open questions capture nuance; the best questionnaires mix both.
- Skipping the pilot test — pilot testing is part of the marking schedule.
- Confusing sampling methods — random sampling gives every member of the population an equal chance; stratified sampling ensures subgroup representation.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Question types

**Closed questions** have a fixed set of responses. They are easier to analyse and compare but can lose nuance.

- Yes/No: "Do you exercise daily?"
- Multiple choice: "How often do you exercise? (a) Daily (b) Weekly (c) Monthly (d) Never"
- Likert scale: "Rate your agreement with the statement (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree)"

**Open questions** allow free responses. They capture nuance but are harder to analyse.

- "What do you enjoy most about exercising?"
- "Describe your typical exercise routine."

The best questionnaires mix question types: closed questions for the main variables, open questions for nuance and follow-up.

#### Sampling methods

- **Random sampling** — every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected. Reduces selection bias.
- **Stratified sampling** — the population is divided into strata (e.g., year groups) and a random sample is taken from each stratum. Ensures subgroup representation.
- **Systematic sampling** — every kth member of the population is selected (e.g., every 10th customer). Easy to implement but can introduce bias if the population has a pattern.
- **Cluster sampling** — the population is divided into clusters (e.g., schools) and whole clusters are randomly selected. Useful when the population is geographically dispersed.
- **Convenience sampling** — whoever is easy to reach. Cheap but biased.

#### Wording bias

A question with biased wording can skew the results. Common sources of bias:

- Leading questions: "Don't you agree that exercise is essential?" (assumes the answer is yes).
- Double-barrelled questions: "Do you exercise and eat healthily?" (two questions in one).
- Vague terms: "How often do you exercise regularly?" (what does "regularly" mean?).
- Loaded words: "Don't you think our school lunch programme is a disaster?"

#### Layout

A well-laid-out questionnaire:

- Has a clear introduction explaining the purpose and expected time.
- Groups questions by topic.
- Uses consistent response formats within each section.
- Avoids cramped spacing.
- Ends with a thank-you and an opt-out for the data to be removed.

#### Ethical considerations

- **Informed consent** — participants know what they are agreeing to.
- **Confidentiality** — responses are anonymous; data is stored securely.
- **Right to withdraw** — participants can opt out at any time.
- **Cultural sensitivity** — questions are appropriate for the cultural context (in NZ, this includes te ao Māori considerations).
- **School approval** — for school-based surveys, the school has approved the questionnaire.

#### Pilot testing

A pilot test is a small-scale trial of the questionnaire (typically 5-10 people from the target population). The purpose is to:

- Check that questions are understood as intended.
- Estimate the time to complete.
- Identify wording or layout issues.
- Test the data-collection process.

After the pilot test, revise the questionnaire based on the feedback before the full rollout.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Sample size considerations

A larger sample gives more precise estimates (narrower confidence intervals) but costs more to collect. At Level 2, the rule of thumb is:

- For a population estimate with ±5% precision, a sample of about 400 is sufficient for a population of more than 10,000.
- For subgroup estimates, the sample for the smallest subgroup must be at least 30 to make statistical inference valid.

#### Validity and reliability

- **Validity** — the questionnaire measures what it claims to measure. A questionnaire about "exercise habits" should ask about exercise, not just gym membership.
- **Reliability** — the questionnaire produces consistent results on repeated use. A reliable questionnaire gives similar results for similar respondents.

Pilot testing helps establish both validity (are we asking the right things?) and reliability (are we asking clearly?).

#### Excellence in design

The Excellence-level design touches every aspect of the questionnaire:

- The questions are clearly linked to the research question.
- The sampling method is justified (random sampling is preferred when feasible).
- The wording is unbiased.
- The layout is professional.
- The pilot test led to specific revisions.
- The ethical considerations are addressed in the introduction.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Random sampling means asking anyone." No — random sampling means every member has an equal chance; convenience sampling is asking anyone.
- "Closed questions are always better." No — closed questions lose nuance. A mixed questionnaire is usually best.
- "Pilot testing is optional." No — it is part of the marking schedule.
- "Sampling method doesn't matter." It does — biased sampling gives biased conclusions.
