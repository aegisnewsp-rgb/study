# Haloalkanes

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Haloalkanes** (alkyl halides) are saturated aliphatic compounds where one or more **H atoms** of an alkane are replaced by halogen atoms (**F, Cl, Br, I**). The general formula for a saturated mono-haloalkane is **CₙH₂ₙ₊₁X**, with **X bonded to an sp³ carbon** through a polar covalent C–X sigma bond.

The C–X bond is polar — carbon carries **δ⁺** and halogen carries **δ⁻** — making the α-carbon electrophilic and reactive toward **nucleophiles**.

| Property | C–F | C–Cl | C–Br | C–I |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bond length (pm) | 139 | 177 | 191 | 214 |
| Bond enthalpy (kJ mol⁻¹) | 485 | 339 | 276 | 238 |

**Bond enthalpy** decreases down the group, so **reactivity in SN2 follows RI > RBr > RCl > RF** (inverse of bond strength, not molecular mass).

#### High-yield NEET pointers:
1. **SN2** → one-step, **Walden inversion**, favoured by **1°** substrates, polar aprotic solvent, strong nucleophile.
2. **SN1** → two-step via **carbocation**, **racemisation**, favoured by **3°** substrates, polar protic solvent.
3. Name reactions to memorise: **Wurtz, Finkelstein, Swarts, Hunsdiecker, Grignard formation**.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Classification and Structure

Haloalkanes are classified by (a) number of halogen atoms — **mono, di, tri, poly** — and (b) hybridisation of the carbon bearing X. An **sp³ carbon** with one X is the textbook haloalkane; **sp² carbons** give vinylic (C=C–X) and aryl (Ar–X) halides, which behave very differently.

The **C–X bond** is polar covalent because halogens are more electronegative than carbon. This polarity activates the α-carbon toward **nucleophilic substitution (SN)** and makes β-hydrogens available for **elimination (E)**.

#### Preparation Methods

| Method | Reagent / Condition | Product type |
|---|---|---|
| Alcohol → haloalkane | HCl/ZnCl₂ (Lucas), PCl₃, PCl₅, SOCl₂ | R–Cl |
| Alkene → haloalkane | HX (Markownikoff); HBr + peroxide (anti-Markownikoff) | R–X |
| Halogen exchange | NaI/acetone (Finkelstein); AgF or SbF₃ (Swarts) | R–I or R–F |
| Hunsdiecker | RCOOAg + Br₂ / CCl₄ | R–Br (one C less) |
| Free-radical halogenation | Cl₂ or Br₂ / hν (alkane) | R–X (mixture) |

> **Exam tip:** Anti-Markownikoff addition works **only with HBr** in the presence of peroxides (peroxide effect). Applying the peroxide rule to HCl or HI is a deliberate NEET trap.

#### Nucleophilic Substitution — SN1 vs SN2

| Feature | SN2 | SN1 |
|---|---|---|
| Steps | One (concerted) | Two (via carbocation) |
| Kinetics | Rate = k[RX][Nu⁻] | Rate = k[RX] |
| Stereochemistry | **Inversion** (Walden) | **Racemisation** |
| Substrate preference | 1° > 2° > 3° | 3° > 2° > 1° |
| Solvent | Polar aprotic | Polar protic |
| Nucleophile | Strong required | Weak acceptable |

**Allylic** (C=C–C–X) and **benzylic** (C₆H₅–CH₂–X) halides are exceptionally reactive in both SN1 and SN2 because the intermediate carbocation is **resonance-stabilised**.

#### Elimination and Grignard Chemistry

**E2** (anti-periplanar, bimolecular) gives the **Saytzeff** product — the more substituted alkene — with small bases like EtO⁻. With **bulky bases** (t-BuOK) or quaternary ammonium salts, the **Hofmann product** (less substituted) dominates.

**Grignard reagents** form by refluxing R–X with **Mg turnings in dry ether**:
R–X + Mg → R–Mg–X. Any trace of water or alcohol gives RH + Mg(OH)X, destroying the reagent.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases in Reactivity

**Vinyl and aryl halides are practically inert** to SN1 and SN2 because:
1. The C–X carbon is **sp²**, holding the electrons more tightly.
2. The shorter C–X bond has higher bond enthalpy.
3. Phenyl cations are highly unstable, ruling out the SN1 pathway.

In contrast, **allylic and benzylic** halides are **super-reactive** — students confuse these pairs because of similar names. Memorise: "allyl/benzyl = reactive; vinyl/aryl = unreactive."

#### Worked Stereochemistry Example

Consider (R)-2-bromobutane reacting with NaI in acetone (SN2 conditions):
CH₃–CHBr–CH₂–CH₃ (R) + I⁻ → CH₃–CHI–CH₂–CH₃ (S) + Br⁻
A 100% **Walden inversion** occurs — the (R) substrate gives a single (S) enantiomer. In SN1 on the same substrate, the planar carbocation is attacked equally from both faces, giving a **racemic mixture** (50:50 R:S). NEET frequently tests this distinction using a chiral substrate and asking which mechanism is implied.

#### Environmental and Biological Context

**DDT** (dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane) is a polyhaloalkane responsible for **biomagnification** in food chains due to its lipophilicity and resistance to degradation. **Freons (CFCs)** such as CF₂Cl₂ release chlorine radicals in the stratosphere that catalytically destroy **ozone** — directly tested in environmental chemistry assertion-reason questions.

#### Common NEET Traps

- **Finkelstein** works because **NaCl/NaBr precipitate** in dry acetone, not because NaI is "more reactive."
- **Saytzeff** vs **Hofmann** depends on the **base used**, not the substrate.
- **Lucas test** ordering: tertiary forms turbidity instantly; secondary in 3–5 min; primary shows no reaction at room temperature.
- Higher molecular mass does **not** mean higher SN reactivity — it tracks **C–X bond strength** (C–I is weakest).

#### Two Practice Prompts

1. Identify the major product when 2-bromo-2-methylpropane is treated with aqueous KOH versus alcoholic KOH. (Answer: SN1 → tert-butanol; E2 → 2-methylpropene via Saytzeff.)
2. Predict the configuration of the product when (S)-1-bromo-1-phenylethane reacts with CH₃COO⁻ via SN1, then via SN2. (SN1: racemic; SN2: complete inversion to R.)
