# Venn Diagrams

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **Venn diagram** uses overlapping closed curves (usually circles) so each curve represents a **set** and each overlap represents **shared elements**. For the **Universal Set (U)**, the box around the circles represents everyone or everything under consideration.

The single must-remember formula is **inclusion–exclusion for two sets**:  
`n(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∩ B)`.

For three sets, extend it to:  
`n(A ∪ B ∪ C) = n(A) + n(B) + n(C) − n(A ∩ B) − n(B ∩ C) − n(A ∩ C) + n(A ∩ B ∩ C)`.

High-yield pointers for NAT-I:
- **"Only A"** = `n(A) − n(A ∩ B)`, never the full set A.
- **Disjoint sets** have `n(A ∩ B) = 0`, so the union is just the sum.
- **Complement**: `n(A′) = n(U) − n(A)` — use this when a question gives the "outside" count.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Reading the language of sets
Most NAT-I items hide a simple diagram inside a paragraph. Translate the verbs first:

- **"Like A and B" / "both A and B"** → **intersection** (A ∩ B).
- **"A or B (or both)"** → **union** (A ∪ B); use inclusion–exclusion.
- **"Only A"** → A minus its overlaps with every other named set.
- **"At least one of A, B, C"** → n(A ∪ B ∪ C).
- **"Exactly one"** → sum of individual-only regions.
- **"Neither A nor B"** → n(U) − n(A ∪ B) = n(A′ ∩ B′).

#### Two-circle problems
Start every two-circle problem by writing the **universal total** at the top. Then place numbers starting from the **innermost region** (the intersection), because it is double-counted when you simply add the circle totals.

Worked relationship: if a survey covers 100 people, 60 read English, 40 read Urdu, and 20 read both, the number who read **at least one** is `60 + 40 − 20 = 80`, and the number who read **neither** is `100 − 80 = 20`.

#### Three-circle problems
Three-circle diagrams have **7 distinct regions**: three "only" regions, three pairwise overlaps, one central triple overlap, and the outside region. The trap is the **central overlap** — inclusion–exclusion removes it three times, so you must **add it back once**.

#### Region shortcut formulas

| Region | Formula |
|---|---|
| Only A | `n(A) − n(A ∩ B) − n(A ∩ C) + n(A ∩ B ∩ C)` |
| Exactly one of A, B, C | `Σ n(A) − 2·Σ n(pairwise) + 3·n(A ∩ B ∩ C)` |
| A but not B | `n(A) − n(A ∩ B)` |
| At most one of A, B, C | `n(U) − [n(A ∩ B) + n(B ∩ C) + n(A ∩ C)] + 2·n(A ∩ B ∩ C)` |

#### Typical NAT-I patterns
NAT-I Analytical Reasoning contributes roughly 4% of the test and typically contains 1–3 Venn items. Expect: (a) find the number outside all sets, (b) find "exactly one" given overlapping totals, (c) compute how many liked all three items from given pair totals.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Mechanism behind inclusion–exclusion
The principle rests on counting each element exactly once. When you add `n(A) + n(B)`, elements in `A ∩ B` get counted twice, so you subtract one copy. With three sets, the pairwise subtraction removes the central element **three times**, so you compensate by adding it back once. Each set's elements therefore contribute exactly one to the final union count.

#### Edge cases
- **Disjoint or mutually exclusive sets**: `n(A ∩ B) = 0`, collapsing the formula to a simple sum. The diagram shows two non-touching circles inside U.
- **Subset relationships**: if `B ⊂ A`, then `n(A ∪ B) = n(A)` and `n(A ∩ B) = n(B)`. The diagram shows B fully inside A.
- **De Morgan's equivalents** worth memorising: `n((A ∪ B)′) = n(A′ ∩ B′)` and `n((A ∩ B)′) = n(A′ ∪ B′)`. These appear when a question asks for "neither" or "not both".
- **Negative or impossible answers** signal a misread universal set — always check whether the given total equals the sum of all seven regions plus the outside.

#### Connections to adjacent topics
Venn work overlaps directly with **set notation and truth tables**, and the "exactly one" pattern links to **probability** (sample-space partitioning). Solving ratio problems with Venn diagrams also transfers to **percentage and proportion** questions.

#### Common mistakes examiners exploit
- Adding circle totals without subtracting the intersection → **inflated union**.
- Forgetting to add back the triple overlap in three-set problems → **deflated union**.
- Treating "only A" as the full set A → overcounts shared members.
- Misreading "at least one" as "exactly one" → different region count.
- Ignoring the outside region when the universal total is given.

#### Worked micro-example
In a class of 50, 25 study Physics, 20 study Chemistry, 15 study Biology, 10 study P and C, 8 study C and B, 6 study P and B, and 4 study all three. Total studying **at least one** subject = `25 + 20 + 15 − 10 − 8 − 6 + 4 = 40`. Students studying **none** = `50 − 40 = 10`. Those studying **exactly one** = `25 − 10 − 6 + 4 + 20 − 10 − 8 + 4 + 15 − 8 − 6 + 4 = 20`.

#### Practice prompts
1. Of 200 employees, 120 know Excel, 90 know SQL, 60 know both. How many know **at least one**, and how many know **neither**?
2. Among 150 students, 70 play cricket, 60 play football, 50 play hockey. Pairwise overlaps: C∩F = 25, F∩H = 20, C∩H = 15, and all three = 10. Find how many play **exactly one** sport and how many play **none**.
