# Idioms and Phrases

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

An **idiom** is a fixed multi-word expression whose meaning cannot be reconstructed from its individual words, such as *spill the beans* (reveal a secret) or *kick the bucket* (to die). HAT-UG tests this topic inside the English Usage and Comprehension section at roughly **3% weight**, almost always as four-option MCQs.

- **What to expect:** identify the meaning of an underlined idiom, pick the idiom that replaces an underlined phrase, or choose the sentence where an idiom is used correctly.
- **Strategy:** substitute each candidate meaning into the sentence and check both **syntactic fit** (grammar) and **semantic fit** (sense + register).
- **High-yield traps:** literal reading, near-miss idioms (*beat around the bush* vs *beat the bush*), and over-strong meanings (*once in a blue moon* means *very rarely*, not *never*).

| Idiom | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Spill the beans | Reveal a secret |
| Hit the nail on the head | State something precisely |
| Once in a blue moon | Very rarely |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Definition and Scope

An **idiom** bundles two or more words into a single semantic unit whose figurative meaning is stored in the minds of native speakers. A **phrase** is broader: it may be literal (kick the ball) or idiomatic (kick the habit). The only reliable test is meaning opacity — if the whole means more than the parts, it is an idiom.

#### Categories Tested in HAT-UG

| Type | Feature | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pure idiom | Meaning is fully figurative | Bite the bullet |
| Semi-idiom | One word used figuratively | Lose one's head |
| Literal phrase | No figurative shift | Break the news gently |

#### Idiom vs Proverb vs Collocation

- **Idiom:** figurative, fixed, e.g., *burn the midnight oil*.
- **Proverb:** states a general truth, e.g., *A stitch in time saves nine*.
- **Collocation:** habitual pairing, not figurative, e.g., *make a decision*.

#### Standard Question Formats

1. "The meaning of the underlined idiom is..." — pick the closest paraphrase.
2. "Choose the idiom that best replaces the underlined phrase."
3. "In which sentence is the idiom used correctly?" — register and collocation matter.

#### Disambiguation Method

Read the stem, identify the **subject**, the **object**, and the **mood** (formal, casual, sarcastic). Plug each option into the sentence; the one that preserves grammar and tone is the answer. Synonyms and antonyms of fixed phrases are also asked, so memorise opposite pairs such as *at sixes and sevens* vs *in apple-pie order*.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Mechanism

Idioms behave as **non-compositional lexical units** in the mental lexicon, which is why literal decoding fails. Three edge cases appear repeatedly in HAT-UG:

- **Tense shifts:** *have a chip on one's shoulder* (present resentfulness) vs *carry a torch for someone* (ongoing unrequited feeling) — the verb inside the idiom is fixed and cannot be swapped freely.
- **Register mismatch:** *pull someone's leg* (informal joke) cannot substitute for *deceive* in a formal essay-style sentence without sounding awkward.
- **Polarity error:** *move heaven and earth* is *positive and extreme effort*, not a neutral description; *cry over spilt milk* is *wasteful regret*, not simple sadness.

#### Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Wrong pick | Correct pick |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reading literally | Kick the bucket = hit a pail | To die |
| Confusing near-miss idioms | Beat the bush | Beat around the bush (avoid the topic) |
| Over-strong meaning | Once in a blue moon = never | Very rarely |
| Mixing categories | Treating a proverb as an idiom | Proverbs state truths; idioms do not |

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics

Idioms sit beside **one-word substitution**, **synonyms and antonyms**, and **reading comprehension** in the HAT-UG English paper. Mastering idioms lifts cloze-test and vocabulary scores simultaneously.
