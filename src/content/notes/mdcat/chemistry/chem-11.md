# Transition Elements

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Transition elements are **d-block metals** (Groups 3–12) whose atoms or common ions carry a **partially filled d-subshell**. They show four signature behaviours: **variable oxidation states**, **coloured complex ions**, **catalytic activity**, and **interstitial compound** formation.

- Electronic configuration anomaly: **Cr is 3d⁵ 4s¹** (not 3d⁴ 4s²); **Cu is 3d¹⁰ 4s¹** (not 3d⁹ 4s²) — extra stability of half-filled and fully-filled d-sets.
- Spin-only magnetic moment: **μ = √[n(n+2)] BM**, where *n* = number of **unpaired electrons**.
- Colour in complexes arises from **d–d transitions**; if d is empty (Sc³⁺, Ti⁴⁺) or full (Zn²⁺, Cu⁺), the compound is **colourless**.

Exam pointer: MDCAT packs 1–2 MCQs testing Cr/Cu configuration, the magnetic-moment formula, or the origin of colour — usually in a "select the correct statement" format.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Electronic Configuration & the d-Block Definition
Across the first row (Sc → Zn), the 4s orbital fills before 3d, so a generic configuration is **[Ar] 3dˣ 4s²**. Two deliberate exceptions maximise exchange energy: **Cr = [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹** and **Cu = [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹**. When ionising, **4s electrons leave before 3d electrons** because the 4s orbital expands once 3d is populated.

#### Atomic Size, Density, and Melting Point
Poor shielding by 3d electrons means the effective nuclear charge rises across the series. **Atomic and ionic radii contract**, while **density and melting points peak in the middle** (W is highest melting transition metal at 3422 °C).

#### Variable Oxidation States
Because 3d and 4s energies are close, many oxidation states are accessible. For example, Mn shows **+2, +3, +4, +6, +7**, and Fe shows **+2, +3**. The +2 state stability across M²⁺ follows the **Irving–Williams order**: Mn²⁺ < Fe²⁺ < Co²⁺ < Ni²⁺ < Cu²⁺ > Zn²⁺.

#### Complex Ions and Shapes
A complex has a central metal ion bound by **ligands** through **coordinate (dative) bonds**.

| Coordination number | Common geometry | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Linear | [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ |
| 4 | Tetrahedral / Square planar | [CoCl₄]²⁻ / [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻ |
| 6 | Octahedral | [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ |

#### Crystal Field Theory and Colour
In an **octahedral field**, d-orbitals split into lower **t₂g** and higher **e_g** sets with separation **Δ_o**. **CFSE = (−0.4 n_t₂g + 0.6 n_e_g) Δ_o**. Electrons absorbing visible light jump t₂g → e_g; the transmitted complement gives the observed colour. **Δ_t ≈ 4/9 Δ_o** for tetrahedral fields (splitting inverted: *e* below *t₂*).

#### Exam Pattern
MDCAT (3% weight) tests this topic as **single-statement MCQs**: choose the correctly written configuration of Cr³⁺; identify the coloured species from a list; or compute μ for a given ion.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Catalysis Mechanism
Transition metals catalyse by offering **empty d-orbitals** for substrate adsorption and **variable oxidation states** to shuttle electrons. Heterogeneous case: Ni/H₂ for hydrogenation (H₂ binds to surface Ni atoms, weakening H–H). Homogeneous case: **Wilkinson's catalyst** RhCl(PPh₃)₃ cycles Rh between +1 and +3 during alkene hydrogenation.

#### Interstitial and Alloy Behaviour
Small non-metals (**H, C, N, B**) enter metal lattice voids to give **interstitial compounds** like TiC and Fe₃H — these retain metallic lustre and conductivity but become much harder. Transition metals also dissolve one another to form **alloys** (brass, bronze, stainless steel) whose properties differ sharply from pure constituents.

#### Worked Example — Magnetic Moment
Question: a Co²⁺ ion (high-spin, octahedral) has **3 unpaired electrons**. Find μ.

```
μ = √[n(n+2)] = √[3 × 5] = √15 ≈ 3.87 BM
```

A measured value closer to 4.7 BM means **orbital contribution** is non-negligible, so spin-only is an *approximation*, not identity.

#### Common Mistakes to Avoid
1. Writing **Cr as 3d⁴ 4s² or Cu as 3d⁹ 4s²** — both incorrect for ground states.
2. Treating **Zn as a transition metal** — Zn²⁺ is d¹⁰, so Zn fails the partial-fill test.
3. Claiming Cu⁺ is coloured in water — it is colourless; only Cu²⁺ (d⁹) gives the blue [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺.
4. Setting **Δ_t = Δ_o**; tetrahedral splitting is roughly 4/9 of octahedral.
5. Confusing which electrons leave first: **4s electrons ionise before 3d electrons**, despite 4s filling first.

#### Practice Prompts
1. Explain why [Ti(H₂O)₆]³⁺ is purple but [Sc(H₂O)₆]³⁺ is colourless, citing d-electron count.
2. Predict, with reasoning, the relative reducing power of the M²⁺/M couples for Mn, Fe, and Cu using E° values.
