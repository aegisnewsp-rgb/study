# States of Matter

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

States of matter in the PMDC MDCAT syllabus cover the physical behavior and molecular dynamics of solids, liquids, gases, and plasma. The macroscopic state of any chemical substance reflects the thermodynamic competition between thermal kinetic energy ($E_k \\propto T$), which drives particles apart, and intermolecular potential energy ($U_{\\text{attr}}$), which binds particles together. Gases possess negligible intermolecular forces relative to kinetic energy, expanding to fill any container volume. Liquids exhibit definite volume with fluid boundary adaptation. Solids maintain fixed spatial lattices with localized vibrational motion.

| Physical Characteristic | Solid State | Liquid State | Gaseous State | Plasma State |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Definite Volume & Shape** | Fixed volume and fixed geometric shape | Fixed volume; assumes container geometry | Variable volume and variable container geometry | Variable volume; expands to boundaries |
| **Intermolecular Forces** | Strongest ($U_{\\text{attr}} \\gg E_k$) | Intermediate ($U_{\\text{attr}} \\approx E_k$) | Negligible ($E_k \\gg U_{\\text{attr}}$) | Long-range Coulombic electrostatic interactions |
| **Compressibility Factor** | Incompressible ($dV/dP \\approx 0$) | Almost incompressible ($< 0.005\\% / \\text{atm}$) | Highly compressible ($V \\propto 1/P$) | Highly compressible; magnetically responsive |
| **Kinetic Motion Mode** | Vibrational about fixed lattice points | Translational, rotational, vibrational | Rapid, random, straight-line translation | High-energy ionized electrons and cations |
| **Diffusion Rate** | Extremely low ($10^{-15} \\text{ m}^2/\\text{s}$) | Moderate ($10^{-9} \\text{ m}^2/\\text{s}$) | High ($10^{-5} \\text{ m}^2/\\text{s}$) | Extremely rapid plasma drift |

#### 1. Kinetic Molecular Theory of Ideal Gases

The Kinetic Molecular Theory (KMT) proposed by Bernoulli, Clausius, Maxwell, and Boltzmann provides the micro-mechanical foundation for the macroscopic gas laws. 

1. **Volume of Gas Particles**: Gas consists of tiny discrete molecules. The actual physical volume occupied by gas particles is negligible compared to the total volume of the container.
2. **Intermolecular Forces**: Gas particles exert no attractive or repulsive electrostatic forces on one another.
3. **Continuous Motion**: Gas particles are in constant, random, straight-line motion until they collide with each other or the container walls.
4. **Elastic Collisions**: All collisions between gas molecules or with container boundaries are perfectly elastic; total kinetic energy and linear momentum are conserved.
5. **Kinetic Energy and Absolute Temperature**: The average translational kinetic energy of gas molecules is directly proportional to absolute temperature in Kelvin:
   $$\\bar{E}_k = \\frac{3}{2} k_B T = \\frac{3}{2} \\frac{R}{N_A} T$$
6. **Gas Pressure Origin**: Measured gas pressure arises from continuous momentum exchange during molecular collisions per unit surface area of the container walls:
   $$P = \\frac{1}{3} \\frac{m N \\bar{v}^2}{V}$$

#### 2. Classical Gas Laws Synthesis Matrix

The empirical gas laws describe two-variable state relationships while holding remaining thermodynamic state variables constant.

| Gas Law Name | Constant Variables | Mathematical Formulation | Graphic Curve Type | MDCAT Exam Question Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Boyle\x27s Law** | Temperature ($T$), Moles ($n$) | $P_1 V_1 = P_2 V_2$ or $P \\propto \\frac{1}{V}$ | Rectangular hyperbola (*Isotherm*); $P$ vs $1/V$ is linear | Calculating compressed syringe or diving bell volumes |
| **Charles\x27s Law** | Pressure ($P$), Moles ($n$) | $\\frac{V_1}{T_1} = \\frac{V_2}{T_2}$ or $V \\propto T$ | Straight line through absolute zero ($-273.15^\\circ\\text{C}$) (*Isobar*) | Thermal expansion; zero volume extrapolation at $0\\text{ K}$ |
| **Gay-Lussac\x27s Law** | Volume ($V$), Moles ($n$) | $\\frac{P_1}{T_1} = \\frac{P_2}{T_2}$ or $P \\propto T$ | Linear ray originating at origin (*Isochore*) | Autoclave or gas cylinder pressure surges upon heating |
| **Avogadro\x27s Law** | Pressure ($P$), Temp ($T$) | $\\frac{V_1}{n_1} = \\frac{V_2}{n_2}$ or $V \\propto n$ | Linear through origin | Molar volume at STP ($1\\text{ atm}, 273.15\\text{ K} \\rightarrow 22.414\\text{ L}$) |
| **Dalton\x27s Law** | Volume ($V$), Temp ($T$) | $P_{\\text{total}} = \\sum P_i = \\sum X_i P_{\\text{total}}$ | Linear additive summation | Gas collected over water: $P_{\\text{dry}} = P_{\\text{moist}} - P_{\\text{aq}}$ |
| **Graham\x27s Law** | Pressure ($P$), Temp ($T$) | $\\frac{r_1}{r_2} = \\sqrt{\\frac{M_2}{M_1}} = \\sqrt{\\frac{d_2}{d_1}} = \\frac{t_2}{t_1}$ | Inverse square root curve | Effusion velocity comparisons of light vs heavy gases |

#### 3. Dalton\x27s Law & Collection of Gases Over Water

When collecting an insoluble gas over water in a pneumatic trough, the collected vapour is saturated with water vapour (aqueous tension). Dalton\x27s Law governs the accurate dry gas calculation:
$$P_{\\text{total}} = P_{\\text{moist gas}} = P_{\\text{dry gas}} + P_{\\text{aqueous tension}}$$
$$P_{\\text{dry gas}} = P_{\\text{moist gas}} - P_{\\text{aqueous tension}}$$

The partial pressure of any component $i$ in a non-reacting gas mixture depends directly on its mole fraction $X_i$:
$$X_i = \\frac{n_i}{n_{\\text{total}}} \\implies P_i = X_i \\cdot P_{\\text{total}}$$

#### Real Gas Deviations and the Van der Waals Equation

Real gases deviate from ideal behavior because real molecules occupy finite physical volume and experience intermolecular attractive forces (London dispersion, dipole-dipole). These deviations become pronounced under **high pressure** (molecules forced close together) and **low temperature** (molecular thermal speed decreases, allowing intermolecular attractions to pull particles together).

The compressibility factor $Z$ measures ideality:
$$Z = \\frac{P V_m}{R T}$$
- For an ideal gas, $Z = 1$ under all conditions.
- When $Z < 1$, attractive forces dominate, reducing the pressure below the ideal value (observed in $\\text{CH}_4, \\text{CO}_2, \\text{NH}_3$ at intermediate pressures).
- When $Z > 1$, repulsive molecular volume effects dominate (observed in $\\text{H}_2, \\text{He}$ at all ordinary temperatures).

Johannes Diderik van der Waals modified the ideal gas equation by introducing two substance-specific constants:
$$\\left(P + \\frac{n^2 a}{V^2}\\right)(V - n b) = n R T$$

| Constant | Physical Meaning | Dimensions & SI Units | Influencing Molecular Properties |
|---|---|---|---|
| **$a$** (Cohesion parameter) | Measures the magnitude of attractive intermolecular forces between gas molecules | $\\text{atm}\\cdot\\text{L}^2\\cdot\\text{mol}^{-2}$ or $\\text{N}\\cdot\\text{m}^4\\cdot\\text{mol}^{-2}$ | Polarizability, dipole moment, molecular mass ($\\text{He} < \\text{H}_2 < \\text{CH}_4 < \\text{CO}_2 < \\text{NH}_3 < \\text{H}_2\\text{O}$) |
| **$b$** (Co-volume parameter) | Measures effective excluded volume occupied by molecules ($b = 4 \\cdot N_A \\cdot v_{\\text{molecule}}$) | $\\text{L}\\cdot\\text{mol}^{-1}$ or $\\text{m}^3\\cdot\\text{mol}^{-1}$ | Actual geometric diameter and size of the constituent molecules |

#### Critical Phenomena and Gas Liquefaction

Every gas possesses a characteristic **Critical Temperature ($T_c$)** above which it cannot be liquefied, regardless of the magnitude of pressure applied.

$$\\text{Critical Temperature: } T_c = \\frac{8 a}{27 R b}$$
$$\\text{Critical Pressure: } P_c = \\frac{a}{27 b^2}$$
$$\\text{Critical Volume: } V_c = 3 b$$
$$\\text{Critical Compressibility: } Z_c = \\frac{P_c V_c}{R T_c} = \\frac{3}{8} = 0.375$$

Gases with high critical temperatures ($\\text{NH}_3: 132.4^\\circ\\text{C}, \\text{CO}_2: 31.1^\\circ\\text{C}$) have strong intermolecular attractions (large $a$) and liquefy easily under pressure at room temperature. Permanent gases with low critical temperatures ($\\text{O}_2: -118.8^\\circ\\text{C}, \\text{N}_2: -147^\\circ\\text{C}, \\text{H}_2: -239.9^\\circ\\text{C}, \\text{He}: -267.9^\\circ\\text{C}$) require extensive pre-cooling via the Joule-Thomson expansion (Linde\x27s process) before compression can induce liquefaction.

#### Worked MDCAT Numerical Solutions

**Example 1: Gas Collected Over Water with Aqueous Tension**  
*Problem:* A sample of nitrogen gas is collected over water at $27^\\circ\\text{C}$ and an atmospheric pressure of $785\\text{ torr}$. The collected gas volume is $450\\text{ mL}$. Calculate the dry gas volume at STP ($0^\\circ\\text{C}, 760\\text{ torr}$). The aqueous tension of water at $27^\\circ\\text{C}$ is $25\\text{ torr}$.

*Step-by-Step Solution:*
1. Calculate dry nitrogen pressure:
   $$P_{\\text{dry}} = P_{\\text{total}} - P_{\\text{aqueous}} = 785\\text{ torr} - 25\\text{ torr} = 760\\text{ torr}$$
2. Convert temperatures to Kelvin:
   $$T_1 = 27 + 273.15 = 300.15\\text{ K}$$
   $$T_2 = 0 + 273.15 = 273.15\\text{ K}$$
3. Apply the combined gas equation:
   $$\\frac{P_1 V_1}{T_1} = \\frac{P_2 V_2}{T_2} \\implies V_2 = V_1 \\cdot \\left(\\frac{P_1}{P_2}\\right) \\cdot \\left(\\frac{T_2}{T_1}\\right)$$
4. Substitute values:
   $$V_2 = 450\\text{ mL} \\cdot \\left(\\frac{760\\text{ torr}}{760\\text{ torr}}\\right) \\cdot \\left(\\frac{273.15\\text{ K}}{300.15\\text{ K}}\\right) = 450 \\cdot 0.9100 = 409.5\\text{ mL}$$
*Final Answer:* The dry nitrogen volume at STP is **$409.5\\text{ mL}$**.

**Example 2: Graham\x27s Law Relative Effusion Time**  
*Problem:* It takes $20.0\\text{ seconds}$ for $100\\text{ mL}$ of unknown gas $X$ to effuse through a porous plug. Under identical pressure and temperature conditions, $100\\text{ mL}$ of pure oxygen gas ($\\text{O}_2, M = 32.0\\text{ g/mol}$) effuses in $14.14\\text{ seconds}$. Calculate the molar mass of gas $X$.

*Step-by-Step Solution:*
1. State Graham\x27s effusion time relationship for identical volumes:
   $$\\frac{t_X}{t_{\\text{O}_2}} = \\sqrt{\\frac{M_X}{M_{\\text{O}_2}}}$$
2. Substitute given effusion times:
   $$\\frac{20.0\\text{ s}}{14.14\\text{ s}} = 1.4144$$
3. Square both sides to solve for molar mass ratio:
   $$(1.4144)^2 = \\frac{M_X}{32.0} \\implies 2.000 = \\frac{M_X}{32.0}$$
4. Calculate $M_X$:
   $$M_X = 2.000 \\times 32.0 = 64.0\\text{ g/mol}$$
*Final Answer:* The molar mass of gas $X$ is **$64.0\\text{ g/mol}$** (consistent with sulfur dioxide, $\\text{SO}_2$).

#### Exam Traps and Pitfalls

- **Temperature Units**: Never substitute Celsius in gas equations. A temperature shift from $10^\\circ\\text{C}$ to $20^\\circ\\text{C}$ is not a doubling of volume ($283.15\\text{ K}$ to $293.15\\text{ K}$ represents only a $3.5\\%$ volume increase).
- **Universal Gas Constant ($R$) Consistency**:
  - $R = 0.0821\\text{ L}\\cdot\\text{atm}\\cdot\\text{K}^{-1}\\cdot\\text{mol}^{-1}$ requires volume in liters and pressure in atmospheres.
  - $R = 8.314\\text{ J}\\cdot\\text{K}^{-1}\\cdot\\text{mol}^{-1} = 8.314\\text{ N}\\cdot\\text{m}\\cdot\\text{K}^{-1}\\cdot\\text{mol}^{-1}$ requires volume in $\\text{m}^3$ and pressure in pascals ($\\text{N/m}^2$).
  - $R = 62.4\\text{ L}\\cdot\\text{torr}\\cdot\\text{K}^{-1}\\cdot\\text{mol}^{-1}$ requires volume in liters and pressure in millimeters of mercury.
- **Effusion Rate vs Time**: Effusion rate $r$ is inversely proportional to effusion time $t$ ($r = V/t$). Therefore, $\\frac{r_1}{r_2} = \\frac{t_2}{t_1}$, not $t_1/t_2$.
