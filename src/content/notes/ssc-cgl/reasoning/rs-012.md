# Statement Conclusion

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Statement–Conclusion** items present one or two factual or situational premises; your task is to mark each given conclusion as *definitely follows*, *probably follows*, or *does not follow*, using only the information inside the premises.

- **Premise** = the given factual/situational statement(s), assumed true for reasoning.
- **Conclusion** = an inference drawn by deduction from the premise(s).
- **Definitely follows** → true in 100% of cases derivable from the premise.
- **Probably follows** → true in most, but not necessarily all, cases.
- **Does not follow** → introduces new info, comparison, cause, or advice not supported by the premise.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"S-P-I-C-E"** — **S**tatement (read carefully), **P**articular vs universal (scope), **I**nternal reasoning only (ignore real-world truth), **C**omparison/Cause/Advice = reject, **E**xception-free check (one counter-case kills "definite").

| Verdict | Rule of Thumb | SSC CGL Option |
|---|---|---|
| Definitely follows | No possible counter-example exists | "Conclusion I and II follow" |
| Probably follows | Holds for most, exceptions allowed | "Only conclusion II follows" |
| Does not follow | New info, comparison, cause, or advice added | "Neither I nor II follows" |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Deduction Framework

A **deductive conclusion** is a logically necessary inference: if the premise is accepted, the conclusion must also be accepted. SSC CGL tests this through three verdict categories — definite, probable, or invalid — applied to one or two short premises.

The premises are **assumed true** even when factually wrong in reality (e.g., "All cats fly" must be treated as true for that question). Your job is internal consistency, not fact-checking.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Feature | Definitely Follows | Probably Follows | Does Not Follow |
|---|---|---|---|
| Truth requirement | Must hold in every possible case | Holds in most cases, exceptions possible | Holds in few/no cases, or introduces new data |
| Scope of premise | Universal ("all", "no", "none") or strictly factual | Particular ("some", "many", "in many papers") or general factual | Any premise type, but conclusion jumps scope |
| Typical content | Restatement, subset inference, logical equivalent | Likely behavioural/attitudinal inference | Comparisons ("better", "more"), causes, advice, time-sequence |
| SSC CGL frequency | Most tested verdict | Frequently the correct answer when scope is particular | Common distractor |

#### Step-by-Step Method

1. **Read the premise twice**, marking every universal quantifier (all, every, no, none) and particular quantifier (some, few, many, in many papers).
2. **Read each conclusion** and tag it: *restatement / subset / equivalent* → likely definite; *comparative / causal / advisory* → likely reject.
3. **Test for counter-examples** — if even one realistic exception breaks the conclusion, it cannot be "definite".
4. **Apply scope rules** — from "some" you cannot infer "all"; from "part" you can infer "whole contains the part".
5. **Match the verdict** to the SSC CGL answer option (I, II, both, either, neither).

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

#### Question:
*Statements:* "All doctors are graduates. Some graduates are teachers."
*Conclusions:*
- **I.** Some teachers are doctors.
- **II.** Some teachers are graduates.

Which conclusions follow?

#### Solution:
- From premise 1: doctors ⊂ graduates (every doctor is a graduate).
- From premise 2: teachers ∩ graduates ≠ ∅ (some teachers share the graduate set).
- The graduates who are teachers can or can not include any doctor — **Conclusion I is not certain**.
- Conclusion II restates premise 2 directly — it is **definitely true**.

**Answer:** Only **Conclusion II** follows.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students commonly mark **both I and II** as following because premise 1 links doctors to graduates. But premise 2 only guarantees *some* graduates are teachers, not that the overlapping set includes doctors. One counter-example (a teacher who is a graduate but not a doctor) breaks Conclusion I.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Advanced Traps and Edge Cases

| Trap | Mechanism | How to Detect |
|---|---|---|
| Particular-to-universal | Conclusion jumps "some" to "all/every" | Look for universal quantifier in conclusion absent in premise |
| Reverse scope | Conclusion narrows whole to part incorrectly | Subset inference must be one-way (part → whole only) |
| Comparative injection | Conclusion adds "better/worse/more/less" | Check whether premise contains any explicit or implicit comparison |
| Cause-effect smuggling | Conclusion uses "because", "therefore", "leads to" | Premise must contain the causal link explicitly |
| Advisory drift | Conclusion becomes a recommendation or moral | Look for imperative verbs ("should", "must do") |
| Real-world bias | Conclusion "feels true" from outside knowledge | Strip premise of real-world context, reason internally only |

#### Edge Cases and Boundary Conditions

A **probable conclusion** becomes the intended correct answer when the premise uses **particular quantifiers** (some, few, many, in many papers) or **general factual statements** about human behaviour, because exceptions are admissible. For example, from *"Most employees prefer flexible hours"*, you can validly conclude *"Many employees like flexible hours"* as a **probable** inference.

When two conclusions are individually probable but **cannot both be true simultaneously**, SSC CGL in many papers offers an *"Either I or II follows"* option. Recognise this when both conclusions reference **the same subject** with **complementary predicates** (e.g., "X is Y" and "X is not Y").

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics

- **Assumptions** differ from conclusions: an **assumption** is hidden and *necessary* for the premise to hold; a **conclusion** is *directly stated* or *logically drawn* from the premise.
- **Statement–Course of Action** items extend conclusions with advice; in pure Statement–Conclusion, any advice-type option must be rejected.
- **Syllogism** shares the same quantifier rules but tests multi-premise chains; mastering scope logic here directly speeds up syllogism solving.
- **Inference** vs **Conclusion**: inference is the mental act; conclusion is the output. SSC CGL phrasing uses "conclusion" almost exclusively.

#### Exam Strategy for SSC CGL

The Reasoning section carries **~4% weight** with roughly **3–5 Statement–Conclusion items** per shift in Tier-I. Budget **≤45 seconds per item**. First eliminate conclusions containing comparisons, causes, or advice — these are instant rejects. Then check the remaining candidates against the universal/particular quantifier in the premise.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. *Premise:* "All metals are conductors. Some conductors are brittle." Test whether *"Some brittle substances are metals"* is a definite, probable, or invalid conclusion.
2. *Premise:* "No politician is corrupt. All judges are honest." Decide which of *"Some honest people are judges"* and *"All politicians are honest"* follow, and identify whether an "Either–Or" option applies.
