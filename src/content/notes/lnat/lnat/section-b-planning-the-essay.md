# Section B — Planning Under 40 Minutes — LNAT Notes

Section B gives you 40 minutes and a choice of three essay questions. The LNAT Consortium's official guidance recommends a maximum of 750 words, ideally about 500 to 600, so the essay is a short argument under a tight clock rather than a long survey. The first three or four minutes should be spent choosing the question you can argue best rather than the one you know most about, because the section is marked on the ability to argue economically and to come to a conclusion. A two-line plan — position plus two or three reasons — fits on the front of the answer booklet and decides whether the rest of the 40 minutes produces an essay or a survey. Students who skip the plan usually produce a piece that lists considerations on both sides and never commits, which is the failure the section is designed to detect.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### The 40-minute time budget

- **3–4 minutes.** Read all three questions. Pick the one you can argue best. Write the two-line plan.
- **3 minutes.** Open with a paragraph that states your position in plain language. Do not warm up; do not paraphrase the question.
- **25–28 minutes.** Body paragraphs. Two or three reasons that carry the position. One short paragraph on the obvious objection.
- **2–3 minutes.** Conclusion that answers the exact question. No new arguments.
- **2–3 minutes.** Reread the essay against the question. Fix the one paragraph that has drifted off the question.

The total is 40 minutes because the Consortium states that is the time given. The Consortium's official guidance is a recommended maximum of 750 words, ideally about 500 to 600. Working to that word range is what lets the reread fit.

#### The two-line plan

The plan lives on two lines and answers two questions:

- **Line 1.** *My position is…* (one sentence, plain language, no hedge)
- **Line 2.** *The two or three reasons I will use are…* (a list, not paragraphs)

If you cannot fill line 2 in 60 seconds, you have picked the wrong question. Choose again.

#### The question you can argue best vs the question you know most about

The two are usually different, and the difference is the whole point of the planning step. The question you know most about may be the one where the field is huge — you know a lot because the topic is large. The question you can argue best is the one where you can take a clear position and defend it with two or three reasons inside the word range. When in doubt, pick the one with the clearer either/or shape.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Reading the three questions

The official practice test shows the kind of variation you will see. Some questions are narrowly framed ("To what extent should X be regulated?"). Others are open ("Critically evaluate the claim that…"). The narrow ones usually have a clearer either/or shape and are easier to plan; the open ones usually give you more room but reward a stronger position more than a longer survey.

A useful triage when you turn to Section B:

1. **Read all three.** Do not start writing on the first question that looks familiar. Sixty seconds of reading now saves ten minutes of writing into the wrong essay.
2. **Mark the one with the clearest either/or shape.** A question that can be answered with a position in one sentence is the one you can argue best.
3. **Mark the one with the most obvious objections.** Section B rewards dealing with the obvious objection in one paragraph. If a question has no obvious objection, you will struggle to demonstrate the skill the section is marked on.
4. **Pick the one that has both.** That is the question you can argue best.

#### Worked example: choosing between three past-question styles

Three representative questions from past LNAT papers (paraphrased here for practice; the originals are on the official practice page at lnat.ac.uk):

- **Q-A.** "To what extent should judges be allowed to make new law?"
- **Q-B.** "Critically evaluate the claim that human rights are universal."
- **Q-C.** "Is the use of anonymous witnesses in criminal trials justifiable?"

Triage:

| Question | Either/or shape | Obvious objection | Best-arguable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q-A | Yes — extent is a position on a scale | Yes — without judge-made law, the common law would not evolve | Yes |
| Q-B | No — universality is a softer claim with many positions | Yes — cultural relativism, but the objection is so large it takes more than one paragraph to handle | Harder |
| Q-C | Yes — justifiable or not is a clean position | Yes — anonymous evidence risks wrongful conviction | Yes |

Q-A and Q-C are both arguable. Choose between them by asking: which has two or three reasons you can write down in 60 seconds? For many students the answer is Q-C — the case for anonymous witnesses (witness protection, prosecution of organised crime) and the case against (wrongful conviction, confrontational right) both fit inside 500 to 600 words. Q-A's reasons are good but the objection (the common law must evolve) takes longer than one paragraph to handle. Choose the one where the obvious objection is small enough to deal with in a single paragraph.

#### The two-line plan on paper

Once you have picked the question, write the plan on the front of the answer booklet. It should look like one of these:

> **Plan for Q-C.** Position: anonymous witnesses are justifiable only with strong judicial safeguards. Reasons: (1) witness intimidation is a real risk to prosecutions; (2) anonymous evidence has produced wrongful convictions when relaxed; (3) safeguards (corroboration requirements, cross-examination limits) can reduce the wrongful-conviction risk without abolishing the practice. Conclusion: justifiable, conditionally.

> **Plan for Q-A.** Position: judges should be allowed to make new law, but the power must be openly named. Reasons: (1) the common law evolves by judge-made principle; (2) the alternative — frozen statute law — produces injustice in new cases; (3) judicial transparency about the law-making function improves democratic accountability. Conclusion: yes, and the power should be named.

Each plan is two lines (a sentence and a list). It is not a draft essay. It is the spine you write the essay around.

#### The 500–600 word range and what fits inside it

The Consortium's recommended maximum is 750 words, ideally about 500 to 600. A 500–600 word essay at 40 minutes works out to roughly 13–15 words per minute, which is achievable without sounding rushed. The shape inside that range:

- **Opening paragraph: ~80–100 words.** State your position. Name the question. Do not paraphrase the question — that wastes the opening.
- **Two or three body paragraphs: ~120–150 words each.** Each paragraph carries one reason. Each reason is a claim, a piece of support, and a sentence on why the alternative does not hold.
- **Objection paragraph: ~80–100 words.** The strongest objection, named and granted. Then the limit of the objection — what it does not defeat. Then back to the position.
- **Conclusion: ~50–80 words.** Answer the exact question. Do not introduce a new argument. Do not restate the introduction.

The total is 550–650 words. Inside the recommended range.

#### What to do in the last five minutes

The reread budget is the section's quietest advantage. Most students spend the last five minutes writing one more sentence that does not need to exist. The right move is to reread the essay against the exact question and check three things:

1. **Does every paragraph answer the question?** A paragraph that drifts off the question is the most common mark-loss. Two minutes to cut or rewrite that paragraph is worth more than another sentence elsewhere.
2. **Is the position stated in plain language in the opening?** If the opening hedges ("there are arguments on both sides"), the essay has failed the section's stated requirement to come to a conclusion. Rewrite the opening in 60 seconds.
3. **Does the conclusion answer the exact question?** A conclusion that summarises the body is not a conclusion. Rewrite it in 60 seconds to state the position one more time in light of the reasons.

Total reread time: two to three minutes. Use the rest as buffer.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Why planning matters more than fluency

The LNAT Consortium states the essay is marked on the ability to argue economically and to come to a conclusion. A fluent writer who has not planned will produce a piece that reads well but drifts — paragraphs that survey the field, an opening that hedges, a conclusion that summarises. The marker can see the drift regardless of how well the sentences are written. A planned essay with rough sentences will outscore a fluent drift, because every paragraph does work.

The lesson is not "be less fluent". The lesson is: fluency without a plan is wasted. Spend three or four minutes planning; spend the next 30 writing; spend the last three rereading. That order produces essays that score.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- **"Planning wastes time I could spend writing."** Three or four minutes of planning saves ten minutes of writing into the wrong essay. The plan is the highest-yield four minutes in the section.
- **"I should pick the question I know most about."** You should pick the question you can argue best. The two are different, and the section is marked on the second.
- **"A balanced essay is safer than a committed one."** The section is marked on the ability to come to a conclusion. A balanced essay that never commits is the failure the section is designed to detect. Commit.
- **"Longer essays score better."** The Consortium's recommended maximum is 750 words, ideally about 500 to 600. A shorter essay with a clear spine scores better than a longer one that hedges.
- **"I should deal with every objection equally."** Deal with the obvious objection in one paragraph. Other objections can be acknowledged in passing or set aside. The marker is looking for argument, not coverage.
- **"The conclusion should introduce one more point."** The conclusion should answer the exact question. New arguments belong in the body.

#### Practice routine that builds the planning habit

The most efficient Section B training is to plan, not to write. Do not write full essays every session. Plan three different past questions in fifteen minutes each — choosing the one you could argue best in thirty seconds, then writing the two-line plan and a one-sentence outline of each body paragraph. The point of planning under time pressure is to compress the planning step until it fits in three or four minutes on the day.

Then, once a week, write one full essay and reread it against the exact question. The reread is where the lessons live. A reread that finds one paragraph drifting off the question teaches more than another full essay written from scratch.
