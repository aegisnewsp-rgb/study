# Critical Study of Literature (Year 11) — HSC English Advanced (NESA 2024) Notes

Critical Study of Literature is a Year 11 module in the HSC English Advanced syllabus. Students engage in close study of a Shakespearean drama and additional literary texts. The module builds the close-reading and analytical skills that the Year 12 Critical Study of Literature module and the HSC examination depend on.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Module focus.** Close study of literary works, including a Shakespearean drama, and the development of critical response skills.
- **Texts.** Schools select at least one Shakespearean drama and additional literary texts (typically poetry, prose or further drama). There are no prescribed texts for Year 11.
- **Outcomes.** Students demonstrate understanding of how composers use language forms and features to construct meaning; analyse the ways textual features and form contribute to meaning; compose critical responses.
- **Assessment.** School-based assessment tasks in textual analysis and composition.

#### Examiner traps

- Treating the Shakespearean drama as a stand-alone text — NESA's expectation is that students also read the play in performance (film, theatre or audio recording).
- Confusing summary with analysis — Year 11 students often paraphrase the plot rather than analyse the language choices.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Shakespearean drama

Shakespeare wrote in iambic pentameter (10 syllables per line, alternating unstressed/stressed). His plays are organised in acts and scenes, with prose for common characters and verse for noble characters. Soliloquies give access to a character's inner thoughts.

Common Shakespearean features: blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter); prose (for common characters or for emphasis); shared lines (where two characters complete a single line); rhetorical devices (metaphor, simile, personification, antithesis, dramatic irony).

#### Analytical framework for literary texts

When analysing a literary text, structure the response as follows:

1. **Thesis.** A clear claim about the text's meaning or the composer's purpose.
2. **Topic sentence.** A claim about a specific aspect (character, theme, language feature).
3. **Evidence.** A quote from the text (with act/scene/line reference for Shakespeare).
4. **Analysis.** An explanation of HOW the evidence supports the topic sentence — language features, imagery, sound, structure.
5. **Link back to thesis.**

#### Critical vocabulary

Imagery = sensory language (visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory, gustatory). Symbol = an object or image that stands for a larger idea. Motif = a recurring image or idea. Tone = the narrator's or speaker's attitude. Voice = the persona through whom the text speaks. Irony = the gap between what is said and what is meant.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Why Critical Study of Literature (Year 11) matters

- **It teaches close reading.** The HSC examination requires students to identify language features and explain their effect in 5-mark analytical questions. Year 11 is where this habit is built.
- **It prepares for the HSC Critical Study of Literature module.** Year 12 Module B requires the same close-reading skills at a higher level.
- **It introduces Shakespeare.** Many Year 12 prescribed texts include Shakespeare; familiarity built here reduces Year 12 load.

#### Year 11 planning pattern

- **Term 1.** Read the Shakespearean play alongside watching at least one production. Introduce the analytical framework.
- **Term 2.** Read additional literary texts (poetry, prose). Build the language features vocabulary.
- **Term 3.** Practice timed analytical responses (45 minutes per response).
- **Term 4.** Revision and assessment.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Shakespeare is too hard." With practice the syntax becomes readable. Read aloud, watch the play, and refer to a modern translation when stuck.
- "Analysis is about giving my opinion." Analysis is about explaining what the composer has done and why. Personal opinion may appear briefly in a conclusion but the bulk is textual evidence and explanation.
- "A theme is what the text is about." A theme is what the text says about a topic. "Hamlet is about a prince" is a topic; "revenge corrupts the avenger" is a theme.

#### Specification reference

This module is school-assessed in Year 11. The skills, not the texts, carry forward. Confirm the live module descriptor and any NESA updates on curriculum.nsw.edu.au for the current specification.
