# Finding the Error in a Proof

The official TMUA content specification gives Paper 2 a Section 2 with three named families: Arg1 to Arg4 on the logic of arguments, Prf1 to Prf5 on mathematical proof, and Err1 to Err2 on identifying errors in purported proofs. This page is the Err family, and it is the most learnable part of the entire TMUA syllabus because the skill is mechanical: walk the argument line by line and find the first line that does not follow.

Err is also the family most candidates skip. It looks like proof, so it feels like the hard part of Paper 2, and it gets left alone in favour of drilling the algebra.

## Find the first error, not the last

This is the whole technique. Once a proof contains an invalid step, everything after it is unsupported — including steps that happen to be true. So the question "where does this go wrong?" has exactly one answer, and it is the earliest invalid line, not the most obviously wrong one near the end.

Work forward, one line at a time, and at each line ask a single question: does this follow from what has already been established? If yes, move on. If no, stop — that is the answer.

The discipline matters more than it sounds. Candidates habitually read the whole thing, form a view about which step "looks fishy", and then choose an answer that is a real error but not the first one. The option that matters is the earliest.

## The invalid moves that actually appear

These recur, because there are only so many ways to be wrong in elementary proof.

Dividing by something that can be zero. The proof assumes $x \ne 0$ without establishing it, divides, and the conclusion fails at $x = 0$. Always check what you divided by and whether its non-zero-ness has been shown.

Assuming the conclusion. A proof of $P$ that begins "assume $P$" and derives $P$ is circular. It is the most common invalid move in the family and it is easy to miss when the intermediate steps look sophisticated.

Reversing an implication. From "$A$ implies $B$" you may conclude "not $B$ implies not $A$" — that is the contrapositive and it is valid. You may not conclude "$B$ implies $A$"; that is the converse, and it is the single most common error in the whole of elementary logic.

Concluding a universal from an example. Showing that something holds for one value of $n$ does not show it for all $n$. This is the standard error in an induction proof that checks only the base case.

Negating incorrectly. Not of $P$ and $Q$ is not of $P$ or $Q$. Double negatives about quantifiers are the classic trap: "not all" becomes "some", not "none".

Excluding a root and then forgetting it. Solving $x^2 = 4$ by dividing by $x - 2$ silently discards $x = 2$, and the item will offer "one solution" as the option that is wrong.

Taking a square root of both sides without the sign. From $x^2 = y^2$ you get $x = y$ or $x = -y$, and taking only one is the error.

## The method, written out

Read once for the shape: what is being claimed, and by what route — induction, contradiction, direct argument, or a computation with several algebraic steps? Then walk again, line by line, marking each line OK or not. The first line marked not is your answer, whatever later lines look like.

If the claim itself is false, then the error can be nowhere in the middle — it is in an unjustified starting assumption, and one of the earlier lines is where it happened. Test the claim on a simple case if you can: if the claim is about every integer, check $n = 1$ and $n = 2$.

## Common mistakes

Choosing the error that is easiest to notice rather than the earliest one. Ease of spotting and position in the argument are unrelated.

Marking a step invalid because it "looks wrong" rather than because it does not follow. Many valid steps look strange until you write the justification.

Missing the error when the claim itself is false. If no individual step fails, suspect the starting assumptions.

Confusing a typo with an invalid step. A wrong result that still follows is not an error in the proof; check whether the logic holds regardless of the arithmetic.

Stopping the walk once you have found an error and then checking whether later lines are also wrong. Some options list several errors and ask which is the *first*. Only one answer can be.

Assuming the proof is valid because the conclusion is true. A true conclusion can be reached by invalid means, and that is exactly what this family tests.
