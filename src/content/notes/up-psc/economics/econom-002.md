# Demand and Supply

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Demand** is the quantity of a good consumers are willing and *able* to buy at each possible price during a period, other factors held constant. **Supply** is the mirror idea — the quantity producers are willing and *able* to sell at each possible price. **Equilibrium** is the single price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.

- **Law of Demand:** Inverse relation between price (P, ₹) and quantity demanded (Qd, units); demand curve slopes downward.
- **Law of Supply:** Direct relation between P and quantity supplied (Qs, units); supply curve slopes upward.
- **Linear forms:** Qd = a − bP and Qs = c + dP, where a, c are intercepts (units) and b, d are slope coefficients (units per ₹).
- **Equilibrium price:** Pe = (a − c) / (b + d) in ₹; substitute back to get equilibrium quantity Qe (units).
- **Elasticity of demand:** Ed = (%ΔQd) / (%ΔP) — dimensionless; |Ed| > 1 elastic, < 1 inelastic, = 1 unit elastic.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"DIPS-SC"** for the six demand determinants — **D**emographics/buyers, **I**ncome, **P**rice of related goods, **S**elf-price (movement only), **C**onsumer tastes & expectations. For supply, think **"PET-REN"** — **P**rice of inputs, **E**xpectations, **T**echnology, **R**elated goods' prices, **E**xcise/taxes-subsidies, **N**umber of sellers.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Definitions and the Demand–Supply Identity
"Demand" and "supply" are *schedules*, not single numbers. A demand schedule lists the quantity buyers will purchase at every price; the demand **curve** is its graphical plot with P on the vertical axis and Q on the horizontal. The same holds for supply. **Market equilibrium** is the intersection where Qd = Qs; at any price above Pe a surplus (excess supply) pushes price down, and below Pe a shortage (excess demand) pushes it up — this is the Walrasian adjustment process.

#### Linear Functions and Equilibrium Algebra
A standard UPPSC problem supplies two linear equations. Set Qd = Qs and solve for P:

| Symbol | Meaning | SI / Standard Unit | Typical Sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| Qd | Quantity demanded | units (or kg, litres) | — |
| Qs | Quantity supplied | units | — |
| P | Price of the good | ₹ per unit | — |
| a | Autonomous demand intercept | units | positive |
| b | Slope of demand w.r.t. P | units per ₹ | positive |
| c | Autonomous supply intercept | units | positive |
| d | Slope of supply w.r.t. P | units per ₹ | positive |

- **Equilibrium price (₹):** Pe = (a − c) / (b + d), provided b + d ≠ 0.
- **Equilibrium quantity (units):** Qe = a − bPe = c + dPe.
- **Verification step:** plug Pe back into both equations; the two Qs must match exactly — examiners often test this.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix — Easy-to-Confuse Pairs

| Concept A | Concept B | Key Distinction | Exam Cue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Change in **quantity demanded** | Change in **demand** | Movement *along* the curve from own-price change | Shift of entire curve |
| Movement along supply curve | Shift of supply curve | Caused by own-price change only | Caused by non-price factors |
| Price elasticity of demand | Slope of demand curve | Dimensionless ratio; depends on P/Q | Has units units/₹; constant on a linear curve |
| Normal good | Inferior good | Demand rises with income | Demand falls as income rises |
| Substitute goods | Complementary goods | Cross-elasticity > 0 | Cross-elasticity < 0 |
| Excess demand (shortage) | Excess supply (surplus) | Qd > Qs at given P → price rises | Qs > Qd at given P → price falls |
| Consumer surplus | Producer surplus | Area below demand, above Pe | Area above supply, below Pe |

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** The demand and supply functions for wheat in UP are Qd = 500 − 4P and Qs = 100 + 2P, where P is in ₹ per quintal and Q is in quintals per day. Find (i) the equilibrium price and quantity, (ii) the quantity exchanged if the government fixes a ceiling price of ₹ 40, and (iii) the elasticity of demand at equilibrium.

#### Solution:
1. **Equilibrium:** Set Qd = Qs → 500 − 4P = 100 + 2P → 400 = 6P → **Pe = ₹ 66.67 per quintal**.
2. **Equilibrium quantity:** Qe = 100 + 2(66.67) = **233.33 quintals/day**. Check: 500 − 4(66.67) = 500 − 266.67 = 233.33 ✓.
3. **At price ceiling P = ₹ 40:** Qd = 500 − 4(40) = 340 quintals; Qs = 100 + 2(40) = 180 quintals. Shortage = 340 − 180 = **160 quintals/day** (not 233 — read carefully).
4. **Point elasticity at equilibrium:** Ed = (dQd/dP) × (P/Q) = (−4) × (66.67 / 233.33) = **−1.143**. |Ed| > 1, so demand is elastic at this point.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Many students compute Qe by substituting Pe into only one equation and forget the cross-check; if the two answers differ, you've made an arithmetic slip. Also, a *ceiling* price below Pe always creates a shortage, never a surplus — students often invert this.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Shifts vs Movements — the Distinction Examiners Test
A change in the good's **own price** produces a **movement along** the existing curve, leaving the curve itself untouched. A change in **any non-price determinant** shifts the entire curve. For demand, rightward (outward) shifts come from higher income (normal goods), higher price of a substitute, lower price of a complement, favourable tastes, higher expected future price, or more buyers. Leftward shifts mirror these. Supply shifts right with cheaper inputs, better technology, subsidies, higher expected future price, or more sellers; taxes and cost-push shocks shift it left.

| Non-price factor | Demand shift | Supply shift |
|---|---|---|
| Rise in consumer income (normal good) | Right | No direct shift |
| Rise in input price | No direct shift | Left |
| Subsidy to producers | No direct shift | Right |
| Higher price of a substitute | Right | No direct shift |
| Favourable taste change | Right | No direct shift |
| Technological improvement | No direct shift | Right |

#### Tax Incidence, Elasticity, and Time Horizon
A specific (per-unit) tax of t (₹) on the good shifts the supply curve vertically upward by exactly t. The new equilibrium price rises by ΔP = t × Es / (Es − |Ed|), assuming linear curves. Three takeaways UPPSC expects:

1. The more **inelastic** side of the market bears the larger tax burden — labour (inelastic supply) vs luxury goods (elastic demand).
2. **Long-run supply elasticity exceeds short-run elasticity** because firms can adjust plant size, enter, or exit; demand is also more elastic in the long run as consumers find substitutes.
3. **Elasticity is not slope.** On a straight-line demand curve, |Ed| = ∞ at the price intercept and 0 at the quantity intercept — examiners test this with "is elasticity constant on a linear demand curve?" (Answer: *no*).

#### Exceptions, Surplus Geometry, and Advanced Practice
**Veblen goods** and **Giffen goods** violate the law of demand and give upward-sloping demand curves — rare in UPPSC, but a frequent "which is the exception?" MCQ. **Consumer surplus** equals the triangular area ½ × (Pmax − Pe) × Qe where Pmax is the choke price (a/b); **producer surplus** equals ½ × (Pe − Pmin) × Qe where Pmin = −c/d.

#### Advanced practice prompts:
1. Derive the deadweight loss of a per-unit tax of ₹ 10 on the wheat market above (Qd = 500 − 4P, Qs = 100 + 2P) and identify which side bears more burden.
2. Suppose income rises by 20 % and quantity demanded rises by 10 % at constant prices. Compute income elasticity and classify the good — would a fall in income shift the demand curve left or right?
