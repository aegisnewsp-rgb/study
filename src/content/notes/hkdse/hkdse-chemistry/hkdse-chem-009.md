# IX. Rate of Reaction — HKDSE Chemistry Notes

This topic covers Topic IX of the Compulsory Part of HKDSE Chemistry. It addresses rate of reaction expressions; factors affecting rate (concentration, surface area, temperature, catalysts); collision theory and activation energy; the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution; and industrial applications.

> Verify the live HKDSE Chemistry Assessment Framework on https://www.hkeaa.edu.hk/ before planning revision.

---

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Rate of reaction**: change in concentration of reactant or product per unit time (mol/dm³/s). Measured by following the disappearance of a reactant or the appearance of a product over time.
- **Factors affecting rate**: concentration (higher concentration → faster rate); surface area (smaller particles → faster rate); temperature (higher temperature → faster rate); catalyst (lowers activation energy → faster rate); light (for photochemical reactions).
- **Collision theory**: reactions occur when reactant particles collide with sufficient energy (≥ activation energy) and the correct orientation.
- **Activation energy (Ea)**: the minimum energy that colliding particles must have for a reaction to occur.
- **Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution**: the distribution of molecular energies at a given temperature. At higher temperatures, the curve shifts right and flattens — more molecules have energy ≥ Ea.
- **Catalyst**: a substance that increases the rate of reaction without being consumed. Provides an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy. Homogeneous catalysts are in the same phase as the reactants; heterogeneous catalysts are in a different phase.
- **Industrial applications**: contact process (V₂O₅ catalyst for SO₂ oxidation), Haber process (Fe catalyst for N₂ + H₂), catalytic cracking (zeolite catalysts), enzyme catalysis (biological reactions).

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing rate with equilibrium position — catalysts do not change the equilibrium constant.
- Forgetting that surface area only matters for heterogeneous reactions involving solids.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Measuring rate of reaction

**Methods**:

- Following the disappearance of a coloured reactant (e.g. MnO₄⁻) or appearance of a coloured product (e.g. I₂).
- Following the volume of gas produced (e.g. in a reaction with dilute HCl, gas is collected and the volume measured over time).
- Following the change in mass (e.g. when CO₂ is released, the reaction mixture loses mass).
- Following the change in pH (in acid-base reactions).
- Following the change in conductivity (in reactions involving electrolytes).

The rate is determined from the slope of the concentration vs. time graph (or volume/mass/pH vs. time).

#### Collision theory

For a reaction to occur, reactant particles must:

1. **Collide** — collisions are necessary but not sufficient.
2. **Have sufficient energy** — kinetic energy ≥ activation energy Ea.
3. **Have the correct orientation** — the geometry of the collision must be favourable.

The rate is proportional to the number of "effective" collisions per unit time.

#### Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution

At a given temperature T, the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution shows the fraction of molecules with each kinetic energy. The distribution has:

- A peak at the most probable energy.
- A long tail at high energies (few molecules have very high energies).
- A small fraction of molecules with energy ≥ Ea (these can react).

At higher T, the distribution shifts right and flattens — more molecules have energy ≥ Ea, so the rate increases.

#### Catalysts

A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower Ea. The catalyst is not consumed in the overall reaction (it may be consumed in one step but regenerated in another).

**Heterogeneous catalysis**: the catalyst is in a different phase from the reactants (e.g. solid catalyst with gaseous reactants). Adsorption of reactants on the catalyst surface lowers Ea.

**Homogeneous catalysis**: the catalyst is in the same phase (e.g. aqueous catalyst with aqueous reactants). The catalyst forms an intermediate with the reactants.

**Enzyme catalysis**: enzymes are biological catalysts. They are highly specific, work under mild conditions, and can be regulated by inhibitors or activators.

**Industrial examples**:

- Haber process: Fe catalyst for N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃.
- Contact process: V₂O₅ catalyst for 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃.
- Catalytic converter in cars: Pt, Pd, Rh catalysts for CO + unburnt hydrocarbons → CO₂ + H₂O.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Cross-topic links

- **X. Chemical Equilibrium**: rate of forward and reverse reactions; equilibrium when the rates are equal.
- **VIII. Chemical Reactions and Energy**: activation energy is the kinetic barrier; ΔH is the thermodynamic driving force.
- **XIII. Industrial Chemistry (Elective)**: industrial processes balance rate, equilibrium and economics.

#### Exam technique

- **For "explain the effect" questions**: use collision theory — name the factor, explain how it increases the frequency of effective collisions.
- **For Maxwell-Boltzmann questions**: draw the distribution curves at two temperatures, label the axes, and shade the area above Ea.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Catalysts change the position of equilibrium." No — catalysts speed up both forward and reverse reactions equally, leaving the equilibrium constant unchanged.
- "Higher temperature always means higher rate." For most reactions yes, but for enzyme-catalysed reactions, very high temperatures denature the enzyme.
- "Activation energy is the energy released in a reaction." Activation energy is the energy required to start the reaction; it is distinct from ΔH.
