# Forensic Pathology — Wound Classification, Medicolegal Aspects of Injuries, Ante-mortem vs. Post-mortem Wounds

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Forensic Pathology** concerns the examination of injuries on a living or dead person to determine their nature, causative weapon, age, and whether they are **antemortem** or **postmortem** — and to correlate findings with medicolegal questions of causation and survival.

- **Mechanical injuries** are classified as: **abrasion** (epidermis only), **contusion/bruise** (extravasated blood in tissues), **laceration** (tissue torn by blunt force), **incised wound** (length > depth, sharp edge), **stab wound** (depth > length, pointed weapon), and **firearm wound** (entry, exit, range).
- **Vital reactions** confirming an antemortem wound: **hemorrhage**, **clotting of blood** at margins, **inflammation** (leucocyte margination within 30 min), and later **healing** (granulation tissue at 48–72 h). Postmortem wounds show none of these.
- **Section 320 IPC** lists eight kinds of **grievous hurt** — fracture/dislocation, privation of sight/hearing/member/joint, permanent disfiguration, emasculation, or any hurt endangering life. **Section 319 IPC** defines simple hurt.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Classification of Mechanical Injuries

Mechanical injuries are grouped by the causative force and tissue reaction. Each type carries diagnostic clues about the weapon.

| Type | Defining Feature | Typical Cause |
|---|---|---|
| Abrasion | Superficial epidermis loss; subtypes: scratch, graze, pressure, imprint | Rubbing against rough surface |
| Contusion (bruise) | Extravasated blood within tissues, not wiped off | Blunt force rupturing vessels |
| Laceration | Tearing of skin/soft tissue by blunt force | Fall, blow, vehicular impact |
| Incised wound | Length > depth; clean, everted, retracted edges | Sharp-edged weapon (knife, blade) |
| Stab / punctured wound | Depth > length & breadth; track in tissues | Pointed weapon (dagger, ice-pick) |
| Firearm wound | Entry + possible exit; abrasion collar, tattooing, soot | Bullet, shotgun, pellet |

#### Antemortem vs. Postmortem Wounds

The single most-tested distinction in FMGE Forensic is whether a wound was inflicted before or after death. Antemortem wounds show **vital reactions** — the body's living response to injury.

> **Vital reactions** are tissue-level proof of life at the moment of injury: bleeding, clotting, inflammation, and later repair.

| Feature | Antemortem | Postmortem |
|---|---|---|
| Hemorrhage | Copious, arterial if major vessel cut | Scant, venous ooze |
| Edges | Swollen, retracted, everted | Gaping, not swollen |
| Clotting | Firm clots present | No clots / soft postmortem clot |
| Inflammation | Leucocyte margination in 30 min–hours | Absent |
| Healing | Granulation tissue over days | Absent |
| Tissue colour | Red, infiltrated with blood | Pale, yellowish |

#### Age of Injury (Vital Reaction Timeline)

- **0–12 h:** hemorrhage only, no cellular reaction.
- **12–24 h:** polymorphonuclear leucocytes appear.
- **24–48 h:** macrophages, early epithelialization.
- **48–72 h:** capillary budding, fibroblast proliferation, granulation tissue.
- **3–5 days:** collagen deposition.
- **1–2 weeks:** well-formed granulation tissue.
- **Weeks–months:** scar maturation.

#### Medicolegal Aspects

A wound certificate must record **size, shape, situation, margins, direction, depth, dimensions, weapon class, age of injury, simple vs grievous nature**, and bear the doctor's signature with date and the patient's identification marks. Under **Section 320 IPC**, grievous hurt includes eight specific kinds — *any one* of them qualifies, not only "danger to life." FMGE frequently tests this list and the distinction between **cause of death** (the disease/injury producing death) and **manner of death** (homicide, suicide, accident, natural, undetermined).

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Forensic Traps

**Contusion vs postmortem hypostasis.** Both appear bluish, but a contusion shows *extravascular* blood that does not shift on repositioning the body, whereas hypostasis is *intravascular* and migrates with posture. Histology of a sliced contusion reveals RBCs infiltrating dermal/interstitial planes; hypostasis shows RBCs confined within vessels.

**Laceration regularity.** A laceration over a bony ridge (scalp, shin, iliac crest) can have surprisingly clean margins — the defining criterion is *blunt-force tissue tearing*, not margin shape. Misclassifying it as an incised wound is a common FMGE trap.

**Hesitation cuts.** Self-inflicted incised wounds in suicide typically show multiple superficial, parallel, tentative incisions near the fatal wound, on the **accessible side** — left forearm or throat in a right-handed individual. Their absence should prompt consideration of homicide or fabrication.

**Defence wounds.** Injuries on the **ulnar aspects of forearms, palms, and dorsum of hands** indicate the victim attempted to ward off an attack — a key medicolegal marker of assault.

#### Enzyme Histochemistry and Modern Methods

When histology is equivocal (early postmortem interval, decomposed tissue), **enzyme histochemistry** can demonstrate antemortem vitality: increased **ATPase, esterase, and aminopeptidase** activity at wound margins reflects pre-death cellular metabolism. Immunohistochemistry for **fibronectin, fibrin, and selectins** also helps in decomposed or charred bodies.

#### Wound Certificate — Practical Pearls

> Always record dimensions in **centimetres**, direction by **clock face** or anatomical landmarks, and specify the **weapon class** (sharp/blunt/firearm/pointed) the injury is consistent with — never over-commit to a specific weapon.

#### Practice Prompts

1. A body recovered 6 hours after death has a 4-cm neck incised wound. List **five features** you would examine to determine whether it is antemortem.
2. Apply **Section 320 IPC** to a victim with fracture of the mandible, permanent privation of sight of one eye, and two rib fractures — does each injury qualify as grievous hurt independently, and why?
