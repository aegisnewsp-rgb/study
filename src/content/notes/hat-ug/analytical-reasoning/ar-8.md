# Statement and Arguments

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A Statement-and-Arguments item on HAT-UG presents a short opinion, policy, or course of action, then asks you to judge each numbered argument as **Strong** or **Weak**. A **Strong argument** is directly relevant, factually grounded, and persuasive — it advances or genuinely weakens the statement. A **Weak argument** is vague, off-topic, emotionally skewed, or merely restates the obvious.

- **Direct relevance** — the argument must address the *core* issue, not a side point.
- **Logical force** — it relies on evidence or sound reasoning, not rhetoric alone.
- **Hidden assumptions** — every Strong argument rests on an unstated premise that must hold.
- **Independence** — each argument is judged on its own merit; one Strong does not imply the other is Weak.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Definition and Scope
The statement is a proposition — often a proposed policy or an opinion — while the arguments are reasons offered either in favour (pro) or against (con). HAT-UG tests your ability to filter rhetoric from reasoning. The five-option answer key is fixed:

| Answer code | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Only argument I is strong | I supports/weakens; II is vague or off-topic |
| Only argument II is strong | II supports/weakens; I is vague or off-topic |
| Both I and II are strong | Both are directly relevant and persuasive |
| Both I and II are weak | Neither advances the core issue convincingly |
| Either I or II is strong | Both are individually plausible but mutually exclusive in context |

#### The Four Tests of Strength
1. **Relevance** — does the argument address the statement's main claim?
2. **Specificity** — does it give concrete reasons rather than generalities?
3. **Factuality** — is the underlying premise factually credible?
4. **Persuasiveness** — would a reasonable reader be moved by it?

If any test fails, classify the argument as **Weak**.

#### Pro vs Con Arguments
- A **pro argument** strengthens the proposed course of action.
- A **con argument** undermines it by exposing flaws, costs, or unintended consequences.
- Misreading direction (treating a con as a pro) is a common HAT-UG trap; always re-read the statement's polarity before judging.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Common Traps
**Restated arguments** sound supportive but simply echo the statement in different words — these are Weak. **Emotionally charged** appeals without data are Weak even when they feel compelling. **Comparative** arguments ("X worked in country Y, so it will work here") are Strong only when the parallel is genuinely applicable; otherwise they fail the factuality test.

#### Worked Micro-Example
**Statement:** "Mobile phones should be banned in classrooms."
- **Argument I:** "Banning phones removes distractions, so students will concentrate better." → **Strong** (direct, plausible, addresses the core claim).
- **Argument II:** "Phones are bad." → **Weak** (vague, no specific mechanism, restates the position).

Answer: *Only argument I is strong.*

#### Exam Strategy for HAT-UG
- Analytical Reasoning contributes ~4% of the paper; expect 2–4 such MCQs.
- Read the statement twice, underline the *core claim*, then test each argument against the four gates.
- Eliminate options systematically: if Argument I fails relevance, codes containing "I is strong" drop out.
- Practice mixing Statement–Arguments with Statement–Assumption and Statement–Conclusion items — the Strong/Weak logic transfers across all three formats.

#### Practice prompts:
1. A statement proposes mandatory yoga in offices. Judge: (I) "It reduces stress." (II) "It improves productivity." — which are Strong?
2. A statement defends remote work. Judge: (I) "Commute time is wasted." (II) "Employees prefer comfort." — classify each.
