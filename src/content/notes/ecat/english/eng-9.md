# Spotting Errors

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Spotting Errors in ECAT English presents one sentence split into four underlined parts (a, b, c, d). You pick the part carrying a grammatical, idiomatic, or usage-based fault, or mark "no error" when the sentence is clean. Most items collapse into one of ten recurring traps: subject-verb agreement, tense slippage, wrong article, wrong preposition, pronoun case, broken parallelism, a dangling modifier, an unidiomatic word order, a faulty comparative, or voice confusion. ECAT awards roughly 4% weight to this section inside Verbal, and a single missed error can drop a borderline score below the merit cut-off.

| Part of Speech to Check | What Goes Wrong |
|---|---|
| Verb | Disagrees with subject in number/person |
| Tense marker | Switches tense without time-shift clue |
| Article (a / an / the) | Sound-based rule ignored |
| Preposition | Wrong collocation (interested *in*, not *on*) |
| Pronoun | Case, number, or gender mismatch with antecedent |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **S.T.A.M.P.** — **S**ubject-verb, **T**ense, **A**rticle, **M**odifier, **P**ronoun-Preposition-Parallelism. Scan the sentence in that order and 90% of ECAT errors surface within thirty seconds.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### How ECAT Frames the Question

Each item shows a single sentence with four underlined segments labelled a–d. Only one segment usually carries the defect; the "no error" option appears on roughly one in six items, so do not invent a fault to "have" an answer. Time budget is under 60 seconds per item — speed comes from pattern recognition, not from rereading the sentence three times.

#### The Ten Recurring Error Families

1. **Subject–Verb Agreement** — singular subject needs singular verb; ignore the prepositional phrase sandwiched between them (*The bag of nuts **is** on the table*).
2. **Tense Consistency** — present, past, and perfect forms cannot mix without a time-shift marker (*He **went** to the market and **buys** vegetables* → wrong).
3. **Article Choice** — *a* precedes consonant **sound**, *an* precedes vowel **sound**: *an honest man* (silent *h*), *a one-rupee note* (/w/ sound), *a European* (/j/ sound).
4. **Preposition Collocation** — fixed pairings: *good at, fond of, angry with, interested in, capable of, different from*. Memorise these as chunks.
5. **Pronoun Case** — after *than* and *as*, the nominative case is correct when the verb is understood: *She is taller than **I** (am)*, not *than me*.
6. **Parallelism** — items joined by *and, or, but, either-or, neither-nor, not only-but also* must share the same grammatical form (*to read, to write, and **to speak***, not *to read, writing, and speak*).
7. **Misplaced / Dangling Modifier** — the participial phrase must touch its subject: *Running across the road, **the bus hit him*** is wrong because *the bus* did not run.
8. **Faulty Comparative / Superlative** — *between* takes *between*; *among* takes *among*; comparative for two, superlative for more than two.
9. **Voice Mix-up** — active and passive cannot be spliced: *The teacher **explained** the lesson and the homework **was given*** is correct; mixing tenses breaks the link.
10. **Idiomatic Word Order** — *take it for granted*, *by and large*, *in spite of*, *on the whole* are frozen; do not "correct" them.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Trap | Correct Pattern | Common Wrong Choice | Why Students Slip |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subject–Verb | *The jury **has** given its verdict.* | *The jury **have** given…* | Treating collective noun as plural |
| Article | *an honest man* | *a honest man* | Spelling-based guess, not sound-based |
| Preposition | *He is good **at** math.* | *He is good **in** math.* | Borrowed from native idiom confusion |
| Pronoun case | *He is taller than **I**.* | *He is taller than **me**.* | Casual speech overruling grammar |
| Parallelism | *to read, to write, **to speak*** | *to read, writing, and **speak*** | Loose list-style "feels right" |
| Modifier | *Walking to school, **Ravi** saw a dog.* | *Walking to school, **a dog** bit Ravi.* | Modifier drifts from its logical subject |
| Tense | *She **went** and **came back**.* | *She **went** and **comes back**.* | Verb-final word hides earlier slip |

> 📌 **Formula Check:** There is no arithmetic formula here — the "formula" is the part-of-speech scan: **find the head noun, lock the verb; locate the modifier, lock the noun it touches; find the comparative, lock the case after *than/as*.**

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** *"Neither the principal nor the teachers (a) / were present at (b) / the meeting that (c) / was held yesterday. (d) / No error."* — Which segment contains the error?

#### Solution:

- Apply the **nearest-subject rule** under *neither…nor*: the verb agrees with the noun placed immediately before it, which is **teachers** (plural).
- Therefore the verb must be **were**, not *was*.
- Segment (a) carries the faulty verb form. Correct sentence: *"Neither the principal nor the teachers **were** present…"*

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Candidates flip the nearest-subject rule and select segment (c) or (d), assuming *principal* (singular) dictates the verb. ECAT specifically tests this inversion — train your eye to read the noun **after** *nor*, not the noun before *neither*.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases the Standard List Misses

- **Collective nouns split by "members of":** *A team of researchers **has** published…* — verb agrees with *team* (the head noun), not *researchers*.
- **"Each / Every / Either / Neither" as subject:** always singular, even when followed by *of* + plural: *Each of the students **was** given a book*.
- **Relative pronoun as subject:** in *The boy who **was** late…*, the verb agrees with *boy*, not *who*.
- **"More than one" + singular noun:** takes singular verb: *More than one student **has** topped*.
- **Titles and names as subject:** take singular verb even if plural-looking: *"The Arabian Nights" **is** a famous book*.
- **"As well as / along with / together with":** the verb follows the **first** subject, not the second: *The principal, along with the teachers, **was** absent*.
- **Conditional "If I were / he were":** use *were* for all persons in subjunctive: *If I **were** you…*; never *If I **was*** in formal ECAT items.
- **Gerund vs. infinitive after specific verbs:** *enjoy, mind, avoid, finish* take gerund; *want, decide, expect, hope* take infinitive. *I enjoy **to read*** is wrong; *I enjoy **reading*** is right.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. Identify the error: *"Hardly had I reached the station when the train (a) / has left (b) / and I had (c) / to wait for the next one. (d) / No error."* — *(Hint: past perfect pair, not present perfect.)*
2. Identify the error: *"Of the two proposals submitted (a) / by the committee, (b) / the latter one (c) / seems more practicable. (d) / No error."* — *(Hint: only two items → comparative, not superlative.)*

#### Exam Strategy Notes for ECAT

The Verbal section runs about 30 MCQs in roughly 25 minutes, so Spotting Errors (≈4% of total test, ≈3–5 items) rewards ruthless elimination. Read the underlined segment aloud silently — your ear often catches agreement slips your eye misses. Always test the **head noun first**, then the **verb**, then the **modifier placement**; this three-step scan catches every item on this page within 45 seconds. Leave ambiguous items for the second pass instead of burning two minutes on one question. Official ECAT preparation resources at `ecat.nts.org.pk` and `hec.gov.pk` confirm that Verbal accuracy, not speed alone, decides the merit list — clean grammar separates the top decile from the middle pack.
