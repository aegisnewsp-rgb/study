# Alcohols, Phenols, and Ethers

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

- **Alcohols (R–OH)**: hydroxyl bonded to an sp³ carbon of an aliphatic chain; classified as **1°, 2°, or 3°** by the carbon bearing –OH.
- **Phenols (Ar–OH)**: hydroxyl bonded directly to an sp² aromatic carbon; the **phenoxide ion** is resonance-stabilised, so pKa(phenol) ≈ 10 versus pKa(ethanol) ≈ 16.
- **Ethers (R–O–R′)**: an oxygen linking two alkyl/aryl groups; **no O–H**, so ethers cannot H-bond as donors — this is why their boiling points sit *below* alcohols of similar mass.
- **Acidity ladder**: carboxylic acid > phenol > water > alcohol — a one-line ranking examiners love to test.
- **Lucas test**: 3° alcohol → turbidity in ≈1 min; 2° → 5–10 min; 1° → no turbidity at room temperature.
- **Williamson synthesis**: R–ONa + R′–X → R–O–R′; uses a **primary** alkyl halide (SN2) — avoid tertiary, which gives elimination.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Classification and Nomenclature
Monohydric alcohols carry one –OH; diols (glycol), triols (glycerol), and polyols extend the family. IUPAC names use the suffix **-ol** with the lowest locant (propan-1-ol, butan-2-ol). Phenols are named as hydroxy derivatives of benzene (e.g. 2-nitrophenol). Ethers are named **alkoxyalkanes** (methoxyethane) or, for aryl alkyl ethers, as phenoxyalkanes.

#### Physical Properties and Hydrogen Bonding
Boiling point follows the order **R–OH > R–O–R′ > R–H** at comparable molar mass. Alcohols donate and accept H-bonds; ethers accept only, via oxygen's lone pairs. Water solubility of lower alcohols and ethers is high because oxygen acts as an H-bond acceptor.

#### Acidity Comparison

| Compound | Approx. pKa | Conjugate base stability |
|---|---|---|
| Carboxylic acid | 4–5 | Two equivalent resonance structures |
| Phenol | ≈ 10 | Resonance across aromatic ring |
| Water | 15.7 | None beyond inductive |
| Ethanol | ≈ 16 | No resonance; +I from alkyl |

> **Tip:** The sp² carbon in phenol is **more electronegative** (33% s-character) than the sp³ carbon of alcohols, which further stabilises the phenoxide.

#### Preparation of Alcohols
- Acid-catalysed hydration of alkenes (Markovnikov).
- **Oxymercuration–demercuration** for anti-Markovnikov-free Markovnikov addition.
- Reduction: **NaBH₄** reduces aldehydes/ketones; **LiAlH₄** reduces carboxylic acids, esters, amides.
- **Grignard addition**: RCHO gives a 2° alcohol; RCOR′ gives a 3° alcohol.

#### Reactions of Alcohols
- With Na → alkoxide + ½ H₂.
- With **PCl₅, PCl₃, SOCl₂** → alkyl chloride.
- **Esterification** (Fischer) with RCOOH / H⁺.
- Oxidation: **1° → aldehyde → carboxylic acid**; **2° → ketone**; **3° resists** oxidation.
- **Dehydration** (conc. H₂SO₄, 413–440 K) gives alkene; ease follows 3° > 2° > 1°.

#### Phenol-Specific Reactions
- Neutral **FeCl₃** → violet/blue/green colour (diagnostic).
- Bromination in CCl₄ → **2,4,6-tribromophenol** (–OH is strongly activating, ortho/para-directing).
- **Kolbe–Schmitt** (CO₂ + NaOH, then H⁺) → salicylic acid.
- **Reimer–Tiemann** (CHCl₃ + aq. NaOH) → salicylaldehyde.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases in Acidity and Substitution
Phenols bearing **electron-withdrawing groups** (–NO₂, –CN) at ortho/para positions drop pKa sharply — *picric acid* (2,4,6-trinitrophenol) reaches pKa ≈ 0.4, behaving as a strong acid. Conversely, electron-donating groups (–CH₃, –OCH₃) make phenols less acidic than phenol itself.

#### Williamson Synthesis — Substrate Rules
The alkoxide should attack via **SN2**, so the alkyl halide must be **methyl or primary**. With secondary halides, E2 competes; with tertiary, elimination dominates and ether yields collapse. Bulky alkoxides (tert-butoxide) also push toward elimination when paired with secondary halides.

#### Ether Cleavage Mechanism
With excess HI at reflux: **R–O–R′ + 2 HI → R–I + R′–I + H₂O**. The smaller (less hindered) alkyl group departs as the alkyl iodide; the larger one leaves as the alcohol that is then re-iodinated. Methyl aryl ethers (anisole) cleave to give **CH₃I + ArOH**, because aryl C–O bonds resist SN2.

#### Worked Example — Predicting Products
React *2-methylpropan-2-ol* with conc. H₂SO₄ at 430 K.
1. Protonation of –OH → oxonium ion.
2. Loss of water (E1 favoured for 3°) → tert-butyl cation.
3. β-H elimination → **2-methylpropene** (major) and trace 2-methylpropane by reduction.
Compare with propan-1-ol: the same conditions give **propene via E2**, requiring higher temperature (≈ 440 K) because primary carbocations do not form.

#### Common Mistakes in the HAAD Setting
- Marking phenol and alcohol acidity as equal — they differ by **six pKa units**.
- Reporting **immediate turbidity** for 1° alcohols in the Lucas test — they stay clear at 25 °C.
- Pairing a 3° alkyl halide with an alkoxide in Williamson — expect alkene, not ether.
- Forgetting that **anisole cleaves to CH₃I + phenol**, not PhI + methanol.
- Treating ether as unreactive in *all* conditions — concentrated HI/HBr cleave it readily.

#### Practice Prompts
1. Arrange phenol, *p*-nitrophenol, *p*-cresol, and ethanol in **increasing acidity**, and justify using resonance + inductive effects.
2. Outline a two-step synthesis of **ethoxyethane** starting from ethanol and any inorganic reagents, naming the intermediate alkyl halide and the mechanism class at each step.
