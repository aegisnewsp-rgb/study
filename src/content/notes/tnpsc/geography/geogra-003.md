# Himalayas: Three Parallel Ranges

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

**Physical features of India** are the natural landforms — mountains, plateaus, plains, and coastal strips — that shape drainage, agriculture, and settlement across the subcontinent. For TNPSC Group 1, three elements dominate every cycle:

- **The Himalayan System** splits into three ranges: **Greater Himalayas (Himadri)** above 6,000 m, **Middle Himalayas (Himachal)** at 3,000–4,500 m, and **Outer Himalayas (Shivalik)** below 3,000 m. They rose during the **Tertiary period** (50 Ma) when the Indian Plate collided with the Eurasian Plate.
- **The Indo-Gangetic-Brahmaputra Plain** covers ~7 lakh sq km — the world's largest alluvial plain — built by the Indus, Ganga, and Brahmaputra. Sub-regions include Rajasthan desert, Punjab-Haryana plain, Ganga plain, and Brahmaputra valley.
- **The Peninsular Plateau** divides into the **Central Highlands** (Malwa, Bundelkhand, Chota Nagpur) and the **Deccan Plateau** (Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu sections), composed of Archean granites, gneisses, and Dharwar schists.

Coastal features matter too: the **Western Ghats** (UNESCO, 2018; 1,600 km continuous) versus the **Eastern Ghats** (discontinuous, lower). The **Western Coastal Plain** (narrow, estuarine backwaters — Malabar Coast) contrasts with the wider **Eastern Coastal Plain** (Mahanadi–Cauvery deltas, Coromandel Coast).

**TNPSC exam pointers:** Expect match-the-following on mountain ranges and elevations, or description questions on the three Himalayan subdivisions. The Doab–Bhabar–Terai distinction and the Western vs Eastern Ghats contrast appear almost every cycle.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Himalayas: Three Parallel Ranges

The Himalayas formed from the **Tertiary collision** at roughly 40–50 mm/year — the fastest continental convergence on Earth. The **Greater Himalayas (Himadri)** contain peaks above 6,000 m including Mount Everest (8,586 m) and form a continuous barrier. The **Middle Himalayas (Himachal)** lie between 3,000–4,500 m, are heavily forested, and include popular hill stations (Shimla, Nainital). The **Outer Himalayas (Shivalik)** stand below 3,000 m, composed of unconsolidated gravel and sand (siwalik sediments), and are prone to landslides.

The **Siwalik Range** is the newest section of the Himalayan foothills, running from Jammu & Kashmir to Assam. The **Purvanchal Hills** extend the Himalayan system eastward along India's northeastern border, comprising the Patkai, Naga, and Lushai hills. The **Trans-Himalaya** includes the **Ladakh Plateau** and the **Karakoram** — the syntaxial bend at the western extremity marks where the range curves sharply south.

#### Indo-Gangetic-Brahmaputra Plain

This plain's four subdivisions reflect different depositional environments. The **Rajasthan desert section** in the west is arid with sand dunes. The **Punjab-Haryana plain** is the dissected alluvial plain of the five rivers (Punjab = "five rivers"). The **Ganga plain** is the most extensive, fertile, and densely populated. The **Brahmaputra valley** in Assam is marshy (terai-like) and prone to annual flooding.

Three terrace levels matter: **Bhabar** (porous pebble belt at the foothills, 8–16 km wide, where streams disappear underground), **Terai** (damp, marshy zone south of Bhabar, reclaimed for agriculture), **Bhangar** (older Pleistocene alluvium with calcareous kankar nodules), and **Khadar** (newer floodplain alluvium, sandy, renewed annually).

#### Peninsular Plateau

The **Central Highlands** include the **Malwa Plateau** (Madhya Pradesh, volcanic soils), **Bundelkhand** (P.State, granite domes), **Chota Nagpur Plateau** (Jharkhand, rich in mica and coal), and **Meghalaya Plateau** (northeastern extension). The **Deccan Plateau** — a vast basaltic lava flow region called the **Deccan Traps** (Cretaceous period, 65 Ma) — covers Maharashtra, Karnataka, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, and Tamil Nadu. These traps thin eastward and are overlaid by Laterite.

The **Aravalli Range** (Delhi to Gujarat, 692 km) is the oldest fold mountain in India, running northwest–southeast. The **Vindhya Range** and **Satpura Range** form the connecting link between the Central Highlands and the Deccan, with the Narmada and Tapti rivers flowing through their valleys.

#### Coastal Plains

| Feature | Western Coastal Plain | Eastern Coastal Plain |
|---|---|---|
| Width | 50–100 km | 100–130 km |
| Key sections | Konkan (Mumbai–Goa), Malabar Coast (Kerala) | Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Cauvery deltas; Coromandel Coast |
| Character | Estuarine backwaters, narrow beaches | Wide deltas, extensive beaches |
| Rivers | Narmada, Tapti (form estuaries) | Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Cauvery (form deltas) |

The **Western Ghats** (Sahyadri) run 1,600 km continuously from Tapi to Kanyakumari at 900–1,100 m average elevation; major passes include **Palakkad Gap** (Kerala–Tamil Nadu trade route), **Bhor Ghat** (Mumbai–Pune rail route), and **Goa Gap**. The **Eastern Ghats** are discontinuous, lower (average 450 m), and dissected by eastward-flowing rivers.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Geomorphological Mechanisms

The Himalayas continue to rise ~5 mm/year through **seismic activity** along the Main Central Thrust (MCT) and Main Boundary Fault (MBF). The **syntaxial bend** at the western extremity (Nanga Parbat–Kashmir syntaxis) represents the most intense crustal shortening. The **Garo-Rajmahal Gap** — a structural discontinuity in the Himalayan system — allows the Brahmaputra to breach the range and enter Bangladesh. TNPSC has previously asked about the Brahmaputra's anomalous transverse course through this gap.

The **Deccan Traps** represent one of Earth's largest continental flood basalt provinces, erupted over 5 million years during the Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary. Their weathering produced the rich **black cotton soil (regur)** of Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh, ideal for cotton cultivation. The **Laterite** capping on Eastern Ghats and Deccan plateau edges formed through intense tropical weathering and is a key distinguishing feature.

#### Biodiversity and UNESCO Recognition

The **Western Ghats** were designated a **UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2018** and rank among Earth's eight "hottest biodiversity hotspots." They host ~7,400 plant species (375 endemic), 139 endemic amphibian species, and 16 endemic bird species. The **Silent Valley** in Kerala, **Mahabaleshwar–Koyna** region, and **Brahmagiri** wildlife sanctuary are TNPSC-relevant micro-topics.

#### Common TNPSC Mistakes

1. **Confusing Bhabar and Terai**: Bhabar is the narrow pebble-zone at the foothills; Terai is the wide marshy belt south of it. Students often swap their descriptions.
2. **Equating Western and Eastern Ghats**: The Western Ghats are continuous, higher, and recognized by UNESCO; the Eastern Ghats are discontinuous, lower, and dissected. They are not equivalent features.
3. **Forgetting the Deccan Traps' age**: The traps are Cretaceous (~65 Ma), not the same age as Himalayan uplift. They post-date the Gondwana sedimentation.

#### Practice Prompts

1. **Describe with a sketch** the three Himalayan ranges from north to south, indicating their average elevation, rock type, and two major peaks or hill stations found in each.
2. **Compare and contrast** the Western Coastal Plain and Eastern Coastal Plain under the heads of width, drainage pattern, agricultural significance, and major ports.
