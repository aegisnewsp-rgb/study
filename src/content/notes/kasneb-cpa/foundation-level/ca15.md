# CA15: Quantitative Analysis

CA15 is one of the six Foundation Level papers of the KASNEB CPA qualification. KASNEB states that examinations are held three times each year — April, August and December — and that CPA papers are three hours long, with morning sessions beginning at 9:00 a.m. and afternoon sessions at 2:00 p.m.

Three hours is the number that should shape your preparation. It is long enough that most candidates who have only done one-hour practice runs lose marks in the final third of the paper, not through ignorance but through fatigue and unfinished working.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> Three checks cover most of the marks here, and all three cost seconds: read what is being compared before computing, show the formula with labelled values, and keep units visible.

| Tool | The thing to hold on to | The error it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| Index numbers | Base-period figures equal 100 | Supplying a base year the question never names |
| Moving average | Averaging four consecutive quarters cancels the seasonal effect | Reading trend half a period late |
| Correlation | $r^2$ carries the share of variation explained, not $r$ | Attributing a percentage to the coefficient |
| Regression | The least-squares line runs through the means of both variables | An intercept that no mean can reproduce |

**Where a three-hour paper actually drains you.** KASNEB sits CPA papers three times a year — April, August and December — and each runs for three hours, with morning sessions from 9:00 a.m. and afternoon sessions from 2:00 p.m. The evidence for treating this as a pacing problem rather than a revision problem is straightforward: candidates who have only rehearsed on one-hour practice runs tend to lose the final third of the paper through fatigue and unfinished working rather than through gaps in knowledge. That is trainable.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

> The working picture — which method belongs to which question shape, and which check is free once you have the answer.

| Question shape | The method | The free check |
|---|---|---|
| A single item compared across two periods | Current value over base value, times 100 | Does the base period itself read 100? |
| A value index built from price and volume | Price index times volume index, over 100 | Have you written down which index you are using? |
| Seasonal variation from a time series | A centred four-quarter moving average | Do the estimated seasonal figures sum to zero? |
| A regression line | Least squares through the means | Does the line still cross both means? |

**Bank the conceptual cluster while you are fresh.** Correlation and regression items are decided by distinctions — a coefficient against the square of that coefficient, an association against a causal claim, a zero reading against an absence of relationship — and several can be answered without any calculation at all. Index and seasonal work is the opposite: the method is short but the arithmetic is unforgiving of carelessness. On a three-hour paper, taking the conceptual items first converts your strongest hour into marks before the working-heavy half can slow you down. This is one of six Foundation Level papers competing with CA11 and CA23 for the same revision hours, which makes the concept items the cheapest return available.

### What the paper actually uses

Quantitative analysis in a professional accounting qualification is applied statistics and applied mathematics, not pure mathematics. The recurring tools are index numbers, time series, measures of central tendency and dispersion, correlation, regression, probability and financial maths. Almost every question is set in a business context — costs, sales, prices, wages, output — so the second half of the work is reading the context correctly before touching the arithmetic.

Three habits cover most of the losses.

Read what is being compared before you compute anything. A statement in a time series can be misleading because the base year changed, and an item that gives you an index without naming a base year is testing whether you will assume one.

Show the formula and substitute labelled values. On a three-hour paper with working shown, a correct method with an arithmetic slip often still earns method marks. A bare answer with the right number by luck does not, and it cannot be checked.

Keep units visible throughout. An index is unitless, a variance has squared units, a correlation coefficient has no units at all. Answering a question about a variance in currency units is a signal you have taken the square root at the wrong point.

### Index numbers

A simple index for one item is $\dfrac{\text{current value}}{\text{base value}} \times 100$. Base period figures equal 100.

Price and volume indices combined give a value index, and the standard formula is Laspeyres for a current-weighting question: value index equals price index multiplied by volume index, divided by 100. Learn one formula properly rather than three approximately, and write out which one you are using — the examiner is marking method.

Chain indices link successive periods, and the trap is that they cannot simply be added or compared directly across the series.

### Time series

Four components: trend, seasonal, cyclical and random. A four-quarter moving average isolates trend because summing four consecutive quarters cancels the seasonal effect, and it must be centred — an uncentred average sits half a period late.

When a moving average is unweighted, the estimated seasonal variations sum to zero by construction. If your seasonal figures do not sum to zero, the arithmetic is wrong, and it is a free self-check that costs nothing.

### Correlation and regression

The Pearson correlation coefficient lies between minus one and plus one, with zero meaning no linear relationship. Correlation measures linear association only — a strong curved relationship can give a coefficient near zero, which is a standard examiner trap.

The coefficient of determination is $r^2$, the proportion of the variation in one variable explained by a linear relationship with the other. Saying "$r$ explains 80 per cent" is the error that loses the mark; $r^2$ explains 80 per cent.

Regression: the least-squares line passes through the means of both variables, always. That single geometric fact gives you a free check on whether your intercept is right.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (3mo+)

> What separates a candidate who can operate the tools from one who can tell whether the tool fits the question in front of them.

#### The base period is the exam's favourite trap
An index is a ratio presented as a percentage, and until the base is fixed the ratio means nothing. When a statement hands you an index without naming its base period, the question is not testing your arithmetic — it is testing whether you will quietly supply a base from memory and carry it through four lines of working. Reading the comparison before computing costs you one line of working and removes the entire error class, which is why the first habit on this paper pays for itself repeatedly.

#### An unweighted moving average checks itself
Average four consecutive quarters with equal weights and the seasonal component cancels by construction, so the seasonal variations you estimate from it must sum to zero. That is not a rule to memorise; it is a consequence you can use as a self-check with no arithmetic attached. When your figures do not sum to zero the fault sits upstream in the centring, and reworking the seasonal step alone will never find it — the sum tells you where to look instead.

#### The line through the means is geometry, not formula
Every least-squares fit runs through the centroid of the data, which is simply the point where the two means sit. That single geometric fact catches a wrong intercept faster than any recalculation, because an intercept that fails to reproduce the mean of the dependent variable is wrong by definition rather than by arithmetic slip. It also explains why correlation and regression belong inside one item: the coefficient and the slope describe the same fit, so a coefficient close to minus one corresponds to a steeply negative line rather than to an absence of relationship.

| Trap | What it looks like on the page | The correction |
|---|---|---|
| $r$ explains the variation | A candidate writes that $r$ explains 80 per cent | Square it before you state a proportion |
| Zero means unrelated | "There is no relationship here" | No *linear* relationship — the narrower claim |
| Chain indices compared directly | Two chain indices added or differenced across a series | They do not aggregate; rebuild the series |
| Seasonal figures that do not sum to zero | The four quarterly estimates do not cancel | The centring is wrong, not the seasonal step |

### Common mistakes

Saying $r$ explains a percentage of the variation. $r^2$ does that.

Forgetting that correlation is not causation, and saying so only as a slogan rather than as part of the answer — an item usually wants you to say what third variable could produce the observed association.

Using an uncentred moving average and then computing seasonal variations from it.

Assuming a coefficient of zero means the variables are unrelated. It means no *linear* relationship, which is a narrower and more precise statement.

Losing method marks by giving bare answers on a three-hour paper.

Confusing a percentage change with a percentage-point change, particularly in a ratio item where the base is not obvious.
