# Management and Initial Stabilisation

Step 3 "tests your ability to apply medical knowledge and understanding of biomedical and clinical science for unsupervised practice". Everything else in that sentence follows from *unsupervised*. In the earlier steps a stem is a question with a right answer. In Step 3 it is a situation with several defensible actions, a limited clock, and no supervisor to check your reasoning — so the exam is testing **what you would do first, and what you would deliberately not do yet**.

## The one structural skill: priority

Almost every Step 3 item is a triage question wearing clinical clothing. Before anything else, ask whether anything is immediately life-threatening, what the single most likely cause is, and what can safely wait at least an hour. Then order your actions by that. The commonest wrong answer is doing all the correct things in the wrong order — including the resuscitation measure, just after an avoidable delay.

## Opening an encounter

Under real pressure, structure beats memory. Check airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure first if anything is unstable; that takes seconds, not minutes. Then take a focused history built around onset, tempo, severity and direction of travel, because tempo is the most informative word available: sudden, gradual, episodic, progressive. Follow with a targeted examination aimed at a differential you are already holding, rather than a full head-to-toe sweep. Manage the reversible problems immediately instead of completing the plan first. Only then come to investigations and the differential you can actually defend.

## What to do before you have a diagnosis

This is where Step 3 differs from everything before it. Frequently the correct answer is to manage a problem you have not fully explained.

In sepsis you take cultures and a lactate, start broad-spectrum antibiotics and give fluids, all without waiting to localise the source. In an acute abdomen you resuscitate, give analgesia, and investigate — pain relief is not withheld pending a diagnosis. In chest pain you treat the worst plausible cause while you exclude the others. In diabetic ketoacidosis fluids and insulin begin before the anion gap is fully worked out. In stroke the diagnostic pathway starts with imaging, but time-critical management runs alongside it.

The judgement underneath is the same every time: the cost of treating correctly is low, the cost of delaying is high, and a diagnosis is not required in order to act safely.

## What to defer

Deferring is an active choice and the exam marks it. The candidate answers that are wrong tend to order every available test. It is safe to defer specialist-only investigations when a first-line test will guide the next step; non-urgent imaging on a patient who is stable and already has a management plan; confirmatory tests when the clinical picture is sufficient to treat; and any investigation that would not change the immediate management.

Say explicitly what you are not ordering and why. In Step 3, answers that omit this read as incomplete rather than thorough.

## Escalation and handover

Escalate on the patient's physiology and trajectory, not on your own discomfort. A patient who is deteriorating despite treatment needs a senior review now, not when the next shift starts.

A good handover is structured: what the problem is, what has been done and with what result, what the current observations are, what you are worried about, and what you want the senior to do. Vague narratives lose the problem; the examiner is listening for whether you can hand over safely.

## Paediatric, obstetric and elderly variations

Children deteriorate faster and compensate better, so a child who looks well may already be in compensated shock and normal heart rate is the wrong benchmark. Pregnancy changes what is safe and what is physiological, altering drug safety, imaging choices and the range of normal observations. Older patients present atypically, with delirium standing in for the symptom and several problems at once, and polypharmacy is frequently the cause of the presentation itself.

## Common mistakes

The most expensive error is getting the right answers in the wrong order and losing marks on sequencing rather than on knowledge. The second is ordering every available test instead of choosing, which reads as poor judgement rather than thoroughness. The third is waiting for a diagnosis before treating something time-critical.

Beyond those: not deferring anything at all, which the marker reads as inability to prioritise; letting your own uncertainty delay escalation on a patient who is deteriorating; and treating a physiologically abnormal value as though it were the diagnosis, when the value is the finding and the diagnosis is what you still have to make.
