# Chemistry Foundations

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

ACER's Biological and Physical Sciences section assesses the ability "to identify knowledge in new contexts, and analyse and interpret data" across a pool whose knowledge ceiling "generally corresponds to the first year of university studies". Chemistry is the part of that pool where a small number of rules, applied without exception, solves most questions: the mole is the unit of thinking, and gradients drive every reaction.

ACER publishes no per-topic weightage for the section, so the split below is StudyRoadmap's own study organisation.

**The five tools and what each one is for.**

| Tool | The question it answers | The trap it removes |
|---|---|---|
| The mole | "How many particles is that?" | Mass is not amount; convert to moles first |
| Stoichiometry | "Which reagent runs out first?" | Limiting reagent decides the yield, not the excess one |
| Concentration | "How strong is this solution?" | Molarity, mass percent and ppm are different measures |
| pH and buffers | "How acidic, and does it resist change?" | Strong acids dissociate completely; weak ones only partly |
| Enthalpy | "Is heat given out or taken in?" | Breaking bonds absorbs energy; forming bonds releases it |

**Periodic trends, with the reason attached.** Radius falls across a period because the extra protons pull the same shell closer, and rises down a group because each new row adds a shell. Ionisation energy rises across and falls down, with two exceptions you can explain rather than memorise: group 2 sits above group 13 because a full s subshell is stable, and group 15 sits above group 16 because paired p electrons repel. Electronegativity follows the same shape as ionisation energy, and metallic character runs the opposite way.

**Bonding and what it predicts.**

| Bond type | How it forms | Properties it explains |
|---|---|---|
| Ionic | Electrons transferred, ions in a lattice | High melting point, brittle, soluble only if polar, conducts when molten |
| Covalent | Shared electron pairs | Molecular substances are soft with low melting points; networks are hard |
| Metallic | Delocalised electrons in a sea of cations | Conducts as a solid, malleable, ductile |

**Molecular shape follows the electron pairs.** Repulsions rank lone pair–lone pair above lone pair–bond pair above bond pair–bond pair, so each extra lone pair squeezes the bond angle. Methane is tetrahedral at 109.5°, ammonia is trigonal pyramidal at about 107°, water is bent at about 104.5°, and carbon dioxide is linear at 180°.

**The mole, in one line.** n = m ÷ M, where m is mass in grams and M is molar mass in grams per mole.

**pH, in two lines.** pH = −log[H⁺], and for a buffer pH = pKa + log([A⁻] ÷ [HA]).

**Memorise for the Lite tier:**
- n = m ÷ M, and n = c × V for solutions.
- Molar mass of an element equals its atomic mass in grams per mole.
- Limiting reagent gives the theoretical yield; the excess reagent is left over.
- M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ for a dilution.
- Strong acid, strong base and strong electrolytes dissociate fully; weak ones partly.
- Buffer capacity is greatest when [A⁻] equals [HA], which is where pH equals pKa.
- Atomic radius decreases across a period and increases down a group.
- Ionisation energy exceptions: group 2 above group 13, group 15 above group 16.
- Shape: methane tetrahedral, ammonia pyramidal, water bent, carbon dioxide linear.
- ΔH = energy of bonds broken − energy of bonds formed.

### 🟡 Standard — Full Coverage (1–2d)

#### Worked example: finding the limiting reagent

A student reacts 5.0 g of zinc with 100 mL of 1.00 mol/L hydrochloric acid:

Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂

Step one, put everything in moles. Zinc has a molar mass of 65.4 g/mol, so 5.0 g is 5.0 ÷ 65.4 = 0.076 mol. The acid is 1.00 mol/L × 0.100 L = 0.100 mol.

Step two, compare against the ratio. Every mole of zinc needs two moles of acid, so 0.076 mol of zinc would need 0.153 mol of acid, and only 0.100 mol is available. The acid is limiting.

Step three, the answer follows from the limiting reagent. Each mole of acid gives one mole of hydrogen, so 0.100 mol of acid gives 0.050 mol of H₂, and 0.026 mol of zinc is left over, untouched.

**The mistake to name:** using the reactant with the larger mass, or the one with the smaller molar mass, as the limiting reagent. Mass tells you nothing on its own. Only the mole ratio does.

#### Worked example: dilution and ppm

**Dilution.** 50.0 mL of a 2.00 mol/L sodium hydroxide solution is made up to 250.0 mL. Moles are conserved, so M₁V₁ = M₂V₂: M₂ = (2.00 × 50.0) ÷ 250.0 = 0.400 mol/L.

**Parts per million.** A water sample contains 25 mg of nitrate in 500 mL. That is 50 mg per litre, and for dilute aqueous solutions 1 mg/L equals 1 ppm, so the concentration is 50 ppm.

**The distinction that trips people:** mass percent is grams per 100 g of solution, mass/volume percent is grams per 100 mL, and molarity is moles per litre of solution. If a question gives you a mass and a volume, decide which of these three it wants before you compute.

#### Worked example: pH, and the trap inside the pH

**The straightforward case.** A 0.010 mol/L hydrochloric solution dissociates completely, so [H⁺] = 0.010 mol/L and pH = 2.00.

**The trap case.** A 1.0 × 10⁻⁸ mol/L hydrochloric solution looks like it should have pH 8.00. It does not, because that concentration is below the 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ mol/L hydrogen ions that water produces on its own. Adding water's contribution gives [H⁺] ≈ 1.05 × 10⁻⁷ mol/L, so the pH is about 6.98 — a solution of a strong acid is never basic.

#### Worked example: a buffer and a titration curve

**Buffer.** A solution contains 10 mmol of acetic acid and 5 mmol of sodium acetate. Acetic acid has a pKa of about 4.76, so:

pH = 4.76 + log(5 ÷ 10) = 4.76 − 0.30 = 4.46

**What happens on adding base.** Add 5 mmol of sodium hydroxide: the hydroxide converts 5 mmol of acetic acid into acetate, giving 5 mmol of acid and 10 mmol of acetate, so pH = 4.76 + log(10 ÷ 5) = 5.06. Notice how little the pH moved for a 10 mmol addition, and that is the buffer doing its job.

**Where the buffer runs out.** Add another 5 mmol of hydroxide, a total of 10 mmol: all the acetic acid is converted, and you are at the equivalence point of a weak acid–strong base titration. The solution now contains acetate ions, which hydrolyse to give a basic solution, and the pH sits above 7, around 8.7 for these concentrations.

**Reading the curve instead of computing it.** In a titration of a weak acid with a strong base, the flat region before the steep rise is the buffer region, and the pH at the midpoint of that region equals the pKa. That means you can read an unknown acid's pKa straight off its titration curve at the half-equivalence point. The equivalence point of a weak acid with a strong base is above 7; the equivalence point of a strong acid with a strong base is exactly 7.

#### Bonding and shape, worked

Carbon dioxide has two double bonds around carbon, two electron domains, so it is linear at 180°, and although each C=O bond is polar the molecule is not polar because the dipoles cancel. Water has two bonds and two lone pairs on oxygen, four domains, but the lone pairs push the bonds together to about 104.5°, so water is bent and strongly polar. Methane has four bonding pairs and no lone pairs, so it is tetrahedral and non-polar.

Three checks run through all of these: count the electron domains around the central atom, count the lone pairs separately from the bonds, then decide the shape and whether the dipoles cancel.

#### Energy changes, worked

Breaking a bond always takes energy in; forming a bond always gives energy out, so the enthalpy change of a reaction is the energy needed to break the reactant bonds minus the energy released when the product bonds form. A reaction is exothermic when forming releases more than breaking requires.

In a heating curve, a plateau means a phase change, and during that plateau the added energy is going into breaking intermolecular attractions rather than raising the temperature. That is why melting and boiling are at fixed temperatures and why the steepness of the curve changes with the specific heat capacity.

A catalyst lowers the activation energy by providing an alternative pathway. It changes the rate, not the position of equilibrium, and it does not change the enthalpy change of the reaction.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (3–5d)

#### Energy, entropy and spontaneity

The free energy change links enthalpy to entropy: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, and a process is spontaneous when ΔG is negative.

| ΔH | ΔS | Behaviour as temperature rises |
|---|---|---|
| Negative | Positive | Spontaneous at every temperature |
| Negative | Negative | Spontaneous at low temperature only |
| Positive | Positive | Spontaneous at high temperature only |
| Positive | Negative | Never spontaneous |

Melting ice is the third row: melting is endothermic, and the liquid has more disorder than the solid, so the entropy term has to overcome the enthalpy term, which is why ice melts only above the melting point. Evaporating water is the third row as well, and it needs a higher temperature still because the vapour has far more disorder than the liquid.

#### Common mistakes

Using grams where moles are needed. Every stoichiometry question starts with a conversion, and skipping it is the single most common source of a wrong answer that still looks reasonable.

Treating a reaction as if it must go to completion. Many reactions are reversible, and the equilibrium position depends on temperature, pressure and concentration, not on the stoichiometry alone.

Believing a catalyst shifts the equilibrium. It does not; it gets you to the same equilibrium faster.

Computing the pH of a very dilute strong acid or base without accounting for water's own dissociation, which is what produces the impossible pH 8 for a 10⁻⁸ mol/L acid.

Confusing percentage by mass with percentage by volume, or mass percent with mass fraction, when a solution label gives you one and the question wants the other.

Assuming an ionic compound will dissolve in water. It dissolves if the lattice energy is beaten by hydration energy, which is why sodium chloride dissolves while calcium carbonate barely does.

#### Exam traps

The electronegativity difference rule of thumb for deciding ionic versus covalent bonding is about 1.7. Below it, covalent; above it, ionic. It is a rule of thumb, not a law, which is why the exam sometimes gives you a polar covalent bond with a difference of 0.5 and expects you to say polar covalent.

An atom's ionisation energy is the energy to remove one electron, and successive ionisation energies rise steeply because each electron is removed from a more tightly held shell. A large jump between the second and third ionisation energy means the element has two valence electrons and forms a 2+ ion.

Equal-arm balances measure mass, so they are useless for density comparisons that need volume, and vice versa.

Isotopes have the same number of protons and different numbers of neutrons, so they have the same chemical properties and different mass numbers. Radioactive decay changes the proton number or the neutron number, and it is a change in neutron number that gives an isomer, not an isotope.

#### Contexts the exam puts this in

None of this material arrives as chemistry. It arrives as a fertiliser bag with a percentage composition, a medicine whose dose is per kilogram of body mass, a food label listing additives by name, an environmental report on acid deposition, or a pharmaceutical whose pKa determines how much is absorbed in the stomach versus the intestine. The skill being tested is rarely the chemistry itself; it is recognising which piece of chemistry the context needs and applying it accurately under a time limit.
