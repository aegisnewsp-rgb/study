# Jumbled Sentences

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Jumbled Sentences is the NAT-I (NTS) **Verbal Reasoning** item type where four to six words, phrases, or clauses appear out of logical order, and you choose the option (A–D) that rearranges them into one grammatically correct, meaningfully connected sentence. The task tests **syntactic sequencing**, **connector logic**, **pronoun reference**, and **tense consistency** — not vocabulary memorisation.

| Term | Meaning on this paper |
|---|---|
| Principal clause | The subject + verb pair that stands alone as the backbone of the sentence |
| Logical connector | A word like *because, although, however, therefore* that joins clauses |
| Pronoun antecedent | The noun a pronoun (he / she / it / they) refers back to |
| Discourse marker | A signal word (however, meanwhile, finally) that orders ideas |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"S-V-C-P-T"** — fix the **S**ubject–**V**erb pair first, attach the **C**onnector clause to its target, check **P**ronouns, then verify **T**ense. Anchoring on the verb pair eliminates three of four options within ten seconds.

Solve each item in **45–60 seconds**; skip and return if two pass-choices both look plausible — in most keys only one keeps tense consistent.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Solving Procedure

NAT-I jumbled-sentence items reward a fixed five-step routine. Apply it in order before reading the options.

1. **Spot the verb.** The word that takes a tense and an object is your anchor — in most keys the only verb stem in the list.
2. **Find the subject.** Match it to the verb for number and person (singular *he goes*, plural *they go*).
3. **Park connector words.** Underline *because, although, since, while, therefore, however, moreover* — these are non-negotiable glue.
4. **Glue the principal clause.** Subject + verb + complement must stand alone.
5. **Attach subordinate pieces.** Reason clauses follow what they explain; result clauses precede what they conclude.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Feature | Principal Clause | Subordinate Clause |
|---|---|---|
| Can stand alone? | Yes | No — needs the main clause |
| Typical starters | Noun / Pronoun / -ing phrase | *Because, although, when, if, that* |
| Punctuation | In many papers ends with full stop | Commas on both sides in most keys |
| Role | States the main fact | Adds reason, contrast, time, or condition |

| Connector | Function | Position relative to main clause |
|---|---|---|
| *because / since / as* | Reason | In most keys after |
| *although / though / while* | Contrast | In most keys before or after |
| *therefore / so / hence* | Result | After |
| *however / nevertheless* | Surprise contrast | Beginning, set off by comma |
| *not only…but also* | Addition pair | Fixed pair, never split |

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** Rearrange the words to form a correct sentence.
*A. / the committee / recommended / because the proposal / was feasible / it*
*B. / because the proposal was feasible / it / recommended / the committee*
*C. / the committee recommended it / because / the proposal was feasible*
*D. / the proposal / because it was feasible / the committee recommended*

#### Solution:

1. Principal clause = **the committee recommended** (subject *committee* matches singular verb *recommended*).
2. Subordinate clause = **because the proposal was feasible** (reason connector *because* + its own subject–verb pair).
3. The reason explains *why* the committee recommended → reason clause follows the main clause.
4. Option **C** preserves the order *main clause → because-clause* and keeps pronoun-less brevity.

#### Answer: C

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Options A and D both begin with the committee but misplace *because*. Students rush the opening words and forget that a *because*-clause modifies the verb it sits next to. Always read the connector as a label for the verb immediately preceding or following it.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases That Decide Top Scores

1. **Promotional / academic openings.** When the list begins with *Indeed, Moreover, Therefore, However,* treat the marker as part of the opening phrase — never split it from its subject.
2. **Participial start.** A sentence beginning with *Having / Being / Knowing* requires its subject immediately after, or the participle dangles. Test rearrangement by reading aloud: if the -ing word sounds unattached, the order is wrong.
3. **Correlative pairs.** *Not only…but also, either…or, neither…nor, both…and, so…that, such…that* must travel as fixed pairs. Options that split them by one or two words are distractors.
4. **Punctuation cues.** A fragment set off by commas on both sides is non-essential; if the meaning changes when you remove it, it is restrictive and stays attached to its noun.
5. **Pronoun traps.** When *it / they / he / she* appears in the jumble, ensure the referent is the nearest matching noun — examiners test ambiguous references in roughly one in five items.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. Rearrange: *"the athletes / although they trained daily / failed to qualify / for the championship / because the rules changed"* — identify which clause belongs to *because* and which to *although*, then decide their order around the verb *failed*.
2. Rearrange: *"not only the manager / but also the staff / contributed to / the charity drive"* — confirm that *not only…but also* straddles the two subjects in correct grammatical order.

> 📌 **Exam Strategy:** Verbal Reasoning holds ~3% of the NAT-I paper, with **4–6 jumbled-sentence items** on average across NAT-I 2018–2024 papers. Each carries +1 mark with no negative marking on standalone items, so attempt every one. Spend at most **60 seconds**; if undecided, mark the most connector-faithful option and move on. Strong scores here also lift your performance on **sentence correction** and **ordering of words** in the same section.
