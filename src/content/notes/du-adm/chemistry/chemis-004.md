# Chemical Bonding — DU Unit B Admission (Science)

Chemical Bonding sits at the heart of the DU Unit B Chemistry paper: it is the chapter that ties atomic structure to every reaction mechanism, every physical property (melting point, solubility, conductivity), and every organic reaction the paper later asks about. NCTB HSC Chemistry 1st Paper Chapter 4 is the source scope — ionic, covalent, coordinate, hydrogen and metallic bonds, VSEPR, hybridisation, and the basics of molecular orbital theory for homonuclear diatomics. Three MCQ patterns dominate the paper: (1) classify a given bond as ionic / polar covalent / non-polar covalent from electronegativity difference, (2) predict the geometry and hybridisation of a given central atom using steric number, and (3) decide which intermolecular force explains an observed trend in boiling point or solubility. Get this chapter right and the rest of chemistry (acids-bases, electrochemistry, organic reactions) reads as applications of these rules rather than as separate facts to memorise.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

**The five bond types.**

| Type | Mechanism | Typical participants | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ionic | Electron transfer + Coulomb attraction in a lattice | Metal + non-metal (low χ + high χ) | NaCl, MgO, CaF₂ |
| Covalent | Electron sharing between nuclei | Non-metal + non-metal | H₂, Cl₂, CH₄, H₂O |
| Coordinate (dative) | Both electrons come from one atom (a Lewis base donating to a Lewis acid) | Non-metal + metal ion (or another Lewis acid) | NH₄⁺, H₃O⁺, [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺ |
| Hydrogen | Special dipole-dipole interaction: H bonded to F, O or N attracts a lone pair on a neighbouring F, O or N | N, O, F-bearing molecules | H₂O, HF, NH₃, DNA base pairs |
| Metallic | Cations in a fixed lattice + delocalised "sea" of electrons | Metals and alloys | Fe, Cu, brass, bronze |

**Electronegativity (Pauling scale).** Fluorine is highest at 4.0; caesium is lowest at 0.7. A difference $\Delta\chi \ge 1.7$ is usually classified as ionic, $\Delta\chi$ between 0 and 1.7 is polar covalent, $\Delta\chi = 0$ is non-polar covalent. The cut-off is a soft convention; real bonds sit on a continuum and many (AgCl, HF) are best described as having both ionic and covalent character.

**VSEPR (valence shell electron pair repulsion).** Electron domains (bonding or non-bonding) arrange to maximise their separation. The steric number is the count of all electron domains on the central atom. The most common geometries:

| Steric number | Lone pairs | Molecular geometry | Bond angle | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0 | Linear | 180° | BeCl₂ |
| 3 | 0 | Trigonal planar | 120° | BF₃ |
| 3 | 1 | Bent (V-shape) | < 120° | SO₂ |
| 4 | 0 | Tetrahedral | 109.5° | CH₄ |
| 4 | 1 | Trigonal pyramidal | ~107° | NH₃ |
| 4 | 2 | Bent | ~104.5° | H₂O |
| 5 | 0 | Trigonal bipyramidal | 90°/120° | PCl₅ |
| 6 | 0 | Octahedral | 90° | SF₆ |

A double bond still counts as **one** domain. Lone pairs occupy more angular space than bonding pairs, so they compress the bond angles.

**Hybridisation (central atom).**

| Steric number | Hybridisation | Orbitals used |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | sp | one s + one p |
| 3 | sp² | one s + two p |
| 4 | sp³ | one s + three p |
| 5 | sp³d | one s + three p + one d |
| 6 | sp³d² | one s + three p + two d |

Resonance does not change hybridisation — all six carbons in benzene are sp² regardless of which resonance structure you draw.

**Intermolecular forces (weakest to strongest for similar-sized molecules).**

1. **London dispersion** — present in all molecules; grows with molar mass and surface contact.
2. **Dipole–dipole** — requires a permanent molecular dipole.
3. **Hydrogen bonding** — H bonded to F, O or N interacting with a lone pair on a neighbouring F, O or N.

**Polarity decision rule.** A molecule is polar if (a) it has polar bonds *and* (b) the bond dipoles do not cancel by symmetry. CO₂ has polar bonds but is non-polar overall (linear, dipoles cancel). H₂O has polar bonds and is polar (bent).

**Quick self-check.**

- Can you classify a bond as ionic / polar covalent / non-polar covalent from $\Delta\chi$?
- Can you give the VSEPR geometry and central-atom hybridisation for NH₃, H₂O, BF₃, PCl₅?
- Can you rank the boiling points of HF, HCl, HBr, HI?
- Can you decide whether CH₂Cl₂ and CCl₄ are polar?

### 🟡 Standard — Exam Prep (3d-3w)

#### Ionic bonding and lattice energy

An ionic bond forms when the energy released by lattice formation exceeds the energy cost of ionisation and electron affinity. The Born–Haber cycle partitions the standard enthalpy of formation into five steps: sublimation of the metal, dissociation of the non-metal, ionisation of the metal, electron affinity of the non-metal, lattice formation. The cycle is bookkeeping — the actual reaction is single-step — but it lets you compute the lattice energy $U$, which is otherwise hard to measure directly.

**Lattice energy trends.** Born–Lande theory gives $U \propto q^+ q^- / (r^+ + r^-)$. Within a group of cations with the same charge, $U$ decreases as ionic radius grows (LiF > NaF > KF > RbF > CsF). For the same charge, $U$ increases with charge (NaCl < MgCl₂ < AlCl₃ in principle; the AlCl₃ case is complicated by covalency). Higher charge + smaller radius = stronger lattice = higher melting point, lower solubility (mostly).

**Fajans' rules.** A small, highly charged cation polarises a large, easily-distorted anion, producing partial covalent character. NaCl is mostly ionic; AgCl is significantly covalent because Ag⁺ is a strong polariser. Use Fajans' rules to predict when an "ionic" compound dissolves poorly or has a low melting point — they often have substantial covalent character.

#### Covalent bonding, polarity, resonance, formal charge

A covalent bond is polar if the two atoms differ in electronegativity. The bond dipole moment $\mu = q \cdot d$ (C·m, or Debye: $1\,\text{D} = 3.336 \times 10^{-30}\,\text{C·m}$) is non-zero when $\Delta\chi > 0$.

**Net molecular dipole** is the vector sum of bond dipoles. Symmetric geometries (CO₂ linear, BF₃ trigonal planar, CCl₄ tetrahedral, SF₆ octahedral) cancel bond dipoles and give non-polar molecules despite polar bonds. Asymmetric geometries (H₂O bent, NH₃ pyramidal, CHCl₃) retain a net dipole.

**Resonance.** When a single Lewis structure cannot capture the bonding, draw multiple structures with the same atomic positions and use a double-headed arrow between them. The actual structure is a weighted average. Examples: benzene, carbonate ion CO₃²⁻, nitrate ion NO₃⁻, ozone O₃, acetate CH₃COO⁻. In benzene, all six C–C bonds are identical with length ~140 pm — between a single bond (~154 pm) and double bond (~134 pm). Bond order in benzene is 1.5.

**Formal charge** $= V - N - \tfrac{1}{2}B$, where $V$ is group valence electrons, $N$ is non-bonding electrons, $B$ is bonding electrons. The best Lewis structure minimises formal charges; same-sign charges on adjacent atoms are a red flag; negative formal charge should sit on the more electronegative atom.

**Coordinate (dative) bond.** A covalent bond where one atom contributes both electrons. Examples: NH₄⁺ (N→H⁺), H₃O⁺ (O→H⁺), CO bonding to haemoglobin Fe, and the ligand→metal bonds in complex ions like [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺. Once formed, it is indistinguishable from a regular covalent bond — the label describes how it formed, not its current strength.

#### VSEPR procedure

A reliable procedure for any species:
1. Draw the Lewis structure (assign lone pairs to terminal atoms first, then central).
2. Count steric number: bonded atoms + lone pairs on central atom.
3. Name the molecular geometry from the SN and lone pair count.
4. Predict bond angles — lone pairs compress them.

Example: NO₂⁻ has SN = 3 with one lone pair on N → bent, bond angle near 115°. SO₃ has SN = 3 with no lone pairs on S → trigonal planar, all angles 120°. XeF₄ has SN = 6 with two lone pairs on Xe → square planar (lone pairs occupy opposite positions to minimise repulsion).

A subtle trap: NO₂ (neutral) has an unpaired electron, making it a radical with bond angle ~134°, whereas NO₂⁻ has the lone pair and is bent at ~115°.

#### Hybridisation

Map SN to hybridisation as in the table above. A double bond on the central atom still counts as one VSEPR domain but uses one p orbital. Formaldehyde H₂C=O has SN = 3, central C is sp², and the C=O π bond uses the unhybridised p orbital on C and one on O.

sp³d and sp³d² only appear in period 3 and beyond because the central atom needs accessible d orbitals. Don't try to write sp³d² for carbon.

#### Intermolecular forces in detail

**London dispersion** is always present. Instantaneous dipoles in one molecule induce opposite dipoles in a neighbour; the resulting attractive force scales as $1/r^6$ at short range. Larger, more polarisable molecules have stronger dispersion. This is why I₂ is solid at room temperature while F₂ is a gas, and why long-chain alkanes have higher boiling points than short ones.

**Dipole–dipole** interactions need a permanent molecular dipole. They are a few kJ/mol stronger than dispersion for similar-sized molecules. Acetone ($\mu = 2.88$ D) boils at 56 °C, while the non-polar butane (C₄H₁₀, similar mass) boils at −1 °C.

**Hydrogen bonding** occurs when H is bonded to N, O, or F, and a lone pair on a neighbouring N, O, or F accepts the H. It explains the anomalously high boiling points of H₂O, HF, and NH₃ relative to their heavier congeners (H₂S, HCl, PH₃ boil much lower despite higher molar mass). It also holds DNA strands together (A–T and G–C base pairing) and stabilises protein secondary structure.

**Ion–dipole** forces operate when an ionic compound dissolves: the ions attract the oppositely-charged ends of polar water molecules. Higher charge density on the ion (small + high charge) → stronger ion–dipole → better solubility.

#### Worked example

**Problem.** Rank the boiling points of CH₄, C₂H₆, C₃H₈, and CH₃OH, and explain.

**Solution.** All four are molecular, so boiling points reflect intermolecular forces.

- **CH₄ (16 g/mol)** — non-polar, only London dispersion. Smallest, weakest dispersion → lowest BP (−161 °C).
- **C₂H₆ (30 g/mol)** — non-polar, only dispersion. Larger surface area → stronger dispersion than CH₄ (−88 °C).
- **C₃H₈ (44 g/mol)** — non-polar, only dispersion. Even larger surface area → stronger dispersion (−42 °C).
- **CH₃OH (32 g/mol)** — polar, with O–H capable of hydrogen bonding. H-bonding is much stronger than the dispersion holding propane together, despite methanol being smaller → highest BP (65 °C).

**Order:** CH₄ < C₂H₆ < C₃H₈ < CH₃OH. The reasoning hinges on the *type* of force, not molar mass — methanol has hydrogen bonding, the others do not.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (1mo+)

#### Molecular orbital theory for diatomics

MO theory combines atomic orbitals into bonding and antibonding molecular orbitals that span the whole molecule. For homonuclear diatomics of the second period, the 2p-derived MOs are $\sigma_{2p}$, $\sigma^*_{2p}$, and the degenerate pair $\pi_{2p}$, $\pi^*_{2p}$. The ordering matters: for B₂, C₂, N₂, the $\pi_{2p}$ pair lies *below* $\sigma_{2p}$; for O₂, F₂, Ne₂, the order reverses. The DU paper occasionally asks which ordering is correct for a given molecule.

Configurations (only the 2p electrons matter for these properties):
- **B₂:** $\pi_{2p}^2$; two unpaired electrons → paramagnetic. Bond order = 1.
- **C₂:** $\pi_{2p}^4$; bond order = 2. No unpaired electrons.
- **N₂:** $\pi_{2p}^4 \sigma_{2p}^2$; bond order = 3. This triple bond is the strongest common diatomic bond.
- **O₂:** $\sigma_{2p}^2 \pi_{2p}^4 \pi^{*2}_{2p}$; bond order = 2. Two unpaired electrons in $\pi^*_{2p}$ → **paramagnetic**. MO theory explains this without invoking any excited-state hand-waving; this is one of its most celebrated successes.
- **F₂:** $\pi^{*4}_{2p}$; bond order = 1. Diamagnetic.

Bond order = (bonding electrons − antibonding electrons)/2. Higher bond order → shorter bond length → higher bond energy.

#### Metallic bonding and band theory

A metallic crystal is held together by delocalised electrons moving through the lattice of cations. The **electron sea model** explains malleability, ductility, and high thermal and electrical conductivity: electrons are free to move under an applied field and to accommodate the slip of one plane of cations past another.

**Band theory** sharpens this. In a metal, the valence band (filled) and the conduction band (partially filled, or overlapping with the valence band) are both available for electron motion. In an insulator, the valence band is full and a large band gap ($> 4$ eV) separates it from the next empty band. In a semiconductor, the gap is smaller (~1 eV). Doping a semiconductor — adding P or B to Si — introduces donor or acceptor levels that bridge the gap and dramatically increase conductivity.

For DU Unit B, the qualitative distinction between conductor, insulator, and semiconductor plus the concept of band gap is sufficient. The quantitative treatment of Fermi levels is beyond the HSC scope.

#### Coordinate bonds and complex ions

Transition metals form complex ions with ligands (Lewis bases) donating electron pairs to the metal (Lewis acid). Example: when excess NH₃ is added to Cu²⁺ solution, the deep blue-violet [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺ forms.

**Crystal field theory** splits the d-orbitals in a ligand field. In octahedral geometry: $t_{2g}$ (lower, 3 orbitals) and $e_g$ (higher, 2 orbitals). The splitting energy is $\Delta_{\text{oct}}$. In tetrahedral geometry, the splitting reverses and $\Delta_{\text{tet}} \approx \tfrac{4}{9}\Delta_{\text{oct}}$. Square-planar geometry is favoured for d⁸ systems (Pd²⁺, Pt²⁺, Au³⁺) and gives strong-field splitting.

High-spin vs low-spin configurations depend on $\Delta$ vs pairing energy. This is rarely a DU Unit B MCQ topic, but it can appear in viva for Engineering and Technology applicants.

#### Practice prompts

1. **Geometry.** Predict the geometry, hybridisation and bond angles around the central atom in: (a) ICl₄⁻, (b) ClF₃, (c) XeOF₄, (d) SO₃²⁻.
2. **Polarity.** Without calculating, decide whether each is polar or non-polar, and explain using symmetry: (a) CH₂Cl₂, (b) CCl₄, (c) cis-1,2-dichloroethene, (d) trans-1,2-dichloroethene.
3. **Ranking.** Rank the boiling points of HF, HCl, HBr, and HI, and rank the lattice energies of LiF, NaF, KF, RbF, and CsF. Justify each ranking.
4. **MO theory.** Give the bond order of O₂, O₂⁺, O₂⁻, and O₂²⁻. Which is most stable? Which are paramagnetic and which are diamagnetic?
5. **Resonance.** Draw all resonance structures of CO₃²⁻ and determine the average C–O bond order.

#### Connections to adjacent topics

Chemical bonding is the foundation for **organic chemistry** (every reaction mechanism is a bond break / bond form; hybridisation explains the geometry of every intermediate), for **acids, bases and salts** (electrolyte strength depends on lattice energy vs hydration), for **electrochemistry** (electrode potentials correlate with lattice energies and ion-solvation enthalpies), and for **environmental chemistry** (water's H-bonding explains its solvent properties). Master this chapter and you remove a large fraction of the uncertainty from HSC Chemistry as a whole.

### Common traps

- **Ignoring lone pairs when assigning hybridisation.** NH₃ has SN = 4 (not 3), so it is sp³ (not sp²) and trigonal pyramidal with a bond angle around 107°, not 120°. The same trap catches SO₂ (SN = 3 with one lone pair → sp² bent, not trigonal planar).
- **Conflating bond polarity with molecular polarity.** A molecule with polar bonds can be non-polar overall if the geometry cancels the dipoles. CO₂, BF₃, CCl₄, SF₆ are the classic traps. The paper often tests exactly this distinction.
- **Mixing up hydrogen bonding and covalent bonding.** The H–O, H–N, H–F bonds themselves are polar covalent; the *intermolecular* hydrogen bonding is what raises the boiling point. Saying "water boils high because of strong H–O bonds" loses the mark.
- **Forgetting that VSEPR counts electron domains, not atoms.** A double or triple bond is one domain. Treating the carbon in CO₂ as having SN = 4 (with "two double bonds = four pairs") is wrong — it has SN = 2, sp hybridised, linear.
- **Ranking lattice energy by cation charge only.** Both charge and radius matter. Mg²⁺ vs Na⁺ (Mg²⁺ wins on charge, smaller radius too); Mg²⁺ vs K⁺ (Mg²⁺ wins on charge and smaller radius — K⁺ is larger than Na⁺). The full Born–Lande expression $U \propto q^+q^-/(r^+ + r^-)$ captures it.
- **Assuming hydrogen bonding in any O–H or N–H bond.** Hydrogen bonding requires a *neighbouring* F, O, or N to accept the H. Methanol (CH₃OH) has O–H and shows hydrogen bonding; dimethyl ether (CH₃–O–CH₃) has no O–H and shows only dipole–dipole. Boiling points differ dramatically: 65 °C vs −24 °C.
- **Confusing $sp^3d$ and $sp^3d^2$ availability.** These are only available from period 3. Don't write "the carbon in SF₆ is sp³d²" — that's the sulphur, and the carbon could not have those orbitals.
