# Keyhole Problems

The Perceptual Ability Test is 90 items in the DAT, sitting between the 100-item Survey of the Natural Sciences and the 30-minute scheduled break in a five hour fifteen minute examination. It is the section that most reliably defeats candidates who have prepared everything else, because it is not a knowledge section and cannot be revised in the ordinary way.

Keyhole problems are its signature item type. You are shown a solid made of unit cubes drawn in isometric projection, with a notch or channel cut out of it, and you are asked which of five candidate pieces would fit that channel exactly. The piece is shown as a line drawing, and you have to imagine the volume it occupies.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

**Keyhole problem** — you are shown a solid of unit cubes with a notch cut out of it, and you must pick which of five drawn pieces fits that notch exactly. It is a volume-and-position problem wearing a spatial disguise, and it is settled by arithmetic before it is settled by imagination.

**The two-step method, in this order:**

1. **Count the cubes.** Notch count vs piece count. If they differ, the item is over.
2. **Compare positions.** Only for a piece whose count already matches.

**Memory hook — COUNT then COORDINATE.** C-count the notch, O-only count the piece if the counts match, U-units are unit cubes throughout, N-never skip the invisible cubes, T-then test positions by coordinate. The order is not advice, it is the whole method: the count eliminates roughly a third of the options in seconds and costs nothing when it is wrong, whereas guessing at the picture costs the item.

| Situation | What to do | Time cost |
|---|---|---|
| Notch 16 cubes, piece 14 | Stop — count mismatch | 5 seconds |
| Notch 16 cubes, piece 18 | Stop — count mismatch | 5 seconds |
| Notch 16 cubes, piece 16 | Go to coordinate comparison | 20–30 seconds |
| Piece drawn small, hidden cubes suspected | Count the interior cube first | 10 seconds |
| Three minutes elapsed on one item | Mark and move | — |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

### Do the volume count first, then the shape

Every keyhole item can be settled by arithmetic before you attempt any spatial reasoning, and skipping that step is how candidates burn three minutes on an item that was arithmetically impossible.

Count the cubes in the notch. Count the cubes in the piece, from the drawing. If they differ, the piece cannot fit and the item is over. This eliminates roughly a third of options before any visual work, and it does it in seconds.

The counting has to be honest, which means counting invisible cubes. A solid drawn in isometric shows its exterior only, and the standard trap is a piece drawn so that a cube is hidden behind another. A piece whose visible cubes look like six may well be eight. Look for the interior cube in the drawing and count it.

### Then hold the shape in your head

Work by coordinates rather than by picture. Pick an origin at one corner of the notch and describe the notch as a set of occupied positions in three axes — length, width, height, each a count of unit cubes. The piece is then a second set of occupied positions.

Compare the two sets. A piece fits if and only if its occupied positions are a subset of the notch's positions. This is slower than looking at the picture for an easy item and much faster than looking at the picture for a hard one, because it does not depend on your ability to hold a solid in your imagination while you rotate it.

Most people can rotate a simple shape. Almost nobody can rotate a shape, notice an error, and go back. Coordinates do not have that failure mode.

### Build the practice that works

The skill is procedural, so practice has to be procedural. Thirty items a day, untimed for the first fortnight and timed for the second, is worth more than two hours of reading about spatial reasoning.

Use real DAT items, because the difficulty of the drawings is the difficulty of the test and a beginner book will be easier in ways that do not transfer. The ADA publishes Perceptual Ability Test question instructions, and following the practice items to those instructions is the point.

Sit the section as a block. Once you can do keyhole items one at a time, practice them in runs of 20 with the clock running, because the section has 90 items in 60 minutes and that is roughly 40 seconds each. Speed on keyhole comes from the volume check first and the coordinate comparison second, in that order.

### The hidden-cube rule

A drawing in isometric projection shows three faces of the solid and hides everything behind them. Count the interior cube before you accept a count.

| What the drawing shows | Interior cube present? | True count |
|---|---|---|
| 6 visible cubes in a 2×2×2 arrangement | Yes | 8 |
| 6 visible cubes in a 3×2×1 slab | No | 6 |
| 12 visible in a 3×2×3 block | Yes, 6 hidden | 18 |
| A flat L of 5 squares | No | 5 |

The rule that settles it: a solid is a solid only if some cube is *behind* another from the viewer's position. Count depth rows, not just the visible face. A 2×2×2 cube shows six faces but occupies eight positions.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

### The subset test, written out

Let the notch occupy every position

> **N = {x: 0–3} × {y: 0–1} × {z: 0–1}**  — 4 × 2 × 2 = **16 cubes**

A piece fits if and only if every position it occupies is inside N. Formally, the piece's occupied set **P** must satisfy **P ⊆ N**. Two conditions follow, and candidates who check only the first miss the trap that the section is built on:

1. **Count condition:** |P| = |N| = 16.
2. **Containment condition:** no element of P lies outside N.

A piece can satisfy the count condition and fail containment. That single gap is where most lost keyhole items go.

### Worked item — the piece with the right count and the wrong shape

**The notch** is a rectangular channel: 4 cubes long, 2 wide, 2 high, 16 cubes total.

**The options**, each drawn in isometric and each looking plausible:

- **Piece A** — 3 × 2 × 2 slab. Count = 12.
- **Piece B** — 4 × 2 × 2 slab. Count = 16.
- **Piece C** — a 3 × 2 × 2 block with two cubes added at one end and a two-cube bite taken out of the top at the other. Count = 12 + 2 + 2 = 16.
- **Piece D** — 4 × 2 × 3 block. Count = 24.
- **Piece E** — flat 4 × 2 × 1 sheet. Count = 8.

**Step 1 — count.** Notch is 16.

- A: 12 → eliminate.
- D: 24 → eliminate.
- E: 8 → eliminate.
- B: 16 → survives the count.
- C: 16 → survives the count.

Three options gone in about fifteen seconds, without touching the picture.

**Step 2 — coordinates on the two survivors.**

Piece B occupies {0–3} × {0–1} × {0–1} — identical to N. Every position is inside the notch. **B fits.**

Piece C occupies {1–3} × {0–1} × {0–1} (12 cells) **plus** two cells at x = 4 and two cells at z = 2. All four of those extra cells lie outside N, because N stops at x = 3 and at z = 1. **C does not fit**, despite having exactly the right volume.

> ⚡ **Examiner Trap.** Piece C is the item's whole design. It has the correct cube count, it is drawn so that its added cubes sit at the far end where the perspective compresses them, and its bite is taken from a face the viewer sees edge-on. A candidate who counts and stops selects C. The count test is necessary, not sufficient — the containment test is what the section actually tests, and the piece is drawn specifically to survive the first test and fail the second.

### The rotation trap

Rotating a piece mentally is where most candidates actually lose time, because the failure mode is asymmetric: most people *can* rotate a simple shape correctly, and almost nobody can rotate one, spot that it does not fit, and return to check a second option without having lost track of where they started.

The coordinate method has no such failure mode, because it never asks you to hold an intermediate image. You write two sets of numbers and compare them. The set comparison can be done wrong, but it is wrong *legibly* — you can look at your two rows and see which one is wrong, which is not possible with a shape held in imagination.

### Common mistakes, ranked by cost

| Mistake | Cost per occurrence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Guessing from the visual impression | Whole item, 40+ seconds | Count first, always |
| Ignoring hidden interior cubes | Wrong count, wrong elimination | Count depth rows |
| Count matches, stop and select | Whole item, on the trap piece | Run the containment check |
| Three minutes on one item | Three minutes stolen from later items | Mark and move at the cut-off |
| Mental rotation on a hard piece | Whole item plus recovery time | Coordinates instead |
| Untimed practice only | Section pace collapses under the clock | Runs of 20, clock running |

### ⚡ Quick Exam Checklist

- Notch counted first, before any spatial reasoning
- Interior and hidden cubes included in the piece count
- Count mismatch → item closed, no further time spent
- Count match → coordinates written before selecting
- Containment checked, not just count
- Coordinate sets written legibly, so an error can be found and re-checked
- Unanswered item marked rather than abandoned in place
- Section run as timed blocks of 20, never one item at a time
