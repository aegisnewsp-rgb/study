# Legal Maxims

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **legal maxim** is a short Latin or English phrase condensing a rule of law, doctrine, or principle that judges cite as authority in judgments. CLAT tests roughly **4–6 questions** on maxims inside the Legal Reasoning section, worth about **4%** of the paper's weight.

- **Substantive maxims** govern rights and liabilities (e.g., *Mens Rea*, *Caveat Emptor*, *Pacta Sunt Servanda*).
- **Procedural/evidentiary maxims** govern the conduct of trials and proof (e.g., *Audi Alteram Partem*, *Res Ipsa Loquitur*).
- **Two skills examiners test:** (a) recall the meaning, (b) match the correct maxim to a fact pattern.

**Core maxims in 60 seconds:** *Actus Reus* (guilty act), *Mens Rea* (guilty mind — Section 6 IPC), *Ignorantia Juris Non Excusat* (ignorance of law is no excuse), *Volenti Non Fit Injuria* (to a willing person, no injury), *Res Ipsa Loquitur* (the thing speaks for itself), *Audi Alteram Partem* (hear the other side), *Nemo Judex In Causa Sua* (no one should be a judge in their own cause), *Caveat Emptor* (let the buyer beware), *Ubi Jus Ibi Remedium* (where there is a right, there is a remedy), *Stare Decisis* (stand by decided cases).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### What counts as a legal maxim
A maxim is a **canonical formulation** of a legal principle, usually Latin in common-law systems including India. Judges invoke them as **shorthand authority** in judgments, and statutes sometimes embed them — Section 6 IPC codifies *Actus Non Facit Reum Nisi Mens Sit Rea*, and Section 10 of the Indian Contract Act operationalises free consent, which underwrites *Pacta Sunt Servanda*.

#### Two families of maxims
Substantive maxims create, define, or extinguish legal rights (e.g., *Bona Fide*, *Mala Fide*, *Ejusdem Generis*). Procedural maxims regulate how courts adjudicate — the right to be heard (*Audi Alteram Partem*), the bias rule (*Nemo Judex In Causa Sua*), and evidentiary inference (*Res Ipsa Loquitur*). Confusing the family is the most common source of wrong answers.

#### Mapping high-yield maxims to their function

| Maxim | Literal meaning | Legal function | Typical CLAT scenario |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mens Rea | Guilty mind | Element of criminal liability (S.6 IPC) | Theft, fraud, culpable homicide |
| Res Ipsa Loquitur | The thing speaks for itself | Inference of negligence | Object falling from a building injuring a passer-by |
| Audi Alteram Partem | Hear the other side | Natural-justice rule | Dismissal without notice |
| Caveat Emptor | Let the buyer beware | Duty on buyer to inspect | Purchase of second-hand car |
| Volenti Non Fit Injuria | To a willing person, no injury | Defence in tort | Spectator injured at a boxing match |
| Ignorantia Juris Non Excusat | Ignorance of law is no excuse | Strict liability principle | Person unaware a substance is banned |

#### Statutory anchors you must remember
- *Mens Rea* — Indian Penal Code, **1860**, Section 6.
- Free consent (underwriting *Pacta Sunt Servanda*) — Indian Contract Act, **1872**, Section 10.
- Implied conditions overriding *Caveat Emptor* — Sale of Goods Act, **1930**, Section 14.
- Consumer remedies for defective goods — Consumer Protection Act, **2019**.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Distinction traps that cost marks
Examiners exploit five recurring mis-matches. First, **Mens Rea vs. Actus Reus**: a fact pattern mentioning only the physical act (e.g., "A pushed B") tests *Actus Reus*; adding intent, knowledge, or recklessness introduces *Mens Rea*. Second, **Audi Alteram Partem vs. Nemo Judex In Causa Sua**: the former is the hearing rule, the latter is the bias rule — students often swap definitions. Third, **Caveat Emptor's modern limits**: it no longer governs consumer sales after the Consumer Protection Act 2019 and Section 14 of the Sale of Goods Act 1930. Fourth, **Volenti Non Fit Injuria**: it fails where consent was not free, not informed, or obtained under misrepresentation. Fifth, **Expressio Unius vs. Ejusdem Generis**: the former excludes what is not mentioned; the latter restricts general words to the same class as specific ones already named.

#### Worked application
**Fact pattern:** Ramesh boards a train despite a visible "travel at own risk" board; he is injured when a coach derails. The defence argues *Volenti Non Fit Injuria*. **Correct analysis:** the defence fails because Volenti requires free and voluntary consent to risk, and a *public carrier* owes a non-derogable duty of care under the Railways Act 1989 — a classic trap question.

#### Practice prompts
1. A factory's chimney collapses during a cyclone and injures a worker. Which maxim allows the court to infer negligence without direct proof? (Answer: *Res Ipsa Loquitur*.)
2. A municipal officer cancels a trader's licence without giving a hearing. Which maxim is breached? (Answer: *Audi Alteram Partem*; remedy is *Ubi Jus Ibi Remedium*.)
