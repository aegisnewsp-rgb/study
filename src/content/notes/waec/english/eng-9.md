# Idioms and Figurative Language

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your WAEC paper.

Idioms are fixed expressions whose meaning cannot be inferred from the individual words, e.g. *kick the bucket* = die. Figurative language is the umbrella of devices — **simile, metaphor, personification, hyperbole, litotes, euphemism, oxymoron, irony** — used to go beyond plain prose. A simile uses *like* or *as*; a metaphor states one thing **is** another.

- **Simile:** *as brave as a lion*
- **Metaphor:** *the world is a stage*
- **Personification:** *the wind whispered*

| Figure | Marker word(s) |
| --- | --- |
| Simile | like, as, than, so |
| Metaphor | none (direct equation) |
| Hyperbole | exaggeration marker |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with days to months before the exam.

#### Definitions and Distinctions

**Denotation** is the dictionary meaning; **connotation** is the implied or emotional shade. An **idiom** is a culture-bound phrase whose meaning is fixed by usage, not by grammar — *to bite the bullet*, *spill the beans*, *beat around the bush*. Translating these word-for-word destroys the meaning. **Figurative language** covers all deliberate deviations from literal sense.

#### How Figures Are Formed

A **simile** uses a comparator (*like/as/as if*). A **metaphor** drops the comparator and fuses tenor with vehicle. **Personification** gives human verbs or attributes to objects (*anger boiled over*). **Hyperbole** magnifies; **litotes** softens via negation (*not bad* = good). **Euphemism** disguises unpleasant facts (*passed away* for *died*). **Oxymoron** pairs contradictions (*deafening silence*); **paradox** is a self-contradictory statement that reveals truth. **Irony** signals the opposite of expectation; **sarcasm** is biting irony aimed to wound. **Onomatopoeia** echoes sound (*buzz*, *hiss*). A **pun** exploits double meaning; an **allusion** references a known story, person or event.

#### WAEC Question Patterns

Paper 1 (Objective) tests lexis through identification MCQs: *“The figure of speech in ‘the camel is the ship of the desert’ is…”* Paper 2 (Essay & Comprehension) asks candidates to explain idioms in passages or to use at least two figures in essays. Expect 3–5 items per paper set.

| Figure | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simile | *My love is like a red, red rose* | compares using *like* |
| Metaphor | *Time is a thief* | equates directly |
| Hyperbole | *I have told you a million times* | overstates for emphasis |
| Litotes | *It wasn’t a bad film* | understates via negation |

- Always check for **like/as** before naming a simile.
- Read the **context** before deciding irony vs sarcasm.
- Idioms cannot be decoded by individual word meanings.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Mechanism

**Transferred epithet** shifts a qualifier from its true owner to a nearby noun (*a sleepless night* — the *night* does not literally sleep). **Synaesthesia** mixes senses (*a warm colour*). **Apostrophe** directly addresses an absent or abstract entity (*O Death, where is thy sting?*). These advanced figures appear in WAEC comprehension passages drawn from prose and poetry. Tone shifts created by irony depend on situational context, not grammar — the sentence *“What a wonderful day”* during a storm is ironic only because of the scene, not the syntax.

#### Worked Identification Drill

Sentence: *“The flowers danced in the gentle breeze while sorrow knocked at her door.”*
- *Flowers danced* → **personification** (human verb on plants).
- *Sorrow knocked at her door* → **personification** + **metaphor** (sorrow treated as a visitor).
A WAEC stem may offer three options; choosing *metaphor only* loses the mark because two devices coexist.

#### Common Candidate Errors

1. Marking *“He runs fast like a cheetah”* as metaphor — the *like* makes it a simile.
2. Treating idioms like *“hold your horses”* as literal riding instructions.
3. Confusing paradox with oxymoron — oxymoron is two words; paradox is a whole statement.
4. Misreading sarcasm as sincerity in comprehension passages.

#### Exam Strategy

The 3% weight clusters in Paper 1 (Lexis & Structure, usually 8–10 lexis items) and Paper 2 comprehension (1–2 explanation questions). Budget 30 seconds per lexis MCQ and 2 minutes per idiom explanation. When asked to *“give the meaning of the expression as used in the passage,”* answer in context, never the general dictionary sense.
