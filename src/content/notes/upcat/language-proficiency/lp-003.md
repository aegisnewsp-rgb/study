# Vocabulary from Context and Word Families

The University of the Philippines names Language Proficiency as one of the four UPCAT subtests and tests it in English and Filipino. UP publishes no vocabulary list, no question count, no per-subtest duration and no weightage for it, so there is no official word list to memorise — and anyone selling you "the top 500 UPCAT words" is selling you a guess.

That is good news. Vocabulary items in this subtest rarely ask what a word means in the abstract. They ask what a word means in a particular sentence, which of two near-synonyms the author actually used, or which word could replace the one printed there without changing the sense. All three are answerable by decoding: by building meaning out of prefixes, suffixes and roots, and by reading meaning out of the sentence around the word. Both skills train in weeks, which makes this one of the highest-return topics in the subtest.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

Every unknown word is decodable in four moves, and the order matters.

1. **Read the slot.** What job is the word doing in this sentence — is it naming the actor, describing it, negating something, or stating a degree?
2. **Hunt for a relative.** Look for a family member nearby: the same root with a different affix. *…the claim remained **unverified**; the witnesses were **verifiable**.*
3. **Substitute and test.** Drop the candidate word into the sentence. If the sentence still means the same thing, the candidate is probably right.
4. **Let the affix filter.** The suffix tells you the part of speech and the prefix tells you the direction of the meaning. Most wrong answers fail at this step.

**The prefixes that carry most of the items.**

| Prefix | Usual meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| un-, in-, im-, il-, ir- | not | unfunded, invalid, irregular |
| dis- | not, or apart | disagree, dismantle, discard |
| mis- | wrongly | misread, miscalculate |
| re- | again, back | review, refund, rewrite |
| pre- | before | preview, precaution, pre-test |
| post- | after | postoperative, postpone, postgraduate |
| over-, under- | too much, too little | overestimate, underfund, underestimate |
| inter- | between | intercity, intervene, interdependent |
| sub- | under, below | submerge, subordinate, subway |
| trans- | across | transmit, transport, transplant |
| anti-, counter- | against | antiseptic, counteract |
| co-, com- | together | coexist, combine, commune |

**The suffixes that carry most of the items.**

| Suffix | Part of speech and meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| -tion, -sion, -ment, -ance, -ity | noun of an action or state | reduction, decision, agreement, accuracy |
| -able, -ible | can be done; capable of being | verifiable, readable, defensible |
| -ive | tending to | reactive, selective, instructive |
| -ous, -ful | full of | numerous, careful, harmful |
| -less | without | countless, careless, endless |
| -ly | adverb of manner or degree | rapidly, partially, evidently |
| -ise, -ize | to make or cause to become | analyse, prioritise, sterilise |
| -er, -or | one who or that which | researcher, container, conductor |
| -ist | one who does or believes | chemist, analyst, journalist |
| -ism | a doctrine, system or condition | criticism, metabolism, idealism |
| -logy | the study of | biology, sociology, pathology |
| -itis | inflammation of | arthritis, appendicitis |

**The two affixes worth memorising first** are the negation prefixes and the noun-forming suffixes. Items flip a negation — a claim that is *unverified* against one that is *verified*, a result that is *inconclusive* against one that is *conclusive* — and they ask for the noun form of a verb, because that is a mechanical conversion you can do on the spot.

**What to memorise.**

- The four decoding moves, in order: read the slot, find the relative, substitute, filter by affix.
- The negation prefixes, and the fact that *dis-* and *in-* can also mean "apart", not only "not".
- The noun-forming suffixes, which turn a verb into the thing the verb produces.
- *-less* and *-ful* are opposites, and *un-* plus a word is not the same as *less*: *unhelpful* (not helpful) differs from *helpless* (without help).
- The four registers a passage can be written in, and the clue that tells you which one is in front of you.
- The near-synonym pairs that items use as options: *affect/effect*, *complement/compliment*, *assure/ensure/insure*, *emigrate/immigrate*, *principal/principle*, *farther/further*.
- A handful of Latin and Greek roots with two or three derivatives each.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Read the slot before you read the word

The grammatical job of a word narrows the answer faster than any dictionary would.

| Where the unknown word sits | It is probably | So expect an option that is |
|---|---|---|
| right after a verb, no determiner | an adverb or a direct object | *quickly*, *the results* |
| right after "to" | a verb in its base form | *analyse*, *revisit* |
| right before a noun | an adjective | *marginal*, *preliminary* |
| right after a determiner or adjective | a noun | *methodology*, *assumption* |
| after "of" | a noun | *a finding of fact* |
| after "not", "never", "without" | an adjective or verb | *unverified*, *disagree* |

Read the sentence around the blank as carefully as you read the blank itself. A word that fits the meaning can still be the wrong part of speech, and part of speech is what the options usually separate.

#### Morphology is a decoder, not a memory test

Once you can see that a suffix changes the part of speech, the words start decomposing on sight.

| Base | As a verb | As a noun | As an adjective |
|---|---|---|---|
| verify | verify | verification | verifiable |
| analyse | analyse | analysis | analytical |
| decide | decide | decision | decisive |
| expand | expand | expansion | expansive |
| conclude | conclude | conclusion | conclusive |
| compare | compare | comparison | comparative |
| qualify | qualify | qualification | qualified |

Read across the row and the derivation is obvious. Read down the column and the inflection is obvious. Both are worth having in the same table, because the item that asks for the noun form of *verify* and the item that asks you to distinguish *conclusive* from *conclusion* both come straight out of it.

**Word families** — the same root, different affixes, one shared sense.

| Root | Meaning | Derivatives |
|---|---|---|
| spect, spic | to look | inspect, respect, suspect, spectator, perspective, conspicuous |
| duc, duct | to lead | conduct, introduce, deduct, produce, reduce |
| port | to carry | export, import, transport, portable, support |
| mit, miss | to send | transmit, submit, dismiss, commit, permit |
| scrib, script | to write | describe, prescribe, subscribe, manuscript, transcript |
| fac, fect, fic | to make | manufacture, factory, efficient, affect, effect |
| cred | to believe | credit, credible, incredible, creed |
| vit, viv | life | survive, vivid, vitality, vitamin |
| therm | heat | thermometer, exothermic, endothermic |
| hydr | water | dehydrate, hydrogen, hydrophilic |
| bio | life | biology, biotic, antibiotic, biography |
| geo | earth | geology, geography, geothermal |
| chron | time | chronology, chronic, synchronise |
| struct | to build | construct, structure, infrastructure |
| tract | to pull, draw | attract, contract, extract, tractor |
| ven, vent | to come | intervene, prevent, convention, event |
| cap, cept, cip | to take | capture, accept, receive, susceptible |
| dict | to say | predict, dictate, contradict, verdict |
| log, logue | word, study | dialogue, biology, logic, catalogue |
| path | feeling, disease | pathology, empathetic, symptom |

#### Collocation: the pairing is the word

A definition tells you what a word means. A collocation tells you which words it lives with, and items test the pairing more often than the definition.

| Use this | Not this |
|---|---|
| make a decision | do a decision |
| take a course | do a course |
| draw a conclusion | make a conclusion |
| raise a concern | rise a concern |
| conduct research | do research |
| meet a deadline | reach a deadline |
| comply with the rules | obey to the rules |
| cite a source | refer a source |
| refine a method | refine a methodology |
| address an issue | solve an issue |
| grant permission | grant a permission |
| breach a contract | breach of a contract |

The wrong column is not random. Each wrong phrase keeps the right noun and swaps the verb, or keeps the verb and swaps the preposition, and each one trips a speaker coming from a language that puts the object after a preposition (*do research*, *do a decision*) where the standard phrase uses a verb that carries the sense (*conduct*, *make*). That is how the option was built.

#### Near-synonyms that are not synonyms

| Pair | The distinction |
|---|---|
| affect, effect | *affect* is the verb, to influence; *effect* is the noun, the result. The noun *effect* is also the verb in formal legal and medical writing. |
| assure, ensure, insure | *assure* a person, *ensure* an outcome, *insure* money against loss. |
| complement, compliment | *complement* completes something; *compliment* praises it. |
| emigrate, immigrate | *emigrate* leaves a country, *immigrate* enters one. |
| principal, principle | *principal* is the head of a school or the main part; *principle* is a rule or belief. |
| farther, further | *farther* for physical distance, *further* for additional extent — *further research*. |
| adapt, adopt, adept | *adapt* to change, *adopt* to take up, *adept* to be skilled. |
| imply, infer | *imply* is what the speaker hints; *infer* is what the reader concludes. |
| precede, proceed | *precede* comes before; *proceed* to go on. |
| comprise, compose | *comprise* takes no *of* — a group *comprises* members; *compose* takes an object — the group is *composed of* members |
| continuous, continual | *continuous* without a break; *continual* repeatedly, with breaks. |
| historic, historical | *historic* means important in history; *historical* means relating to the past. |
| economic, economical | *economic* about the economy; *economical* about saving money. |
| classic, classical | *classic* means definitive or typical; *classical* refers to the ancient style or to music. |

#### Register: right sense, wrong voice

An item that offers a word of the right meaning in the wrong register is testing audience, not vocabulary.

| Register | It uses | A clue in the passage |
|---|---|---|
| Academic | *ascertain*, *notwithstanding*, *endeavour* | third person, passive voice, hedged claims |
| Professional | *assay*, *protocol*, *titre*, *correlation* | named methods, instruments, quantities |
| Journalistic | *start*, *try*, *find out*, *because* | direct address, short clauses, contractions |
| Everyday | *get*, *put off*, *figure out* | asides, informal asides, slang |

If the passage is a formal report on a study, "the results were **catastrophic**" may be right and "the results were **lousy**" wrong — not because of the meaning but because of the voice. The same item in a chat message reverses the answer.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Worked item 1 — decoding a word you have never seen

**Passage.** The committee's findings were **equivocal**. Although the trial had been conducted over several years, the reduction in disease incidence was marginal, and the researchers cautioned against drawing firm conclusions from a sample of this size.

**Item.** In the passage, *equivocal* most nearly means:

- **A.** contradictory
- **B.** ambiguous or inconclusive
- **C.** widely criticised
- **D.** carefully measured

**Step 1 — read the slot.** "The committee's findings were ___" wants an adjective describing findings. Option D is an adjective, so it survives the first filter — that is why D is in the list.

**Step 2 — find the relative.** *Equivocal* comes from the Latin *aequus* (equal) and *vocare* (to call), a word that originally meant using one term for more than one thing — which is where "ambiguous" comes from. Its relatives are *equivocate* and *equivoque*.

**Step 3 — test by substitution.** Drop B in: "the findings were ambiguous or inconclusive", and the next sentence — marginal reduction, caution against firm conclusions — follows directly. Drop D in: "the findings were carefully measured", and the researchers' caution becomes odd, because a carefully measured finding does not need a warning about sample size.

**Step 4 — test the trap.** A reads plausible because the passage talks about caution and weak results, but *contradictory* means the findings conflict with one another. The passage never says that; it says they are weak. Weak is not the same as conflicting.

**Answer: B.**

> **Trap.** Choosing A because the passage sounds negative. The stem asks what the word means, and the passage describes a limitation rather than a contradiction. Option A is the most tempting because the surrounding sentences are all about doubt.

#### Worked item 2 — the near-synonym trap

**Passage.** Because the reagent had been stored at the wrong temperature, the reaction did not proceed as expected, and the researchers **repeated** the experiment with fresh stock.

**Item.** Which word best completes the sentence?

- **A.** reiterated
- **B.** repeated
- **C.** duplicated
- **D.** repeatedly

**Step 1 — read the slot.** The subject is people, the object is *the experiment*, and the action is doing it again.

**Step 2 — test word class.** *Reiterate* takes a statement: you reiterate a claim, a point, a warning. An experiment is not a statement, so A is wrong on grammar before sense is considered. *Repeatedly* is an adverb and cannot take the object, so D is ungrammatical in this slot.

**Step 3 — test the meaning.** *Duplicate* means to make an identical copy of something, which is a different act from running it again with new material. *Repeat* means to do again, and the passage supplies the reason for doing it again: the reagent had been stored wrong.

**Step 4 — check the collocation.** *Repeat an experiment* is the standard pairing in a research write-up, and the item is scored on that pairing rather than on which word "sounds most academic".

**Answer: B.**

> **Trap.** Reading the four options as four flavours of "did it again" and picking the most academic-sounding. *Redo* is a real word that would pass a meaning test, and it is still the wrong answer in a formal research passage. On register-sensitive items, two options can share a meaning and only one survives on tone.

#### Worked item 3 — the item with two right meanings

**Passage.** The agency published the findings without an accompanying explanation of its methodology. Readers had no way to judge whether the sample was representative.

**Item.** In the passage, *accompanying* most nearly means:

- **A.** contradicting
- **B.** supplying
- **C.** concealing
- **D.** repeating

**Step 1 — read the slot.** The phrase is "without an accompanying explanation", so the noun is *explanation* and the adjective modifies it. An explanation cannot contradict or conceal — it supplies information. Options A and C fail on the noun they attach to.

**Step 2 — check the passage's logic.** The second sentence says readers could not judge the sample because the methodology was not explained. "Without an accompanying explanation" is exactly that, which confirms B.

**Step 3 — check the trap.** D, *repeating*, is not wrong in the abstract — an accompanying explanation could be a repeat of an earlier one. But the passage's causal link to readers being unable to judge points to missing information, not to repeated information. The passage settles the tie, and that is the whole reason the item is built from a passage rather than a single sentence.

**Answer: B.**

#### The register trap in full

Two options can carry the same sense and only one fits the passage's voice. When that happens, ask three questions in order: **Who is speaking?** **How formal is the setting?** **What would the writer avoid?**

| Situation | The sense the item wants | The word to reject |
|---|---|---|
| A scientific paper reporting an inconclusive trial | "showing no definite result" | "useless", "a disaster" |
| A company memo to staff about a new procedure | "the method must be followed" | "mandatory" in a legal sense, "compulsory" in a regulatory sense |
| A history essay about a turning point | "the event that changed the course" | "revolutionary" applied to a minor reform |
| A news report on a public inquiry | "the officials were questioned" | "interrogated" |

#### Common mistakes

Picking the option that shares a root with a word elsewhere in the passage. A morphological echo is not a meaning match, and options are sometimes built from the passage's own vocabulary precisely to attract you.

Decoding from the prefix alone and stopping. The prefix gives the direction; the suffix gives the part of speech. A word with the wrong suffix is wrong even when the prefix is perfect.

Assuming a prefix always means "not". *Pro-* means forward, *anti-* means against, *dis-* can mean apart, *in-* can mean into, and *counter-* means against. A decode that produces a negative sense from any of these is wrong.

Ignoring the paragraph when the sentence is ambiguous. When two options fit the sentence, the paragraph decides, because the paragraph carries the writer's stance, the referents and the tense.

Choosing the rare word over the plain one. If a plain word is what the passage supports, the plain word is the answer.

Learning word lists instead of roots. A list of two thousand words is a wall; twenty roots that generate two hundred words is a ladder.

### 🎯 High-Yield Topics for UPCAT (UP College Admission Test)

- **Decoding order:** slot, relative, substitution, affix filter — in that order, every time.
- **Negation prefixes:** *un-*, *in-*, *im-*, *il-*, *ir-*, *dis-*, *mis-*; the items flip a negation and the flipped word is usually the answer.
- **Noun-forming suffixes:** *-tion*, *-sion*, *-ment*, *-ance*, *-ity* — the mechanical conversion from verb to noun.
- **Word families:** one root, several affixes, one shared sense — *spect*, *duc*, *port*, *mit*, *scrib*, *fac*, *cred*, *viv*, *therm*, *bio*, *geo*, *chron*, *struct*, *tract*, *ven*, *cap*, *dict*, *log*, *path*.
- **Collocation:** the pairing is what the item tests, so practise the verb-plus-noun pair, not the definition.
- **Near-synonyms:** *affect/effect*, *assure/ensure/insure*, *complement/compliment*, *principal/principle*, *farther/further*, *adapt/adopt/adept*, *imply/infer*, *emigrate/immigrate*.
- **Register:** academic, professional, journalistic and everyday each use a different vocabulary, and the passage tells you which one is present.

### 📝 Previous Year Question Patterns

UP does not release UPCAT papers with answers, so the entries below describe item types rather than reproduce real items.

1. **Word in context:** an unfamiliar word underlined in a passage, with four near-synonym options and one passage-supported reading.
2. **Word replacement:** four candidates for a blank, where only one preserves the sentence's meaning in its paragraph.
3. **Affix decoding:** a word built from an unfamiliar root, where the prefix and suffix together narrow the options to one.
4. **Near-synonym discrimination:** two options of the same broad sense, separated by collocation or register.
5. **Register match:** a formally worded passage where the plain-sounding option is wrong and the technical-sounding option is right.
6. **Sentence-embedded vocabulary:** a word that only makes sense once you have read the two sentences before it.

### 💡 Pro Tips

- Never read an option before you have decided what the word is doing in the sentence.
- Write the root down, then rebuild the word in front of you before you check the options.
- If two options fit, do not choose — go back to the paragraph, because the paragraph is what the item is built on.
- Keep a running list of the words you met in past papers and write the root next to each one; the list compiles itself into families.
- Learn the near-synonym pairs in pairs, never one at a time.
- Check the register of the passage in the first ten seconds, before you fall for the informal option.
