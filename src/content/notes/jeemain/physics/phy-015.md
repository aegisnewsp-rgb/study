# Electrostatics

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Electrostatics studies **electric charges at rest**, the **Coulomb force** between them, and the resulting **electric field** and **electric potential**. JEE Main tests this unit through 1–2 questions per shift (≈5% weightage), almost always numerical or formula-direct.

| Term | SI Unit | Dimensional Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Charge (Q) | coulomb (C) | A·T |
| Electric field (E) | N/C or V/m | M·L·T⁻³·A⁻¹ |
| Electric potential (V) | volt (V) | M·L²·T⁻³·A⁻¹ |
| Capacitance (C) | farad (F) | M⁻¹·L⁻²·T⁴·A² |
| Permittivity (ε₀) | C²·N⁻¹·m⁻² | M⁻¹·L⁻³·T⁴·A² |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"E is Force, V is Work"** — E = F/q (force per unit positive charge, vector), V = W/q (work per unit charge, scalar). Remember that 1 V/m = 1 N/C, so the units collapse the same way the formulas do.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Coulomb's Law and Superposition

Two point charges exert a mutual force given by **Coulomb's law**:

$$F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}$$

where F is in newtons, q₁ and q₂ in coulombs, r in metres, and ε₀ = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² C²·N⁻¹·m⁻². The **superposition principle** allows you to add forces from multiple charges vectorially, but potentials add algebraically (scalars).

In a uniform dielectric medium of constant k, replace ε₀ by ε = kε₀ — the force reduces by a factor k.

#### Electric Field, Potential, and Gauss's Law

The **electric field** from a point charge q is

$$E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{q}{r^2}\,\hat{r} \quad (\text{V/m})$$

The **electric potential** (scalar) from the same charge is

$$V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{q}{r} \quad (\text{V})$$

The two are linked by E = −dV/dr along a field line. **Equipotential surfaces** are always perpendicular to field lines, and moving a charge along one requires zero work.

For symmetric charge distributions, apply **Gauss's law**:

$$\Phi = \oint \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}$$

| Configuration | Field outside | Field inside |
|---|---|---|
| Uniformly charged sphere | E ∝ 1/r² | E = 0 if conducting, E ∝ r if non-conducting |
| Infinite line charge (λ) | E = λ/(2πε₀r) | N/A |
| Infinite plane sheet (σ) | E = σ/(2ε₀) | Field is uniform, pointing away from sheet |

#### Capacitors and Dielectrics

A capacitor stores energy in its electric field. For a **parallel-plate capacitor**:

$$C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}$$

where A is plate area (m²) and d is plate separation (m). Inserting a dielectric of constant k multiplies the capacitance by k and reduces the internal field to E/k.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** A parallel-plate capacitor of capacitance 6 μF is connected to a 12 V battery. After disconnecting the battery, a dielectric slab of constant k = 2 is inserted, filling the gap completely. Find (i) the new capacitance, (ii) the new voltage across the plates, and (iii) the change in stored energy.

#### Solution:

1. Initial charge: Q = CV = (6 × 10⁻⁶)(12) = **72 × 10⁻⁶ C = 72 μC**.
2. New capacitance: C′ = kC = 2 × 6 = **12 μF**.
3. Since the battery is disconnected, Q remains 72 μC.
4. New voltage: V′ = Q/C′ = 72 μC / 12 μF = **6 V**.
5. Initial energy: Uᵢ = ½ CV² = ½ (6 × 10⁻⁶)(144) = **432 × 10⁻⁶ J = 432 μJ**.
6. Final energy: Uբ = Q²/(2C′) = (72 × 10⁻⁶)² / (2 × 12 × 10⁻⁶) = **216 × 10⁻⁶ J = 216 μJ**.
7. Change: ΔU = 216 − 432 = **−216 μJ** (energy decreases; the dielectric is pulled in, doing positive work).

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students plug k into the voltage (writing V′ = kV = 24 V) instead of the capacitance. With the battery disconnected, charge is conserved — only C changes. The voltage drops, not rises. A similar trap appears with k/2 inserted half-way: use k = 1 + (kᵣ − 1)·(fraction filled) for the slab partially filling the gap.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Limit Behaviour

- **Grounded conducting sphere** of radius R placed near a point charge q: induced charge is generally **not** equal to −q in magnitude except in the special case where the sphere encloses q as an internal cavity charge.
- **Two unlike charges far apart** look like a dipole, and V along the axial line varies as p cosθ / (4πε₀r²), while the transverse line gives p sinθ / (4πε₀r²) for the field.
- **Dielectric breakdown**: above ~3 × 10⁶ V/m in air, k loses meaning — the medium ionises, and the capacitor effectively becomes a conductor.

#### Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

1. **Using ε₀ inside a dielectric**: replace with ε = kε₀ for forces, fields, and capacitance; treat ε₀ as a vacuum-only constant.
2. **Series vs parallel capacitor combinations**: in series, 1/C_eq = Σ(1/Cᵢ) and charge is identical on each; in parallel, C_eq = ΣCᵢ and voltage is identical across each.
3. **Sign in W = qΔV**: the work done by an external agent in moving a charge slowly from point A to B is W_ext = q(V_B − V_A). A positive charge spontaneously moves from high V to low V; a negative charge moves the opposite way.
4. **Direction of induced charge on a grounded sphere**: equal-and-opposite to the inducing charge only when the point charge sits *outside* and the induced value equals −q·(R/x), where x is the distance from the charge to the centre.
5. **Potential is not zero inside a conductor**: only the *field* is zero. The whole conductor is a single equipotential body.

#### Practice Prompts

1. **Numerical**: Three charges +q, +2q, −3q are placed at the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side a. Compute the net force on the +2q charge in vector form and the potential energy of the system.
2. **Conceptual**: A metal sphere of radius R carries charge Q. A concentric thin metal shell of radius 2R is grounded. Derive the charge that flows to ground and the final potential of the inner sphere.
