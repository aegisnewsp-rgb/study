# III. Metals — HKDSE Chemistry Notes

This topic covers Topic III of the Compulsory Part of HKDSE Chemistry. It addresses the occurrence of metals in the Earth; the reactivity series of metals; extraction of metals from their ores (iron, aluminium); the corrosion of metals and its prevention; and the uses of common alloys.

---

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Occurrence**: most metals occur as compounds (oxides, sulfides, carbonates) in ores. Few metals occur native (gold, platinum, copper sometimes, silver sometimes).
- **Reactivity series** (most to least reactive): potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, carbon (used for reduction), zinc, iron, tin, lead, copper, silver, gold. The series predicts displacement reactions, extraction methods and corrosion behaviour.
- **Extraction**: potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium — extracted by electrolysis (too reactive for carbon reduction). Zinc to iron — extracted by reduction with carbon. Copper, silver, gold — found native or extracted by physical methods.
- **Iron extraction**: iron ore (mainly Fe₂O₃) is reduced by carbon monoxide in a blast furnace. Coke (carbon) burns to produce CO, which reduces the iron oxide.
- **Aluminium extraction**: aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃ from bauxite) is dissolved in molten cryolite (Na₃AlF₆) and electrolysed. The Hall-Héroult process.
- **Corrosion**: the reaction of a metal with oxygen and water in the atmosphere. Rusting of iron requires both oxygen and water. Prevention: barrier protection (paint, oil, plastic coating); galvanising (zinc coating); sacrificial protection (more reactive metal attached to iron); alloying (stainless steel).
- **Alloys**: mixtures of metals (or a metal with a non-metal). Properties differ from pure metals — typically harder, stronger, less malleable. Examples: brass (copper + zinc), bronze (copper + tin), stainless steel (iron + chromium + nickel), solder (tin + lead).

#### Examiner traps

- Treating aluminium as low reactivity because it doesn't corrode easily — aluminium is reactive but forms a protective oxide layer.
- Confusing galvanising (zinc coating) with electroplating — galvanising uses hot-dip coating with zinc.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Reactivity series and displacement reactions

The reactivity series lists metals in order of their tendency to lose electrons (form cations). A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from a solution of its salt.

Example: zinc + copper(II) sulfate → zinc sulfate + copper. Zinc is more reactive than copper, so zinc displaces copper from solution. This is also the basis of sacrificial protection (zinc protects iron from rusting).

#### Extraction of metals

**Iron extraction (blast furnace)**:

1. **Charging**: iron ore (Fe₂O₃), coke (C) and limestone (CaCO₃) are charged into the top of the furnace.
2. **Coke combustion**: C + O₂ → CO₂; CO₂ + C → 2CO (carbon monoxide is the reducing agent).
3. **Reduction**: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂.
4. **Slag formation**: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂; CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃ (slag, removed from the bottom).
5. **Pig iron**: the molten iron collected at the bottom contains ~4% carbon (and other impurities). It is converted to steel by the basic oxygen process or electric arc furnace.

**Aluminium extraction (Hall-Héroult process)**:

1. **Bayer process**: bauxite ore is digested with sodium hydroxide to produce pure aluminium oxide (alumina, Al₂O₃).
2. **Hall-Héroult process**: alumina is dissolved in molten cryolite (Na₃AlF₆) at ~960°C to lower the melting point. The mixture is electrolysed with carbon anodes and a carbon-lined steel cathode.
3. **Cathode reaction**: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al (liquid aluminium sinks to the bottom and is tapped off).
4. **Anode reaction**: 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ (the oxygen reacts with the carbon anode to produce CO₂).

#### Corrosion and prevention

**Rusting of iron**: 4Fe + 3O₂ + 2xH₂O → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O. Requires both oxygen and water.

**Prevention methods**:
- **Barrier protection**: paint, oil, plastic coating, tin plating, chromium plating.
- **Galvanising**: hot-dip coating with zinc. Even if scratched, zinc continues to protect the iron (sacrificial).
- **Cathodic protection**: connect the iron to a more reactive metal (e.g. magnesium) which corrodes preferentially.
- **Alloying**: stainless steel (iron + chromium + nickel) resists corrosion because chromium forms a passive oxide layer.

#### Alloys

| Alloy | Composition | Properties | Uses |
|-------|-------------|------------|------|
| Brass | Cu + Zn (20-45%) | Harder than copper, malleable | Musical instruments, fittings |
| Bronze | Cu + Sn (10-15%) | Hard, low friction | Bearings, sculptures |
| Stainless steel | Fe + Cr (≥10.5%) + Ni | Corrosion resistant | Cutlery, surgical instruments |
| Solder | Sn + Pb (or Sn + Ag) | Low melting point | Joining metals in electronics |
| Duralumin | Al + Cu + Mg + Mn | Light, strong | Aircraft frames |

#### Exam technique

- **For extraction questions**: state the method (electrolysis or reduction with carbon), give the equations, and explain why the method is chosen based on the reactivity series.
- **For corrosion questions**: state the conditions needed (oxygen and water for iron rusting) and the prevention method.
- **For alloy questions**: state the composition, the property change from the pure metal, and a specific application.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Stainless steel doesn't rust." Stainless steel is much more corrosion-resistant than carbon steel, but it can still corrode under certain conditions (e.g. chloride exposure).
- "Galvanising is just a paint coating." Galvanising is a metallic coating of zinc, which provides sacrificial protection even when scratched.
- "All metals are magnetic." Only iron, cobalt, nickel and their alloys (ferromagnetic) are strongly magnetic. Aluminium and copper are not.
