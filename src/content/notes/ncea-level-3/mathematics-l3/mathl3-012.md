# Evaluate statistically based reports (91584) — NCEA Level 3 Calculus Notes

Achievement Standard 91584 is one of six external NCEA Level 3 Calculus standards, carrying 4 credits. It tests critical evaluation of a statistical report with formal methods: study design, sampling, statistical techniques, assumptions, conclusions, and limitations. The standard deepens the Level 2 work in 91266 by requiring engagement with formal inference (confidence intervals, hypothesis tests).

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **4 credits**, external NZQA examination at the end of Year 13.
- Topics: critical evaluation of published statistical reports; study design (observational vs experimental); sampling; statistical techniques (confidence intervals, hypothesis tests); assumptions; validity of conclusions; limitations.
- **Merit** requires relational thinking — linking the methodology to the conclusion's validity.
- **Excellence** requires extended abstract thinking — comparing alternative methodologies, reflecting on the broader implications.

#### Examiner traps

- Accepting the report at face value — the whole point is critical evaluation.
- Confusing statistical significance with practical significance.
- Skipping the assumptions of the statistical methods used.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Study design

Evaluate the study design:

- **Observational study:** the researcher observes subjects without manipulating variables. Cannot establish causation, only association.
- **Experimental study:** the researcher manipulates variables (with randomisation) to establish causation.
- **Quasi-experimental:** the researcher manipulates variables but without randomisation. Weaker than experimental for causation.

For Excellence, the report should be classified by study design, and the strengths/weaknesses noted.

#### Sampling

- **Random sample:** every member of the population has an equal chance of selection. Reduces selection bias.
- **Stratified sample:** the population is divided into strata and a random sample is taken from each. Ensures subgroup representation.
- **Convenience sample:** whoever is easy to reach. Biased.
- **Volunteer sample:** whoever responds. Biased.

Sample size: larger is better for precision, but practical constraints apply.

#### Statistical techniques

Evaluate whether the statistical techniques used are appropriate:

- Were the assumptions of the tests met (e.g., normality for t-tests, independence of observations)?
- Were confidence intervals reported alongside point estimates?
- Were p-values correctly interpreted?
- Was the multiple testing problem addressed (if many tests were performed)?

#### Common pitfalls in statistical reports

- **p-hacking:** performing many tests and reporting only the significant ones.
- **HARKing:** Hypothesising After Results are Known.
- **Data dredging:** searching for patterns in large datasets without a priori hypotheses.
- **Confirmation bias:** interpreting ambiguous results to confirm prior beliefs.

A good report acknowledges these risks and uses methods to mitigate them (pre-registration, correction for multiple testing, etc.).

#### Validity of conclusions

The conclusions should:

- Be supported by the data.
- Be limited to the population from which the sample was drawn.
- Acknowledge limitations (sample size, methodology, generalisability).
- Be presented with appropriate uncertainty (confidence intervals, prediction intervals).

#### Limitations

A good report includes a limitations section that acknowledges:

- Sample size and statistical power.
- Sampling method and potential biases.
- Measurement validity and reliability.
- External validity (generalisability to other populations).

#### Worked evaluation

Consider a report claiming "Drinking 3 cups of coffee per day reduces heart disease risk by 25%."

Possible evaluation:

- The study was observational, so causation cannot be established (coffee drinkers might differ from non-coffee drinkers in other ways).
- The 25% reduction is a relative reduction; the absolute reduction might be small.
- The confidence interval for the 25% figure was wide, suggesting imprecise estimate.
- The study was funded by a coffee industry group, raising concerns about bias.
- The sample was from one country, limiting generalisability.

Conclusion: the report suggests an association but does not establish causation; further research (ideally RCT) is needed.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Statistical significance vs practical significance

A statistically significant result is one with p < 0.05 (or other threshold). A practically significant result is one with a meaningful effect size.

A small effect can be statistically significant with a large sample (e.g., 1 mmHg reduction in blood pressure). A large effect can be non-significant with a small sample.

For Excellence, the evaluation should distinguish between statistical and practical significance.

#### Internal vs external validity

- **Internal validity:** the study accurately measures what it claims to measure within the study sample.
- **External validity:** the study results generalise to other populations and settings.

A study can have high internal validity but low external validity (e.g., tightly controlled but on a non-representative sample).

#### Bayesian vs frequentist inference

- **Frequentist:** p-values, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests. The dominant paradigm in most published reports.
- **Bayesian:** posterior probabilities, credible intervals, Bayes factors. Less common but gaining ground.

For Excellence, briefly discuss which paradigm the report uses and what the limitations are.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Any published report is credible." No — evaluate the source, methodology, and conclusions.
- "Statistical significance means the effect is real." Not necessarily — p < 0.05 means the effect is unlikely under H₀, but the effect could still be due to chance (5% of the time).
- "A larger sample is always better." Larger samples reduce sampling error but do not fix biases.
- "Replication confirms the result." Replication by independent researchers is the gold standard for confirmation.
