# Consumer Protection Act & Patient Rights

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

The Consumer Protection Act 68 of 2008 (CPA) treats every patient who receives a dispensed medicine or pharmacy service as a **consumer**, and every pharmacist as a **supplier**. Section 61 imposes **strict (no-fault) product liability**, so harm caused wholly or partly by a defective medicine triggers liability without proof of negligence. Section 53 guarantees an implied **warranty of quality**: medicines must be safe, of merchantable quality, and fit for purpose.

- **Eight statutory rights** include the right to fair value (s. 20), disclosure of information (s. 41–44), and protection against unfair contract terms (s. 48).
- **Burden shift:** once the patient shows harm, the supplier must prove the product was not defective.
- **Dual forum:** complaints can go to the National Consumer Commission, provincial consumer courts, or the SAPC.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Scope of the CPA in Pharmacy Practice

The CPA applies to every transaction for goods or services in South Africa unless expressly exempted. Dispensing a Schedule 1–6 medicine is simultaneously the supply of **goods** (the medicine itself) and a **service** (the act of dispensing, counselling, and record-keeping). Both fall within the Act. A patient who purchases an OTC analgesic or fills a chronic prescription therefore has the same statutory remedies as any retail buyer.

#### Strict Liability Versus Common-Law Negligence

Section 61 creates a separate cause of action from common-law **delict**. The patient need not prove the pharmacist was negligent, only that:

1. The product was supplied by the respondent.
2. The product was defective.
3. The defect caused harm (illness, injury, death, or property damage).

Once these three are shown, the burden shifts to the pharmacist. The defence of "I followed the SOP" is not, by itself, a defence under s. 61 — it is relevant only to common-law negligence.

#### Comparison of CPA Liability Frameworks

| Feature | Common-law negligence | CPA s. 61 strict liability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basis | Fault (reasonable standard of care) | Defective product, regardless of fault |
| Burden of proof | Patient proves negligence | Patient proves defect + harm; supplier then disproves |
| Defences available | Contributory negligence, reasonable care | State-of-the-art, inherent risk, compliance with standards |
| Typical damages | patrimonial, non-patrimonial, loss of amenities | Same, but broader scope |

#### Information, Consent, and Contract Terms

Sections 41–44 require the supplier to disclose pricing, composition, risks, and side-effects in plain language. This obligation mirrors and strengthens the **National Health Act** duty to obtain informed consent. Section 48 voids unfair terms, which catches any waiver that purports to strip a patient of CPA remedies.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Worked Scenario

A patient collects a dispensed Schedule 2 antihistamine. Two days later she is hospitalised with a severe adverse reaction traced to a manufacturing contamination. Under the CPA she does **not** need to prove the pharmacist dispensed negligently — only that the product was defective (s. 53) and caused harm (s. 61). The pharmacist must then prove the medicine left the pharmacy in a non-defective state. A parallel complaint to the **SAPC** can proceed on grounds of unprofessional conduct; a complaint to the **NCC** or provincial consumer court proceeds on statutory liability. Both forums can be pursued concurrently.

#### Edge Cases and Traps

- **Combination products** (e.g., a compounded steroid cream): the compounded item is still "goods" under the CPA, and the pharmacist bears s. 61 risk even if the bulk API came from a reputable wholesaler.
- **Implied warranty of quality (s. 53)** is breached where a dispensed medicine has expired stock or improper storage — the pharmacy's supply chain matters.
- **Cooling-off period** (s. 16) in most cases does **not** apply to medicines once dispensed, because they cannot be re-sold safely, but it does apply to gym contracts, loyalty programmes, and similar pharmacy-adjacent services.
- **Section 48 unfair terms** capture clauses limiting liability in medicine-returns policies and lay-bys that exclude refund remedies.

#### Practice Prompts

1. A patient sues for harm from a dispensed medicine. Identify the three elements she must prove under CPA s. 61, and explain why the pharmacist's defence fails if they only show adherence to SOPs.
2. Differentiate between the National Consumer Commission complaint pathway and the SAPC Pharmacy Council pathway for the same dispensing error.
