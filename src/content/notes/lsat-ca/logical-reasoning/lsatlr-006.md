# Method of Reasoning and Role of a Statement — LSAT Logical Reasoning

Method of reasoning items ask the test-taker to describe how the author argues. Role of a statement items ask what function a specific statement serves in the argument. Both item types test the ability to step back from the argument's content and analyse its structure.

> Verify the live section count, item count, and any in-year changes on https://www.lsac.org/ before planning around the figures below.

---

### Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

#### What method items test

- The structure of the argument (analogy, causation, generalisation, etc.).
- The way the author supports the conclusion (evidence, counterargument, concession, etc.).
- The relationship between parts of the argument.

#### What role items test

- The function of a specific statement within the argument.
- Common roles: premise, conclusion, counterexample, evidence, analogy, concession, refutation, background.

#### One-pass drill

1. Identify the conclusion and the premises.
2. Identify the structure of the argument (deductive, inductive, analogical, causal).
3. For method items, describe the structure in standard LSAT language.
4. For role items, identify what the specific statement contributes to the argument.

#### Examiner traps

- Naming the topic instead of the method. Method items ask about how the author argues, not what they argue about.
- Confusing premise with conclusion. The conclusion is the claim the premises support.
- Missing a counterargument. Some arguments include a counter-argument and response.

### Standard — Regular Study (2d-2mo)

#### Common method structures

- **Argument by analogy:** the author compares two cases and concludes that what is true of one is true of the other.
- **Argument by example:** the author cites specific instances to support a general claim.
- **Argument by generalisation:** the author draws a broad conclusion from specific instances.
- **Argument by causation:** the author argues that one event causes another.
- **Argument by sign:** the author argues that a specific observation indicates a broader phenomenon.
- **Argument from authority:** the author cites an expert or authority to support the claim.
- **Argument by elimination:** the author rules out alternatives to support the conclusion.

#### Common roles

- **Premise:** a reason offered in support of the conclusion.
- **Conclusion:** the main claim of the argument.
- **Counterexample:** an instance that contradicts a general claim.
- **Counterargument:** an argument against the author's position.
- **Concession:** a statement that acknowledges the opposing position.
- **Refutation:** a statement that undermines the counterargument.
- **Background:** information that sets the context but does not directly support the conclusion.
- **Analogy:** a comparison that supports the conclusion by similarity.

#### Practice routine

- Take 10 method items. For each, describe the argument's structure in one sentence before evaluating the choices.
- Take 10 role items. For each, identify the statement's function before evaluating the choices.
- Time yourself: under 1.5 minutes per item.

### Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo-6mo)

#### How method and role items connect to the other LR skills

- **Assumption items** ask for the missing link; method items ask how the link is built.
- **Flaw items** ask what is wrong with the structure; method items ask what the structure is.
- **Parallel reasoning items** ask for an argument with the same structure; method items identify the structure to be matched.

#### Common errors and the correction

- **"Method is the same as content."** Method is about how the author argues; content is about what the author argues.
- **"A premise supports the conclusion."** A premise is offered in support of the conclusion, but a strong argument may include premises that are themselves questionable.
- **"Concession agrees with the author."** Concession acknowledges the opposing position; the author may still disagree.

#### Exam technique

- For method items, identify the structure (analogy, causation, generalisation) before evaluating the choices.
- For role items, identify the function of the specific statement (premise, counterexample, concession).
