# Question Tags and Short Responses

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **question tag** is a short added question (auxiliary + pronoun) that turns a statement into a confirmation request, while a **short response** is a brief echo reply (Yes/No, So…, Neither…) that picks up the auxiliary from the prompt.

- **Rule of polarity:** positive statement → negative tag; negative statement → positive tag. *She came, didn't she?* / *She didn't come, did she?*
- **Subject pronoun in the tag must match the statement's subject:** *The boys left, didn't they?*
- **Special forms to memorise:** *I am late, aren't I?* · *Let's dance, shall we?* · *Nobody phoned, did they?*
- **Short responses repeat the auxiliary:** —*Has he arrived?* —*Yes, he has.* / *No, he hasn't.*
- **So + aux + subject** agrees with a positive prior clause; **Neither/Nor + aux + subject** agrees with a negative one.
- NECO traps: replying "Yes" to a negative question as if it were positive, and using *So do I* after a negative.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Anatomy of a Tag
A question tag has three parts: an **auxiliary or modal verb** (be, have, do, will, can, etc.), a **personal pronoun** echoing the subject, and a **question mark**. The verb and tense of the tag must mirror those of the main clause. *He has finished, hasn't he?* — the *has* in the tag is present-perfect, matching *has finished*. When the main clause carries no auxiliary, insert *do/does/did* according to tense and number. *You understood the poem, didn't you?*

#### Polarity Rule
The tag is **opposite** in polarity to the statement. *Bola is a doctor, isn't he?* A negative statement (with *not, never, hardly, seldom*) takes a positive tag: *She never lies, does she?*

#### Pronoun Reference
The pronoun refers to the grammatical subject, not the nearest noun. *The news is bad, isn't it?* (not *isn't the news?*). Indefinite pronouns such as *everyone, somebody, nobody* take **they**: *Everyone agreed, didn't they?*

#### Short Responses (Yes/No)
The reply uses the same auxiliary/modal as the question or statement, then a contracted pronoun. —*Will they win?* —*Yes, they will.* / —*Did she call?* —*No, she didn't.*

#### So / Neither Echo Responses
- **So + auxiliary + subject** = agreement with an *affirmative* prior statement: *I love jollof rice.* → *So do I.*
- **Neither / Nor + auxiliary + subject** = agreement with a *negative* prior statement: *He can't swim.* → *Neither can I.*
The auxiliary is drawn from the preceding clause, so it can be *am, is, was, have, will, can,* etc., never *do* unless the original clause used *do*.

#### Exam Question Pattern
NECO SSCE English Paper II (Objectives) usually tests this topic through **gap-filling options** such as: *The teacher has gone out, _____?* with options *(A) hasn't he (B) isn't he (C) hasn't it (D) did he*. Get the polarity and pronoun right first; the auxiliary is the last check.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Irregular Forms
Some subject–verb combinations force fixed tags that students must internalise. *I am* always becomes *aren't I* (no *amn't I* in Standard English). *Let's…* invites the listener with *shall we?* and never *will we?*. *Imperatives* without *let's* use *will you / won't you / can you / could you*: *Open the door, will you?* After *there is / there are*, the tag uses *there*: *There is a problem, isn't there?*

#### Reversed Meaning on Negative Questions
When the prompt is itself a negative question, *Yes* and *No* answer the question, not agree with its polarity. —*Don't you like beans?* —*Yes, I do* (I like beans). —*No, I don't* (I dislike beans). Treating "Yes" as agreement with the word *don't* reverses your intended meaning — a classic NECO MCQ trap.

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics
Question tags and short responses drill the same auxiliaries tested in **concord**, **tense**, and **modal usage**. Mastering them reinforces subject–verb agreement with collective nouns (*The team is…, aren't they?*) and the sequence of tenses in reported speech.

#### Common Mistakes
1. **Polarity slip:** *She is happy, is she?* → correct: *isn't she?*
2. **Auxiliary drift:** inserting *do/does/did* where a modal already exists: *He can drive, doesn't he?* → *can't he?*
3. **Wrong echo auxiliary in So/Neither:** *She has finished.* → *So have I* (not *So do I*).
4. **Pronoun mismatch:** *Mary and John are here, isn't he?* → *aren't they?*

#### Worked Examples
- *The children have eaten, _____?* Tag requires present-perfect *have* and plural pronoun: **haven't they?**
- *I am next, _____?* — fixed form: **aren't I?**
- *He doesn't smoke, _____?* — positive tag with the *does*-auxiliary echoed: **does he?**
- *Bisi can sing. _____?* Echo: **So can Adaeze / Can she? (echo question).**

#### Practice Prompts
1. Supply the correct tag: *Hardly anybody came early, _____?*
2. Complete the response: *Tunde won't be at the party.* — *_____ will I.*
