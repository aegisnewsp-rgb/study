# Electrochemistry

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Electrochemistry** links electrical energy to **redox** reactions. Oxidation = loss of electrons at the **anode**; reduction = gain of electrons at the **cathode** (**OIL RIG**).

Key formulas to memorise:
- **E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode**
- **m = (M × I × t) / (n × F)**, where m = mass liberated (g), M = molar mass, I = current (A), t = time (s), n = electrons transferred, **F = 96,500 C mol⁻¹**.
- **Q = I × t**

NABTEB pointers: (1) A **galvanic cell** runs spontaneously and produces current; an **electrolytic cell** needs an external battery. (2) **Electrode polarity flips** between the two — galvanic anode is negative, electrolytic anode is positive. (3) The **electrochemical series** decides which ion gets discharged first.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Cell types and electrode polarity

In a **galvanic (voltaic) cell**, a spontaneous redox reaction converts chemical energy into electrical energy. The half-cell with the more negative reduction potential acts as the **anode (negative terminal)** and is oxidised; the half-cell with the more positive E° acts as the **cathode (positive terminal)** and is reduced. Electrons flow externally from anode to cathode.

In an **electrolytic cell**, an external DC source forces a non-spontaneous reaction. Here the anode is connected to the positive terminal of the battery and is still the site of oxidation, while the cathode is negative and supports reduction.

#### The electrochemical series

Metals are arranged in order of decreasing tendency to be reduced. Metals high in the series (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al) lose electrons easily and are powerful reducing agents; metals low in the series (Au, Ag, Pt) hold onto electrons. This series predicts:
- whether a displacement reaction will occur,
- which metal corrodes when two are in contact,
- which ion is discharged during electrolysis of mixed solutions.

#### Faraday's laws of electrolysis

**First law:** the mass m of substance liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the charge Q passed through the electrolyte, m ∝ Q = I × t.

**Second law:** when the same charge passes through different electrolytes in series, the masses deposited are proportional to their chemical equivalent weights (E = M/n).

Combined: **m = (M × I × t) / (n × F)**, with **F = 96,500 C mol⁻¹** (the charge on one mole of electrons).

#### NABTEB question patterns

Expect short-answer calculations using Faraday's first law, identification of anode/cathode from cell diagrams, ordering of discharge of ions (e.g., during electrolysis of brine: H⁺, Na⁺ at cathode; Cl⁻, OH⁻ at anode), and explanation of applications such as **electroplating**, **electrorefining of copper**, and **cathodic protection**.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Standard electrode potential and the SHE

Every electrode potential is measured against the **Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)**, defined as **E° = 0.00 V** when H₂ gas at 1 atm bubbles over a Pt electrode in 1 M H⁺(aq). All other E° values in the electrochemical series are relative to this reference. A positive E° means the species is a better oxidising agent than H⁺; a negative E° means it is a weaker oxidising agent (hence a better reducing agent).

#### Calculating volume of gas and time of electrolysis

For gases at standard conditions, use **V = (M × I × t) / (ρ × n × F)**, where ρ is the density of the gas (g/dm³). At room conditions (not STP), apply the gas law correction, V₂ = V₁ × (T₂/T₁). NABTEB often gives room-temperature values, so watch the conditions stated.

#### Electrolysis of brine (industrial)

Brine (concentrated NaCl) on electrolysis gives H₂ at the cathode and Cl₂ at the anode — not O₂, because Cl⁻ is preferentially discharged over OH⁻ at the anode in concentrated solution. The remaining solution becomes **NaOH**. This is the **chlor-alkali process**.

#### Common mistakes NABTEB candidates make

1. Swapping anode/cathode polarity between galvanic and electrolytic cells.
2. Using M (molar mass) instead of E = M/n (equivalent mass) in Faraday's second law calculations.
3. Forgetting that during electrolysis of dilute aqueous NaCl, OH⁻ is discharged at the anode (giving O₂), while in concentrated brine, Cl⁻ is discharged instead.
4. Assuming current efficiency is 100 % when the question does not state it — mass predicted by Faraday is the maximum possible.
5. Confusing the sign convention of E° values when computing E°cell.

#### Worked example

What mass of copper is deposited when a current of 2.0 A passes through CuSO₄ solution for 1 hour 20 minutes?
Q = 2.0 × (80 × 60) = 9600 C; n = 2; M(Cu) = 63.5; m = (63.5 × 9600) / (2 × 96500) = **3.16 g**.

#### Practice prompts

1. A current of 5 A is passed through two electrolytic cells in series containing AgNO₃ and CuSO₄. Calculate the mass of each metal liberated after 30 minutes.
2. Explain, with reference to the electrochemical series, why iron tanks are not used to store CuSO₄ solution.
