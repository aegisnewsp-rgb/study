# Indian Polity and Constitution — UPPSC RO/ARO General Studies

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

The **Indian Constitution** is the supreme law of the land, adopted on **26 November 1949** and enforced on **26 January 1950**. It declares India a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic. **Fundamental Rights** (Part III, Articles 12–35) are six justiciable categories, while **Fundamental Duties** (Part IVA, Article 51A) were added by the **42nd Amendment, 1976**, and remain non-justiciable.

- **Preamble** — declares Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity; not justiciable.
- **Six Fundamental Rights** — Equality (14–18), Freedom (19–22), Against Exploitation (23–24), Religion (25–28), Cultural & Educational (29–30), Constitutional Remedies (32).
- **Article 32** — Dr. Ambedkar called it the "heart and soul" of the Constitution.
- **Single citizenship** — followed despite a federal structure; no separate state citizenship.

Common trap: confusing **adoption date (26 Nov 1949)** with **commencement date (26 Jan 1950)** — appears almost every UPPSC paper.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Constitutional Foundations

The Constitution draws from the **Government of India Act, 1935** (federal framework, emergency provisions, public service commissions), the **American** Bill of Rights, the **British** parliamentary system and rule of law, the **Irish** Directive Principles, and the **Canadian** federal–unitary balance. India adopted a **parliamentary system** with a **federal structure plus unitary bias** — three emergency lists (Union, Concurrent, State) tilt power toward the Centre.

#### Fundamental Rights vs. Directive Principles

**Fundamental Rights (Part III)** are justiciable — citizens can move the Supreme Court under Article 32 for enforcement. **Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV, Articles 36–51)** are non-justiciable but fundamental in governance. The **42nd Amendment, 1976** inserted **Fundamental Duties** (11 duties) and three new words — *Socialist*, *Secular*, *Integrity* — into the Preamble.

| Concept | Key point |
| --- | --- |
| Adoption | 26 November 1949 (Constituent Assembly) |
| Commencement | 26 January 1950 (Republic Day) |
| Fundamental Rights | Part III, Articles 12–35, six categories, justiciable |
| Fundamental Duties | Part IVA, Article 51A, 11 duties, non-justiciable |
| DPSP | Part IV, Articles 36–51, non-justiciable, directive to State |
| Writ Jurisdiction | Supreme Court (Art 32) and High Courts (Art 226) |
| Basic Structure Doctrine | *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973, 13-judge bench) |

> Memo booster — Article 32 cannot be suspended except during a **National Emergency** under Article 359 (post-44th Amendment, 1978, Articles 20 and 21 remain protected).

#### Parliament and Federal Structure

The **Parliament** comprises the President, Lok Sabha, and Rajya Sabha. The President is **head of state** (not government); the Prime Minister heads the government and commands a Lok Sabha majority. In money bills, the **Lok Sabha has overriding authority** — the Rajya Sabha can only recommend (within 14 days). The **Election Commission** (Article 324) is a permanent, independent body; the **CAG** (Article 148) audits public accounts.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Emergency Provisions and Constitutional Amendments

Three emergencies are codified: **National Emergency (Article 352)** on grounds of war, armed rebellion, or imminent danger; **State Emergency / President's Rule (Article 356)** on breakdown of constitutional machinery; **Financial Emergency (Article 360)** on threat to financial stability or credit of India. The 44th Amendment, 1978 restricted National Emergency to "armed rebellion" (replacing "internal disturbance") and made Article 20 (protection in respect of conviction for offences) and Article 21 (right to life) **non-suspendable** even during emergency.

#### Citizenship and Single-Citizenship Doctrine

Citizenship is governed by the **Citizenship Act, 1955**. Acquisition is five-fold: **birth, descent, registration, naturalisation, and incorporation of territory**. India rejects dual citizenship but offers **Overseas Citizen of India (OCI)** — not full citizenship, without voting rights. The deliberate choice of **single citizenship** over a dual model discourages regional parochialism.

#### Practice Prompts

1. Article 32 is suspended during a National Emergency under Article 359. Identify which two Article rights remain non-suspendable and name the amendment that protected them.
2. *Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)* struck down which two clauses of the 42nd Amendment? Connect it to the Kesavananda principle.

#### High-Yield Mistake List

- **Basic Structure Doctrine** was delivered in *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973) — a **13-judge bench**, the largest ever — not a 5-judge bench.
- **President** = head of state, **PM** = head of government. Never swapped in mains.
- **Six Fundamental Rights** is the standard count; **11 Fundamental Duties**.
- **Single citizenship** — exam answers saying "dual citizenship" lose marks.

> Final tip — UPPSC RO/ARO objective papers frequently ask about **Article 32 vs Article 226 writ jurisdiction**, **42nd Amendment additions**, and **commencement date trick questions**.
