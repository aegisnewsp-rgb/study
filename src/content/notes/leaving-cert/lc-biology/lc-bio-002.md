# Organisation of Life — Leaving Certificate (Established) Biology Notes

Organisation of Life covers the cellular and molecular foundations of biology that underpin every later unit of the NCCA Leaving Certificate Biology specification. The strand bundles cell biology (prokaryotic and eukaryotic cell structure, the differences between plant, animal and bacterial cells), biomolecules (carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids — their structure, function and the tests used to identify them), enzymes (their mode of action, the factors that affect their activity, and the industrial and medical applications of enzyme technology), and the diversity of life (the principles of classification, the major taxonomic groups, and the basic ecology of populations and communities). The strand carries a weight rating of 4 out of 5 in the topic catalogue and is one of the largest discrete blocks on the SEC paper.

At Higher level, Organisation of Life extends into more detailed biomolecular structure (the four levels of protein organisation, the structure of triglycerides and phospholipids, the antiparallel structure of DNA), more demanding enzyme kinetics (the Michaelis-Menten curve, competitive and non-competitive inhibition), and more detailed classification (the binomial system, the five-kingdom system, the phylogenetic relationships among the major groups). At Ordinary level the same content is examined but the mark scheme is more forgiving on the structural details and the kinetic vocabulary. A candidate who has memorised the structures and the tests, and who can explain *why* each structure suits each function, will pick up marks across multiple units of the paper.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Strand weight & spread**: Weight 4/5 in the topic catalogue. Marks are spread across cell biology, biomolecules, enzymes and diversity — under-preparing any one sub-topic depresses the whole strand.
- **Cell types the strand reaches for**: prokaryotic (bacteria — no nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles, circular DNA, smaller ribosomes) and eukaryotic (animals, plants, fungi — nucleus, membrane-bound organelles, linear DNA). Plant cells add a cell wall (cellulose), a permanent vacuole and chloroplasts; animal cells lack these.
- **Four biomolecule groups**: carbohydrates (energy storage, structure — monosaccharides, disaccharides, polysaccharides), lipids (energy storage, membrane structure — triglycerides, phospholipids, steroids), proteins (catalysis, structure, transport — amino acids linked by peptide bonds), nucleic acids (information storage — DNA and RNA, nucleotides linked by phosphodiester bonds).
- **Enzyme core principle**: enzymes are biological catalysts that lower the activation energy of a reaction without being consumed. They are specific to their substrate (lock-and-key or induced-fit model), they are reusable, and their activity is affected by temperature, pH, substrate concentration, enzyme concentration and the presence of inhibitors.
- **Biomolecule tests**: iodine test for starch (blue-black), Benedict's or Fehling's test for reducing sugars (orange-red precipitate when heated), Biuret test for protein (purple), Sudan III test for lipids (red stain), emulsion test for lipids (cloudy white). Confirm the live test specifications on curriculumonline.ie.
- **Classification core principle**: organisms are classified into a hierarchy — domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species. The binomial system uses the genus and species names (e.g. *Homo sapiens*). The five-kingdom system groups organisms into Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia.
- **Ecology basics**: a population is a group of organisms of the same species in a given area; a community is all the populations in a given area; an ecosystem is the community plus its abiotic environment; a biome is a large community shaped by climate. Confirm the live ecological vocabulary on the NCCA specification.
- **First move on a cell-biology question**: identify the cell type (prokaryotic vs eukaryotic, plant vs animal) before naming the structures. Naming the cell type is the lead-in mark on most Higher-level items.

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells. The vocabulary distinction is a mark — "no nucleus" vs "nucleus present" is the core distinction.
- Treating "carbohydrate" as a synonym for "sugar". The strand tests monosaccharides, disaccharides *and* polysaccharides; a candidate who only knows the first two forfeits the polysaccharide mark.
- Drawing an enzyme as a *rigid* lock-and-key. The mark scheme reads for the induced-fit model in modern specifications; "lock-and-key" is acceptable but "induced-fit" is preferred.
- Confusing competitive and non-competitive inhibition. Competitive inhibition is overcome by adding more substrate; non-competitive inhibition is not. The distinction is a mark.
- Using the word "organism" when "population" or "community" is the correct term. The vocabulary matters; the mark scheme reads for the precise ecological term.

#### 30-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Can you draw a labelled plant cell and a labelled animal cell in under five minutes each?
- [ ] Do you know the test, the positive result and the biomolecule detected for each of the four main tests?
- [ ] Can you sketch a graph of enzyme activity vs temperature and label the optimum?
- [ ] Can you write the binomial name of one common organism correctly?

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### Cell biology as the foundation

Cell biology underpins every other unit — genetics, photosynthesis, respiration, plant biology, animal physiology all run on the cellular machinery introduced in this strand. The first task in any Higher-level cell-biology question is to identify the cell type (prokaryotic vs eukaryotic; plant vs animal vs fungal) and the structures visible. The SEC mark scheme rewards a candidate who can:

- **Name the structure** (e.g. mitochondrion, chloroplast, ribosome, endoplasmic reticulum).
- **State its function** (e.g. mitochondrion — site of aerobic respiration; chloroplast — site of photosynthesis).
- **Connect structure to function** (e.g. the inner mitochondrial membrane is highly folded into cristae, increasing the surface area for the electron transport chain).

The structure-function link is what the mark scheme consistently rewards. A candidate who can draw a mitochondrion with labelled cristae and explain why the cristae are folded — because the electron transport chain requires a large surface area — picks up marks that a candidate who simply lists "mitochondrion = powerhouse" forfeits.

#### Biomolecules — structure determines function

The four biomolecule groups are united by a single principle: *structure determines function*. Each group's structure is what enables its biological role.

- **Carbohydrates**: monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose) are the building blocks. Disaccharides (maltose, sucrose, lactose) are joined by glycosidic bonds. Polysaccharides (starch, glycogen, cellulose) are long chains that serve as energy stores (starch, glycogen) or structural material (cellulose). The glycosidic bond is the structural feature; the storage vs structure distinction is the functional outcome.
- **Lipids**: triglycerides are glycerol plus three fatty acids; saturated fatty acids have no double bonds (solid at room temperature), unsaturated have one or more double bonds (liquid at room temperature). Phospholipids replace one fatty acid with a phosphate group, giving the molecule a hydrophilic head and hydrophobic tails — the basis of the membrane structure.
- **Proteins**: amino acids linked by peptide bonds fold into secondary structures (alpha helix, beta pleated sheet) stabilised by hydrogen bonds, then into tertiary structures (the 3D shape of a single polypeptide) stabilised by interactions among R-groups, then into quaternary structures (multiple polypeptides). The 3D shape is what determines the protein's function; denaturation (by heat, pH or chemicals) destroys the shape and the function.
- **Nucleic acids**: nucleotides consist of a phosphate, a sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA) and a nitrogenous base (A, T, C, G in DNA; A, U, C, G in RNA). DNA is double-stranded and antiparallel; RNA is single-stranded. The sequence of bases is the genetic information; the base-pairing rules (A-T, G-C in DNA; A-U, G-C in RNA) determine how the information is copied and read.

The mark scheme rewards candidates who can connect *each* structural feature to a *specific* function. Memorising "DNA is the genetic material" earns less than explaining how the antiparallel double helix enables semiconservative replication.

#### Enzymes — kinetics and inhibition

The enzyme sub-topic at Higher level extends into enzyme kinetics: the Michaelis-Menten curve (reaction rate vs substrate concentration), the concept of Vmax (the maximum rate, achieved when all enzyme active sites are saturated), and the two main types of inhibition.

- **Competitive inhibition**: the inhibitor resembles the substrate and binds to the active site. The inhibition is overcome by adding more substrate; Vmax is unchanged but the apparent Km (the substrate concentration at half-Vmax) increases.
- **Non-competitive inhibition**: the inhibitor binds to a site other than the active site (an allosteric site), changing the enzyme's shape. The inhibition is *not* overcome by adding more substrate; Vmax decreases.

The mark scheme reads for the kinetic *graph*, not just the verbal description. A candidate who can sketch the two inhibition curves and label the change in Vmax and Km reliably picks up the full enzyme kinetics mark.

#### Classification and ecology

The diversity sub-topic covers the principles of classification (binomial nomenclature, hierarchy, the five-kingdom system) and the basics of ecology (population, community, ecosystem, biome, niche, habitat). The most consistent SEC questions are:

- **Naming the taxonomic level** (e.g. "what is the phylum of *Homo sapiens*?") — vocabulary mark.
- **Explaining the binomial system** — why two names are used, what each name means.
- **Distinguishing habitat and niche** — habitat is where an organism lives; niche is how it lives (its role in the ecosystem).
- **Reading a food web** — naming the producer, the primary consumer, the secondary consumer, the tertiary consumer; identifying the apex predator; tracing energy flow.

A candidate who can name the five kingdoms and give an example organism in each, and who can read a simple food web, will pick up the classification and ecology marks on any SEC paper.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### The prokaryotic-eukaryotic distinction at Higher level

At Higher level the prokaryotic-eukaryotic distinction is tested in detail. The mark scheme reads for:

- **Size**: prokaryotic cells are typically 1-10 µm; eukaryotic cells are typically 10-100 µm.
- **Nucleus**: prokaryotic cells have no membrane-bound nucleus; eukaryotic cells do.
- **Organelles**: prokaryotic cells have no membrane-bound organelles; eukaryotic cells have mitochondria, ER, Golgi, etc.
- **DNA**: prokaryotic DNA is circular and lies free in the cytoplasm (the nucleoid region); eukaryotic DNA is linear and packaged into chromosomes within the nucleus.
- **Ribosomes**: prokaryotic ribosomes are smaller (70S) than eukaryotic ribosomes (80S).

A Higher-grade candidate who can list all five distinctions and *connect* each one to a functional consequence (e.g. the larger eukaryotic ribosome enables more complex protein synthesis) picks up marks that a candidate who merely lists the differences forfeits.

#### The fluid mosaic model of the membrane

The cell membrane is described by the fluid mosaic model: a phospholipid bilayer in which proteins are embedded and move laterally. The mark scheme reads for:

- **Phospholipid bilayer**: hydrophilic heads outward, hydrophobic tails inward. The bilayer is the basic structural feature.
- **Membrane proteins**: channel proteins (allow specific ions or molecules to cross), carrier proteins (transport specific molecules by changing shape), receptor proteins (bind signal molecules and trigger a response), marker proteins (cell-surface identity markers).
- **Cholesterol**: embedded in the bilayer, reducing fluidity at high temperatures and preventing solidification at low temperatures.
- **Glycoproteins and glycolipids**: carbohydrate chains attached to membrane proteins or lipids; function in cell recognition and signalling.

The mark scheme rewards candidates who can connect *each* structural feature to its *function*. The fluid mosaic model is one of the most-tested Higher-level diagrams on the SEC paper.

#### Common mistakes that depress the grade

- **The "powerhouse" trap** — saying "mitochondria are the powerhouse of the cell" without explaining what they actually *do* (aerobic respiration, ATP production). The mark scheme rewards the function, not the slogan.
- **The "enzymes are used up" trap** — saying enzymes are "used up" in the reaction. Enzymes are *reusable*; they catalyse without being consumed. The vocabulary matters.
- **The "DNA is RNA" trap** — confusing DNA and RNA. The mark scheme reads for the sugar (deoxyribose vs ribose), the bases (T vs U) and the structure (double vs single strand). A candidate who mixes these up forfeits the molecular-biology marks.
- **The "five kingdoms = five animals" trap** — listing only animals as examples of the five kingdoms. The mark scheme reads for an example organism in *each* of the five kingdoms.
- **The "habitat = niche" trap** — using the two terms interchangeably. Habitat is the *place*; niche is the *role*. The distinction is a mark.
