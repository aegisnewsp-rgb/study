# Circles and Quadrilaterals

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **circle** is the set of points equidistant from a centre; its area is **πr²** and its circumference is **2πr**. A **quadrilateral** is any four-sided closed polygon whose interior angles sum to **360°**. Two specialised families dominate the HAT-UG paper: **cyclic quadrilaterals** (vertices on a circle, opposite angles summing to 180°) and the **parallelogram family** (parallelogram, rectangle, rhombus, square) with shared diagonal-bisection properties.

Must-know results:
- **Inscribed angle theorem**: an angle at the circumference equals half the central angle subtending the same arc.
- **Power of a point**: for a tangent PT and secant PAB, **PT² = PA × PB**.
- **Rhombus area** uses diagonals: **A = ½ · d₁ · d₂** (NOT base × height unless the rhombus is also a square).
- **Trapezium midline** (joining non-parallel side midpoints) = **(a + b) / 2**, where a, b are the parallel sides.

HAT-UG traps: students halve when they should double the arc, or plug the slant edge into the parallelogram area formula.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Circle Building Blocks
A **chord** joins two points on the circle; the **arc** is the curved portion cut off; a **sector** is the pie-slice bounded by two radii and an arc; a **segment** is the region between a chord and its minor arc. The area of a sector of central angle θ° is **(θ/360)·πr²**, and the arc length is **(θ/360)·2πr**. A **tangent** touches the circle at exactly one point and is **perpendicular to the radius** drawn to that point.

#### The Inscribed Angle Theorem
If an angle is drawn at the centre (central angle) and another at any point on the remaining circumference (inscribed angle), both opening to the **same arc**, then inscribed = ½ × central. Equivalently, all inscribed angles opening to the same arc are **equal to each other** (angles in the same segment).

#### Cyclic Quadrilaterals
A quadrilateral whose four vertices lie on one circle is **cyclic**. Its defining property is that **opposite angles are supplementary**: ∠A + ∠C = ∠B + ∠D = 180°. Conversely, if one pair of opposite angles in a quadrilateral sums to 180°, the quadrilateral is cyclic.

#### The Parallelogram Family
A **parallelogram** has both pairs of opposite sides parallel; its **diagonals bisect each other**. A **rectangle** inherits diagonal bisection and adds **equal diagonals**. A **rhombus** keeps bisection and adds **diagonals that are perpendicular**, giving area **½ · d₁ · d₂**. A **square** combines both: equal AND perpendicular diagonals. A **trapezium (trapezoid)** has exactly one pair of parallel sides; a **kite** has two pairs of adjacent equal sides with one diagonal that perpendicularly bisects the other.

#### Area Formulas at a Glance
| Figure | Area Formula |
|---|---|
| Parallelogram | base × perpendicular height |
| Rhombus / Kite | ½ · d₁ · d₂ |
| Trapezium | ½ · (a + b) · h |
| Sector | (θ/360) · πr² |

**Exam pattern**: HAT-UG typically gives 1–3 MCQs mixing a circle-theorem step with a quadrilateral-area calculation. A common item asks for an unknown angle using cyclic-quadrilateral supplementary property, then asks for a sector or trapezium area in a chained sub-question.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Power of a Point and Tangent-Secant Chains
When two secants meet outside a circle at P, with one secant cutting the circle at A, B and another at C, D, then **PA · PB = PC · PD**. The tangent version is the special case where one chord collapses to a point: **PT² = PA · PB**. HAT-UG occasionally tests this through a "find the tangent length" item where the student must square the whole-chord–external-part product, not just the external segment.

#### Tangential vs Cyclic — Don't Confuse Them
A **tangential quadrilateral** has an **inscribed circle** touching all four sides. Its defining property is **a + c = b + d** (sum of one pair of opposite sides equals the other). This is **not** the same as a cyclic quadrilateral, whose property is about angles. A square satisfies both; a generic rectangle satisfies neither (its opposite sides are equal, so a + c = b + d only when all four are equal, i.e. a square).

#### Edge Cases Worth Memorising
- A **rhombus with 60° angles** has short diagonal = side length and long diagonal = side · √3 — a favourite for numerical MCQs.
- An **isosceles trapezium inscribed in a circle** is automatically cyclic; its non-parallel sides are equal, and its diagonals are equal in length.
- The **midline of a trapezium** is parallel to the parallel sides and equals their average: it is **not** the diagonal.
- A **square's diagonal = s√2**; the area of the square formed by joining the midpoints of the sides of any quadrilateral is **half** the original's area (Varignon's theorem, a high-value shortcut for compound figures).

#### Common Mistakes
1. Using the **slant side** instead of the **perpendicular height** in parallelogram or trapezium area — always drop a perpendicular.
2. For **½ · d₁ · d₂**, applying it to a plain parallelogram (it fails unless the diagonals are perpendicular, i.e. a rhombus or kite).
3. Halving the wrong way on inscribed-angle items: "angle at centre is twice angle at circumference", so divide the central value, do not multiply.
4. Mixing π approximations (22/7 vs 3.14) within one problem — pick one and keep it.

#### Worked Micro-Example
A cyclic quadrilateral PQRS has ∠P = 110°. Find ∠R, and then the area of triangle PQR if diagonal PR = 10 cm and the perpendicular from Q to PR is 4 cm.
Step 1: cyclic ⇒ ∠P + ∠R = 180°, so ∠R = **70°**.
Step 2: area of △PQR = ½ · 10 · 4 = **20 cm²**.

#### Practice Prompts
1. Two chords AB and CD of a circle meet at point E inside the circle, with AE = 4, EB = 9, CE = 6. Find ED.
2. A rhombus has diagonals 16 cm and 12 cm. Find its side length and area, then verify the area using base × height.
