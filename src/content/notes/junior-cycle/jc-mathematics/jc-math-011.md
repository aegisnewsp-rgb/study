# Right-angled Trigonometry — Junior Cycle Mathematics Notes

Sine, cosine and tangent are three definitions, not three formulas to memorise. A candidate who knows which side is *opposite* the angle being used, which is the *adjacent* side and which is the *hypotenuse*, can reconstruct all three ratios in any arrangement. A candidate who memorises without that anchor cannot.

## The three ratios

| Ratio | Definition | Use it when |
|---|---|---|
| sin *θ* | **opposite** ÷ hypotenuse | Given the opposite side or the hypotenuse |
| cos *θ* | **adjacent** ÷ hypotenuse | Given the adjacent side or the hypotenuse |
| tan *θ* | **opposite** ÷ adjacent | Given the other two sides directly |

**SOH-CAH-TOA** is the mnemonic, and the position of each letter is the whole content of it: sine and tangent both start from **O**pposite; cosine starts from **C**dges, i.e. the adjacent side; each of the first two ends at the hypotenuse (**H**), and tangent ends at **T** (the tangent of the angle).

### The two-step procedure

1. **Name the angle you are working with.** The ratios are defined relative to a specific angle, and using the wrong one gives a reciprocal.
2. **Label the three sides** of the right-angled triangle relative to *that* angle: opposite, adjacent, hypotenuse.
3. **Choose the ratio from the pairs you actually have.** Two sides and one angle always fit exactly one of the three ratios.
4. **Set up the ratio, substitute, then invert.** Inverting is the step candidates forget when the ratio has the unknown on the bottom: if *sin θ* = *x*/5* then *x* = 5 sin *θ*, not *x* = 5/*sin θ*.

Candidates who draw the triangle and label the sides cannot make the last error, which is why the marking schemes encourage a labelled diagram.

## Angles of elevation and depression

The two are the same angle measured from opposite ends of a parallel horizontal line. At the top of a tower looking down at a point, the depression angle equals the elevation angle from that point looking up at the top.

The exam usually supplies a diagram showing both. If it does not, draw one: mark the horizontal line at the observer and the vertical at the tower, put the same symbol on the two angles, and the equality is self-evident. Naming them as two separate unknowns is a step that costs time and produces the wrong result.

**What trigonometry cannot do.** A right triangle with one known angle and one known side does not determine the third. In these applications the second known length is the horizontal or vertical distance given in the problem — find it before you start.

## Compound and multi-step problems

The specification asks for problems where one triangle feeds into the next. The routine is:

1. Identify the triangle that contains two known quantities.
2. Solve it completely — including the intermediate angle if the second triangle needs it.
3. Build the second triangle and repeat.

The most common loss is stopping after the first triangle. If the question asks for a length that is not in the first triangle, you have not finished.

**Heights of buildings and trees** usually give a second known length — the distance between two observation points, or the length of a shadow. Identify which length you have before choosing which triangle to solve first.

**Angles in a horizontal plane** use the fact that the bearing from *A* to *B* and from *B* to *A* differ by 180°. Draw the plan view and mark it before substituting anything into a sine or cosine.

## The inverse functions

arcsin, arccos and arctan reverse the operation. They are required whenever the question gives you two sides and asks for the angle, because no direct ratio produces an angle.

**Set the calculator to degrees.** The exam gives angles in degrees and the calculator defaults to radians; the error produces an answer of about 1.57 times too large, which is easy to spot and easy to fix.

**Check with a quick estimate.** sin 30° = 0.5 and sin 60° ≈ 0.87. If your arcsin of 0.5 returns anything other than 30°, the mode is wrong.

## What loses marks

1. **Naming the angle after labelling the sides.** Label relative to the angle you are using, not the angle at the right-angled corner. The right angle is a fixed feature of the triangle, not your chosen angle.
2. **Forgetting to invert.** sin *θ* = *x*/6* gives *x* = 6 sin *θ*.
3. **Radians instead of degrees.**
4. **Using the angle between the line of sight and the vertical** where the question gives the horizontal angle, or the reverse. Read which surface the angle is measured from — the diagram or the wording will say.
5. **Stopping mid-problem** after the first triangle.
6. **Forgetting units** on the final length.

## Self-check

1. In a right triangle, *θ* = 30°, opposite = 7. Find the hypotenuse. *(sin 30° = opposite/hypotenuse gives 0.5 = 7/*h*, so *h* = 14. This is the 30-60-90 ratio in its simplest form — half the hypotenuse equals the shorter leg.)*
2. In a right triangle, *θ* = 40°, adjacent = 12. Find the opposite. *(*tan 40° = *x*/12, so *x* = 12 × 0.839 ≈ 10.07. tan is the ratio starting from opposite and using adjacent, which is why you do not use cosine here — that would give the hypotenuse.)*
3. A tower's angle of elevation from *A* is 20° and from *B*, further away, is 15°. What is the relationship between the two angles? *(They are not equal and not supplementary — they are two different triangles sharing the tower's height. You need the distance AB to relate them: *h* = *d* tan 20° = (*d* + *L*) tan 15°.)*
4. Your calculator returns 85.94° for the angle in a triangle whose diagram shows a clearly acute angle. What is wrong? *(Radian mode. 85.94 radians is about 4,924°; a degrees-mode result for that shape would be around 49°.)*
5. Why must you label the sides relative to the chosen angle rather than the right angle? *(Because the ratios are defined by position relative to the angle in question. The opposite side is opposite that angle — the side across from it — and it is not generally the side across from the right angle. Mislabeling gives you the reciprocal of every ratio.)*
