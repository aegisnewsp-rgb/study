# Number System

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

The **Number System** is the bedrock of SSC CGL Quantitative Aptitude. Every integer, rational value, and divisibility property you meet in arithmetic traces back here. HCF and LCM drive grouping/splitting word problems; unit-digit cyclicity and modular arithmetic handle huge powers quickly.

| Term | Definition | Notation |
|---|---|---|
| Natural numbers | Counting numbers starting at 1 | N = {1, 2, 3, …} |
| Whole numbers | Naturals plus zero | W = {0, 1, 2, …} |
| Integers | Whole numbers plus negatives | Z = {…, −2, −1, 0, 1, …} |
| Rational | p/q with q ≠ 0 | a/b ∈ Q |
| Irrational | Non-terminating, non-repeating decimals | √2, π |
| HCF (GCD) | Largest number dividing all given numbers | HCF(a, b) |
| LCM | Smallest number divisible by each given number | LCM(a, b) |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"H = Highest divide, L = Least multiple"** — HCF is the *biggest* size that *divides* everyone; LCM is the *smallest* size that *contains* everyone. Memorise the unit-digit cycles as **"2-3-7-8 cycle 4, 4-9 cycle 2, 0-1-5-6 stay fixed"**.

Two formulas carry 70% of marks in this topic:

- **HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b** (only for two positive integers; using it for three numbers is a trap).
- **Sum of first n naturals:** S<sub>n</sub> = n(n + 1)/2.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem
**Question:** Three bells ring at intervals of 6, 8, and 9 minutes respectively. If they ring together at 8:00 AM, after how many minutes will they next ring together, and what is the HCF of 6, 8, 9?

#### Solution:
Step 1 — Find LCM of 6, 8, 9 using prime factorisation:
- 6 = 2 × 3
- 8 = 2³
- 9 = 3²

LCM = 2³ × 3² = 8 × 9 = **72 minutes**.
Step 2 — Find HCF: no prime is common to all three, so **HCF = 1**.
Step 3 — They next ring together at **9:12 AM**.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students often swap HCF and LCM. "After how long will they ring **together again**?" needs LCM (the *smallest common multiple*). "What is the **largest** interval at which each bell rings exactly once?" would need HCF. Read the verb — *together* / *maximum size that divides all* → LCM; *biggest equal split* → HCF.

#### Core Divisibility Toolkit
Memorise these tests to avoid long division on every problem.

| Divisor | Rule | Shortcut |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Last digit is even (0, 2, 4, 6, 8) | One-digit check |
| 3 | Sum of digits divisible by 3 | Digit-sum test |
| 4 | Last two digits divisible by 4 | Two-digit check |
| 5 | Last digit 0 or 5 | One-digit check |
| 8 | Last three digits divisible by 8 | Three-digit check |
| 9 | Sum of digits divisible by 9 | Digit-sum test |
| 11 | (Sum of odd-position digits − sum of even-position digits) ≡ 0 mod 11 | Alternating sum |
| 7, 13 | Use factor expansion or double-the-last-digit rule | Advanced |

#### HCF vs LCM — When to Use Which
- "**Maximum size** that can measure/bind/split all given quantities evenly" → **HCF**.
- "**Minimum size** needed so each event repeats, or so all complete cycles align" → **LCM**.
- Example: Cutting three rods of 6 m, 8 m, 9 m into the **longest equal pieces** with **no waste** → HCF = 1 m. The bells problem above → LCM.

#### Number of Divisors Formula
If N = p<sub>1</sub><sup>a<sub>1</sub></sup> · p<sub>2</sub><sup>a<sub>2</sub></sup> · … · p<sub>k</sub><sup>a<sub>k</sup></sup>, then the total number of divisors of N is **(a<sub>1</sub> + 1)(a<sub>2</sub> + 1) … (a<sub>k</sub> + 1)**. The sum of divisors uses the same exponents in a product of (p<sup>a+1</sup> − 1)/(p − 1).

#### Euclid's Algorithm (fast HCF)
HCF(a, b) = HCF(b, a mod b). Keep dividing until remainder becomes 0; the last non-zero remainder is the HCF. This is faster than prime factorisation for large numbers such as HCF(1124, 382).

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Unit-Digit Cyclicity for Large Powers
Powers of any digit follow a fixed cycle. Reduce the exponent using **modulo cycle length**:

| Base | Cycle of last digit | Cycle length |
|---|---|---|
| 0, 1, 5, 6 | Same digit always | 1 |
| 4, 9 | 4 → 6, 9 → 1 | 2 |
| 2, 3, 7, 8 | 4 distinct digits, repeats | 4 |

Worked numeric: Find the last digit of 7<sup>123</sup>. Cycle for 7 is {7, 9, 3, 1}. Divide exponent by 4: 123 ÷ 4 gives remainder 3 (since 4 × 30 = 120). The 3rd digit in the cycle is **3**, so last digit = **3**.

#### Remainder of Large Powers via Euler's Theorem (SSC Level)
For co-prime a and m, a<sup>φ(m)</sup> ≡ 1 (mod m), where φ(m) is Euler's totient. Reduce the exponent modulo φ(m). Quick values to memorise: φ(7) = 6, φ(9) = 6, φ(11) = 10, φ(13) = 12. This handles the otherwise impossible 7<sup>200</sup> mod 13 type of question.

#### Edge Cases and Misconceptions
- **1 is neither prime nor composite.** Many aspirants wrongly classify it as prime.
- **HCF × LCM = a × b** holds only for *two* positive integers, not three. For three numbers a, b, c: product of pairwise HCFs × LCM relationships is more complex.
- **Co-prime ≠ prime individually.** HCF(8, 9) = 1, but neither 8 nor 9 is prime. Two numbers with HCF 1 are co-prime; they can both be composite.
- **Sum-of-digits rule** works for 9 and 3 (divisors of 10 − 1 = 9) but not for 7 or 11. Don't generalise across divisors.
- **Remainder of product:** (a × b) mod m ≠ (a mod m) × (b mod m) mod m is fine only when you take the full product mod m; truncation steps need careful handling.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts
1. A number leaves remainder 2 when divided by 5, remainder 3 when divided by 7, and remainder 4 when divided by 9. Find the smallest such positive integer. (Use CRT-style approach: equate to x − 2 divisible by 5, 7, 9 ⇒ LCM(5, 7, 9) = 315; answer = 2 + k·315. Smallest positive → 2 if it satisfies all, else next.)
2. Find the number of trailing zeroes in 50!. (Count factors of 5: ⌊50/5⌋ + ⌊50/25⌋ = 10 + 2 = **12**.) Combine factorial zero-counting with HCF/divisor logic in complex number-system problems.
