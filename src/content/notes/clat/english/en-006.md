# Fill in Blanks

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Fill in the Blanks (FIB) in CLAT English tests your ability to slot the right word into a sentence or short cloze passage, weighing four contextual and grammatical cues at once. Each blank carries one mark, and the section contributes roughly 3% of the total CLAT UG score. The task is less about dictionary recall and more about **collocational fit**, **syntactic agreement**, and **register match**.

| Cue | What to scan for | Example signal |
|---|---|---|
| Grammar | Tense, number, article | "an ___" (vowel sound) |
| Collocation | Verb + preposition / adj + noun | "ranging ___ from" |
| Register | Formal vs informal tone | "deemed", "reckoned" |
| Logic | Connectors "however", "therefore" | Contrast or conclusion |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **G-C-R-L** — **G**rammar first, **C**ollocation second, **R**egister third, **L**ogic last. Read the blank's clause for grammar cues, then scan options for natural word partnerships, match the tone, and only then confirm logical sense.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### How FIB Actually Works

Each FIB item hands you a sentence (or a 150–200-word cloze passage with 4–6 blanks) and four word options. Only one option is simultaneously grammatical, collocationally natural, and logically coherent with the surrounding text. You are not choosing the "biggest" word or the rarest synonym; you are choosing the word the original author would have used. This is why FIB is a **high-discriminator** item — toppers eliminate distractor pairs using grammar before they even read the full definitions.

#### Grammar Cues That Decide the Answer

Three grammatical signals lock the blank down before vocabulary enters the picture:

1. **Tense** — if the main verb is past ("the court *ruled*"), the blank cannot house a present-tense form like "deciding" unless a continuous aspect is forced by context.
2. **Number** — singular determiners ("each", "either", "this") require singular nouns; plural cues ("several", "both") require plural nouns.
3. **Article + sound** — "a" precedes a consonant sound, "an" precedes a vowel sound (so "an honest man", not "a honest man").

#### Collocations: The Hidden Filter

A collocation is a word pair that native speakers use together so often it feels "fixed". Wrong collocations sound translated, even when each word is correct on its own.

| Collocation type | Correct pair | Wrong pair (common distractor) |
|---|---|---|
| Verb + preposition | *comply with* | *comply to* |
| Adjective + noun | *deeply rooted* belief | *deeply fixed* belief |
| Verb + noun | *reach a consensus* | *make a consensus* |
| Adverb + adjective | *highly unlikely* | *much unlikely* |

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Candidates who memorise a word's dictionary meaning but skip its typical partners pick the option that "means the right thing" — and lose the mark. If two options look synonymous, the collocation test breaks the tie.

#### Look-Alike Pairs the Exam Loves

| Word | Meaning | Confused with |
|---|---|---|
| *adverse* | unfavourable | *averse* (reluctant) |
| *complement* | complete / add to | *compliment* (praise) |
| *stationary* | not moving | *stationery* (paper goods) |
| *principal* | main / head | *principle* (rule) |

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

#### Question:
*"The committee, after much deliberation, decided that the policy was ___ to the interests of the stakeholders and recommended its immediate revision."*  
Options: (A) adverse (B) averse (C) adverse (D) advert

#### Solution:
Step 1 — Read the blank's clause: "was ___ to the interests". The preposition "to" follows; the structure calls for an adjective describing the policy's effect on interests.  
Step 2 — Test meaning: "unfavourable to the interests" makes sense; "reluctant to the interests" does not.  
Step 3 — Match spelling: *adverse* (unfavourable) is needed, not *averse* (reluctant), not *advert* (an advertisement).  
#### Correct answer: (A) adverse.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students who confuse *adverse* and *averse* choose (B) because both end in "-verse". The grammar is identical ("was X to the interests"), so meaning alone disambiguates — meaning you must read the sentence, not just parse it.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases That Change the Answer

FIB items become punishing when three or more cues point opposite directions. Train yourself to weigh them in a fixed order so you do not stall on a single hard item.

1. **Negative polarity items** — words like *any*, *ever*, *yet* appear only in negative or interrogative clauses ("I didn't see **anyone**"). Inserting them in a positive sentence is grammatical suicide.
2. **Parallel structure with correlatives** — *neither...nor*, *either...or*, *not only...but also* demand identical grammatical forms on both sides. A blank inside such a frame is locked to its partner.
3. **Phrasal verb particles** — *put up with* (tolerate), *put up* (erect), *put off* (postpone) share a verb but mean different things. The object and surrounding context dictate the particle.
4. **Modal shadow** — after *would/could/should/might*, the bare infinitive follows ("he **go**"), not the to-infinitive or -ing form.
5. **Connotation shift** — *cheap* (low price) versus *inexpensive* (neutral) versus *economical* (cost-efficient). When the passage is formal, the neutral or positive term wins.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

| # | Prompt | Skill drilled |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Spot the wrong collocation in "make a mistake", "do damage", "take a decision", "reach a verdict" | Verb–noun collocation |
| 2 | Rewrite "*complement / compliment / complement / compliment*" into two sentences using each word with correct meaning | Look-alike disambiguation |
| 3 | Choose between *however*, *therefore*, *moreover*, *nevertheless* given a blank preceded by a comma and a contrast setup | Connective logic |

> 📌 **Exam Strategy:** The CLAT UG English area runs 22–25 questions in roughly 40 minutes, leaving you under two minutes per question. For FIB, spend 30 seconds scanning grammar cues; if two options survive, pick the one whose collocation matches the passage's register — formal passages reward formal vocabulary. Skip only if all four options collapse on meaning; a 25% guess is mathematically superior to a blank you cannot reach.
