# Data Sufficiency

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Data Sufficiency (DS) is a **decision-logic question type**, not a solving task. The stem gives a problem; two statements (I and II) follow. Your job is to judge whether the data is **enough** to produce a unique answer — not to compute it.

- **Sufficient** = statement alone yields ONE definite answer (a single integer, value, yes/no).
- **Insufficient** = statement yields 2 or more consistent answers, or no answer at all.
- Test Statement I alone → then Statement II alone → then BOTH together. Never jump to "both."
- The official 5-option code: **(A)** I only, **(B)** II only, **(C)** both, **(D)** either alone, **(E)** neither.
- In CAT DILR, expect ~3–4 DS questions, 3 marks each, no negative marking on attempted questions.

| DS Option | Meaning |
|---|---|
| A | Statement I alone is sufficient; Statement II is not |
| B | Statement II alone is sufficient; Statement I is not |
| C | Both statements together are sufficient; neither is alone |
| D | Each statement alone is sufficient (rare but valid) |
| E | Neither statement alone nor both together are sufficient |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"I, II, I+II, Either, Neither"** — think of it as the **1-2-3-E-N** ladder. Always climb the ladder in order; never skip a rung. If I alone gives one value → answer is A and you stop. If I gives two values, fall down and test II alone. Only reach the "both" rung when I and II each independently fail.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### What "Sufficiency" Actually Means

Sufficiency is a binary verdict, not a probability. A statement is sufficient only when every reader, using standard arithmetic and logic, lands on **the same unique answer**. The moment a statement permits two consistent values — say, x = 3 or x = 7 — sufficiency collapses, even if one answer seems "more likely."

Yes/No questions follow the same rule. If Statement I can prove the answer is "Yes" in some cases and "No" in others, Statement I is insufficient regardless of how strong it looks.

#### The Independent-Test Rule

The single most-violated rule: **never test statements together before testing them independently.** A common error is reading both statements, seeing that they "fit," and marking C — without checking whether I alone already settles the question. If I alone suffices, the answer is A, not C. This single habit catches the highest number of wrong answers in CAT DS.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Concept | Looks Sufficient But Isn't | Why It Fails |
|---|---|---|
| Range answer (e.g., x between 4 and 9) | Students mark sufficient | Multiple integers satisfy it; no unique value |
| Equation with two solutions (x² = 16) | Looks decisive | x = 4 or x = −4; two possible answers |
| Approximate value (x ≈ 5.3) | Feels close enough | Approximation is not a definitive verdict |
| Always-true statement (e.g., x² ≥ 0) | Seems powerful | Gives no specific answer to the question |
| Either-statement case | In many papers missed entirely | Each must independently yield the same unique answer |

#### Five Standard Problem Types

1. **Arithmetic DS** — questions on percentages, ratios, ages, profit/loss where statements give partial numerical data.
2. **Algebraic DS** — equations in one or two variables where statements supply coefficients or constraints.
3. **Number-theory DS** — divisibility, prime checks, digit properties requiring specific numerical anchors.
4. **Geometry DS** — area, perimeter, angle values where statements give lengths or one extra measurement.
5. **Logic/ordering DS** — ranking, seating, set-membership questions where statements identify a unique person or position.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** What is the value of integer *x*?

- **Statement I:** *x*² − 7*x* + 12 = 0
- **Statement II:** *x* > 2

#### Solution:

- **Test Statement I alone.** Factor: (*x* − 3)(*x* − 4) = 0, giving *x* = 3 or *x* = 4. Two possible values → **I alone is NOT sufficient**.
- **Test Statement II alone.** *x* > 2 is an open inequality; it permits infinitely many integers (3, 4, 5, 6, …) → **II alone is NOT sufficient**.
- **Test both together.** Combine: *x* ∈ {3, 4} ∩ {x > 2} = {3, 4}. Two values still remain → **together they are NOT sufficient**.
- **Answer: (E) Neither statement alone nor both together are sufficient.**

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students stop at Statement I, factor the quadratic, and mark A because the equation "looks solved." A quadratic with two distinct real roots is automatically insufficient — factorisation alone never proves sufficiency. Always check the root count before declaring victory.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Boundary Conditions

The Option D trap is the hardest cell in the matrix. It activates when each statement, by a different route, lands on the **same unique answer**. For instance, Statement I can pin *x* = 5 through an equation, and Statement II can pin *x* = 5 through a divisibility constraint. Both alone suffice → answer is D, not C. CAT setters plant this trap in roughly 1 of every 4 DS sets.

A subtler edge: statements phrased in **percentages versus absolutes**. "The price rose by 10%" plus "the original price was ₹400" yields a unique new price — but "the price rose by 10%" alone, even with a year tag, does not. Watch for unit mismatches between the two statements; they in many papers look combinable but are not.

#### Common DS Traps and Exceptions

1. **The "almost unique" trap** — a statement narrows *x* down to two candidate values; students pick the "more likely" one and mark sufficient. Reject: two valid answers = insufficient.
2. **The hidden constraint** — Statement II introduces a constraint (e.g., *x* is a positive integer) that, when joined with I, collapses two answers into one. Verify the joint domain before answering.
3. **The either-alone misfire** — Option D requires **both** statements independently sufficient, not just consistent with each other. If only Statement II suffices and Statement I does not, the answer is B, not D.
4. **The redundant-statement trap** — when II repeats or rephrases I in different words, both alone in standard papers fail, but together they also fail to add new information. Default verdict: E.
5. **The yes/no reversal** — for Boolean questions, "Yes in one case, No in another" inside the same statement is automatic insufficiency, regardless of how narrow the cases appear.

#### Exam Strategy for CAT DILR

DS questions carry ~4% weight in the DILR section but reward a high accuracy-to-time ratio. The expected payoff: spend 60–90 seconds per question, eliminate two options early by testing one statement, then resolve the choice with a single joint check or a second independent test. Candidates who try to fully solve the underlying problem lose the time advantage DS is designed to offer.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. A stem asks for the **largest integer divisor of N**. Statement I says N is divisible by 12; Statement II says N is divisible by 18. Decide sufficiency without computing — does Option D apply here?
2. A stem asks whether point *P* lies inside triangle *ABC*. Statement I gives the side lengths of the triangle; Statement II gives the distance from *P* to each vertex. Which option is correct, and why does Option D fail in this geometry case?

> 📌 **Formula Check:** DS itself carries no formula — its "formula" is the decision procedure: test I alone → test II alone → test both together → check Option D symmetry. Mastery of this procedure is worth more than any arithmetic shortcut.
