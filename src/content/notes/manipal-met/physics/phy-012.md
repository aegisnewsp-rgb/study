### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

**Current, drift velocity, mobility.** Current $I = \dfrac{q}{t} = nAv_d e$ for electrons, where $n$ is the free-electron density, $A$ the cross-section, $v_d$ the drift speed, and $e$ the elementary charge. Drift velocity is tiny (typically $\sim 10^{-4}\,\text{m/s}$) — current travels fast because the field propagates near $c$, not because electrons move fast. Mobility $\mu = v_d/E$, so $v_d = \mu E$ and $I = nA\mu e E$.

**Ohm's law and resistance.** $V = IR$ for Ohmic conductors (linear $V$-$I$). Resistance $R = \rho L/A$ where $\rho$ is resistivity, $L$ length, $A$ area. Conductivity $\sigma = 1/\rho$. The reciprocal-ohm unit $\Omega$ measures $V/A$.

**Series and parallel resistors.**
- Series: $R_s = R_1 + R_2 + R_3 + \dots$ (current same through each; voltages add).
- Parallel: $\dfrac{1}{R_p} = \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2} + \dfrac{1}{R_3} + \dots$ (voltage same across each; currents add).

**Temperature dependence.** $R(T) = R_0[1 + \alpha(T - T_0)]$ for metals (resistance rises with temperature). For semiconductors and insulators, resistance falls — the formula changes sign convention; the MET typically only asks the metal case.

**Cells, emf, internal resistance.** A real cell has emf $\varepsilon$ and internal resistance $r$. Terminal voltage $V = \varepsilon - Ir$ when delivering current $I$. Power delivered to external load: $P = IV = I(\varepsilon - Ir)$. Cells in series: total emf adds, internal resistances add. Cells in parallel (identical cells): emf unchanged, internal resistance divides by the number of cells.

**Kirchhoff's laws.**
- Junction rule (KCL): sum of currents into a node = sum of currents out.
- Loop rule (KVL): sum of emfs around a closed loop = sum of $IR$ drops.

**Wheatstone bridge.** Balance condition: $\dfrac{P}{Q} = \dfrac{R}{S}$. With a galvanometer between the midpoints, no current flows through it at balance. The metre bridge is just a Wheatstone bridge stretched along a wire, where $P/Q = l/(100 - l)$.

### 🟡 Standard — Exam Prep (3d-3w)

**Worked Example 1 — Series-parallel resistor network.**
Find the equivalent resistance between $A$ and $B$ in this network: $R_1 = 6\,\Omega$ and $R_2 = 3\,\Omega$ in parallel; that combination in series with $R_3 = 4\,\Omega$.
$R_{12} = \dfrac{6 \cdot 3}{6 + 3} = 2\,\Omega$. Then $R_{\text{eq}} = R_{12} + R_3 = 6\,\Omega$. Total current from a $12\,\text{V}$ source: $I = 12/6 = 2\,\text{A}$. Voltage across $R_3$: $V_3 = 2 \cdot 4 = 8\,\text{V}$. Voltage across the parallel pair: $12 - 8 = 4\,\text{V}$. Current through $R_1$: $4/6 = 0.667\,\text{A}$. Current through $R_2$: $4/3 = 1.333\,\text{A}$. Sum = $2\,\text{A}$ — checks against the total.

**Worked Example 2 — Kirchhoff with two loops and two sources.**
A circuit has emfs $\varepsilon_1 = 12\,\text{V}$ and $\varepsilon_2 = 6\,\text{V}$ with internal resistances $r_1 = 1\,\Omega$ and $r_2 = 1\,\Omega$, and a shared resistor $R = 4\,\Omega$. Find the currents in the two loops.
Set $I_1$ clockwise in loop 1 and $I_2$ counter-clockwise in loop 2 (so the branch shared by both carries $I_1 - I_2$). Loop 1 KVL: $\varepsilon_1 = I_1 r_1 + (I_1 - I_2)R$ → $12 = I_1 + 4(I_1 - I_2)$ → $12 = 5I_1 - 4I_2$. Loop 2 KVL: $-\varepsilon_2 = I_2 r_2 + (I_2 - I_1)R$ → $-6 = I_2 + 4(I_2 - I_1)$ → $-6 = 5I_2 - 4I_1$. Solving simultaneously: from the second equation $4I_1 = 5I_2 + 6$, so $I_1 = (5I_2 + 6)/4$. Substitute: $12 = 5 \cdot (5I_2 + 6)/4 - 4I_2 = (25I_2 + 30)/4 - 4I_2$. Multiply by 4: $48 = 25I_2 + 30 - 16I_2$ → $48 = 9I_2 + 30$ → $I_2 = 2\,\text{A}$. Then $I_1 = (10 + 6)/4 = 4\,\text{A}$. Current through shared branch = $I_1 - I_2 = 2\,\text{A}$.

**Worked Example 3 — Metre bridge.**
A metre bridge balances when the jockey is at $l = 40\,\text{cm}$ from the left end, with unknown resistance $X$ in the left gap and a known $R = 8\,\Omega$ in the right gap. Find $X$.
Balance condition: $\dfrac{X}{R} = \dfrac{l}{100 - l}$ → $X = R \cdot \dfrac{l}{100 - l} = 8 \cdot \dfrac{40}{60} = \dfrac{320}{60} \approx 5.33\,\Omega$. Power dissipated in $X$ when the bridge draws $I = 0.1\,\text{A}$ total: $P_X = I_X^2 X$, where $I_X = I \cdot \dfrac{60}{100}$ (current splits in inverse proportion to resistances in series). $I_X = 0.06\,\text{A}$, $P_X = 0.06^2 \cdot 5.33 \approx 0.0192\,\text{W}$.

**Practice Set.**
1. A copper wire of length $2\,\text{m}$ and cross-section $1\,\text{mm}^2$ has resistivity $1.7 \times 10^{-8}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$. Find its resistance. ($R = 0.034\,\Omega$.)
2. A $12\,\text{V}$ battery with internal resistance $0.5\,\Omega$ drives a $5.5\,\Omega$ load. Find the terminal voltage. ($V = 12 - (12/6) \cdot 0.5 = 11\,\text{V}$.)
3. Three $2\,\text{V}$ cells, each with $r = 0.2\,\Omega$, are connected in series and then connected to a $1\,\Omega$ external resistor. Find the current. ($\varepsilon = 6\,\text{V}$, total $r = 0.6\,\Omega$, $I = 6/(1+0.6) = 3.75\,\text{A}$.)

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (1mo+)

**Why Ohm's law fails.** A true Ohmic conductor has $V \propto I$ at constant temperature, so $V/I$ is constant. Filament bulbs, diodes, thermistors, and electrolytes are non-Ohmic — the $V$-$I$ graph curves because the resistivity itself depends on temperature, which depends on $I^2 R$ dissipation. For a metallic filament, $\rho$ rises roughly linearly with absolute temperature over a wide range, but the Joule heating shifts the operating point continuously as $I$ changes.

**Temperature coefficient from first principles.** For metals, lattice vibrations scatter conduction electrons. Mean free path $\ell$ depends on phonon density, which scales with absolute temperature $T$ at low to moderate $T$. Hence $\rho \propto T$, and $\alpha = \dfrac{1}{\rho}\dfrac{d\rho}{dT}$ is roughly constant. At very low $T$, residual resistivity from impurities dominates and $\alpha$ drops toward zero — the "residual resistance ratio" of a clean metal can reach $10^3$ between room temperature and $4.2\,\text{K}$.

**Internal resistance and maximum power transfer.** A real source with emf $\varepsilon$ and internal $r$ delivers $P = I^2 R_{\text{load}}$ where $I = \varepsilon/(r + R_{\text{load}})$. Maximum $P$ occurs when $R_{\text{load}} = r$ (the maximum-power-transfer theorem), giving $P_{\max} = \varepsilon^2/(4r)$. This is the engineering design choice for impedance matching in audio and RF circuits; battery designers deliberately keep $r$ low so most power goes to the load.

**Potentiometer principle.** A potentiometer measures emf without drawing current from the source: balance the unknown emf against a known voltage drop along a uniform resistance wire. Length at balance is proportional to emf: $\varepsilon_x = \varepsilon_{\text{std}} \cdot \dfrac{l_x}{l_{\text{std}}}$. The internal-resistance problem can be solved with a potentiometer by comparing the cell's emf (no current) with its terminal voltage when delivering current — the difference reveals $r$.

**RC transient as a bridge topic.** A capacitor charging through a resistor follows $Q(t) = Q_0(1 - e^{-t/RC})$ with $Q_0 = C\varepsilon$. Time constant $\tau = RC$ — the time to reach $1 - 1/e \approx 63.2\%$ of the final value. The same exponential governs capacitor discharge $Q(t) = Q_0 e^{-t/RC}$. This transient is half Electrostatics (the capacitor), half Current Electricity (the resistor), and shows up in MET NAT problems more often than students expect.

## Common traps

1. **Confusing $R_s$ and $R_p$.** For two resistors of $2\,\Omega$ and $8\,\Omega$, series gives $10\,\Omega$, parallel gives $1.6\,\Omega$. A wrong reading of the diagram inverts the answer by a factor of $>6$. Always read the network twice.
2. **Using $\varepsilon$ instead of $V$ in $P = VI$.** $\varepsilon$ is the open-circuit emf; the terminal voltage $V = \varepsilon - Ir$ is what actually appears across the load. Using $\varepsilon$ overestimates power delivered by exactly the factor $\varepsilon/V$.
3. **Sign errors in Kirchhoff loops.** When traversing a resistor in the assumed current direction, the drop is $+IR$; against the current, $-IR$. Crossing a battery from $-$ to $+$ inside gives $+\varepsilon$; from $+$ to $-$ gives $-\varepsilon$. Sign slips are the single most common reason for a wrong Kirchhoff answer.
4. **Misreading metre-bridge balance length.** The balance condition is $X/R = l/(100 - l)$, not $X/R = l/100$. If $l = 40\,\text{cm}$, the answer is $\dfrac{40}{60}$ of $R$, not $\dfrac{40}{100}$.
5. **Treating internal resistance as zero when it is not.** "A $6\,\text{V}$ battery" almost always means a real cell with $r > 0$. The terminal voltage under load is $\varepsilon - Ir$, not $\varepsilon$. In a circuit where $r$ is given (typically $0.1$–$1\,\Omega$), skipping it produces answers that are off by $\sim 10\%$.
