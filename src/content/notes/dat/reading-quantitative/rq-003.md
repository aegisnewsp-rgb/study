# Quantitative Reasoning: Arithmetic and Estimation

Quantitative Reasoning is 40 items and Reading Comprehension is 50 items in the DAT. Both come after the 30-minute scheduled break that follows the Perceptual Ability Test, and together with the earlier sections they fill a total administration time the ADA states as five hours and 15 minutes. The ADA Candidate Guide allocates 60 minutes to Reading Comprehension and 45 minutes to Quantitative Reasoning.

Forty items in 45 minutes is roughly 68 seconds each — about a minute and a bit. That is enough time for arithmetic done carefully and not enough time for arithmetic done twice, which makes estimation the most valuable habit in this section.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

**Estimate before you calculate.** Round each number to one significant figure, find the order of magnitude of the answer, and only then decide whether the question needs precision. This section rewards candidates who spend twenty seconds per item over candidates whose arithmetic is flawless and whose clock runs out.

| Task type | The move | Worked micro-case |
|---|---|---|
| Percentage of a number | Round the base, then apply the rate | 4% of 2,850 → 4% of 3,000 → 120 (exact 114) |
| Division | Round both, then divide | 62 ÷ 0.31 → 60 ÷ 0.3 → 200 (exact 200) |
| Percentage change | Multiply by the factor, never add to the base | +25% → ×1.25; −20% → ×0.80 |
| Ratio split | Find one part first, then multiply | A:B = 3:5, total 64 → one part = 8 |
| Unit conversion | Convert **before** computing | 60 mph → 88 ft/s, not 60 |
| Weighted average | Weight by the counts, never take the plain mean | 3 of 4 quarters worse than average → year below average |

**Memory hook — ROUND first, UNITS last.** R-round to one significant figure, O-order of magnitude against the options, U-units checked on the answer line, N-never recompute a correct answer, D-decimal point placed before selecting. The order matters: the unit check is the last thing you do, and it is the one that catches the error that costs a power of ten rather than a percentage.

**The cancelling-percentage rule.** An increase of *p* followed by a decrease of *q* returns to the starting value only when the decrease is q = p/(1 + p). That is why +25% then −20% lands exactly back on the original, while +20% then −20% leaves you 4% down — 100 rises to 120 and falls to 96.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Estimate before you calculate

Round every number to one significant figure before you touch it, work out the order of magnitude of the answer, and then decide whether the question needs precision at all.

"What is 4 percent of 2,850?" rounds to 4 percent of 3,000, which is 120. The exact answer is 114. Both round to about a hundred, and if the options are 54, 114, 1,140 and 11,400 you already have your answer.

"What is 62 divided by 0.31?" rounds to 60 over 0.3, which is 200. The exact answer is 200. Estimation found it.

A useful sanity rule for a multiple-choice test: if your calculated answer is not near any option, you have made an error somewhere rather than found a clever result. Options are usually spaced by a factor of a small number precisely so that order-of-magnitude errors are caught.

#### The four recurring types

**Percentage change.** Always state what the percentage is of. "Increased by 20 per cent, then decreased by 20 per cent" is not a return to the starting value, and that asymmetry is examined repeatedly: 100 rises to 120, and 20 per cent off 120 is 24, giving 96 — 4 per cent below the original. The fall is taken from the *larger* base, which is the entire reason the two rates do not cancel.

**Ratio and proportion.** Set up the ratio before substituting. "The ratio of A to B is 3 to 5 and the total is 64" means one part is 8, not that A is 3. Setting up the ratio costs ten seconds and prevents the errors that come from picking the wrong part.

**Rate problems.** Convert to a single unit before calculating, not after. Miles per hour to feet per second, dollars per hour to dollars per minute, grams per millilitre to grams per litre. In a timed section the conversion step is the step that gets skipped, and skipping it produces an answer wrong by a power of ten rather than by a percentage.

**Averages.** A weighted average is not the simple mean unless the weights are equal. If three of the four quarters were worse than average, the year is below average even if the simple mean of the four quarter-averages looks acceptable.

#### The cancelling-percentage rule, derived

The reason +20% then −20% does not return you to the start is that the second percentage is taken from a *larger* base than the first. An increase of *p* multiplies by (1 + p); a subsequent decrease of *q* multiplies by (1 − q). The product is 1 only when

> **(1 + p) × (1 − q) = 1  ⟹  q = p / (1 + p)**

| Increase | Decrease that cancels it | Round-trip result |
|---|---|---|
| +10% | 9.09% | returns exactly to the start |
| +20% | 16.67% | returns exactly to the start |
| +25% | 20.00% | returns exactly to the start |
| +20% | 20% | 4% below the start (100 → 120 → 96) |
| +50% | 50% | 25% below the start (100 → 150 → 75) |

The larger the increase, the more the required cancelling decrease falls short of the naive guess of "the same number". For +50% the honest cancelling decrease is 33.3%, not 50% — a 50% gain needs a one-third loss to undo it.

#### Estimation is not guessing

An estimate you can defend is an answer. Calculate two order-of-magnitude bounds — this is roughly a hundred, roughly a thousand, roughly ten — and pick the option inside them. If two options fall in the same decade, estimate again with more care or calculate properly.

The discipline that makes this reliable is to check the units of your answer before selecting an option. Forty items are not enough to absorb the candidates whose arithmetic is fine and whose units are wrong.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Worked item 1 — the percentage round trip

**Question.** A supplier's price rises by 25% and then falls by 20%. The original price was $480. What is the price now, and by what percentage does it differ from $480?

**Step 1 — never add to the base.** $480 × 1.25 = $600. The 25% is $120, added to $480.

**Step 2 — the fall is taken from the new base.** $600 × 0.80 = $480.

**Step 3 — compare.** $480 against $480: the price is back exactly where it started.

> ⚡ **Examiner Trap.** The obvious answer is "$24 higher", from doing 25% − 20% = 5%. The two percentages are taken from different bases, so subtracting them is meaningless. Check the item for this shape before doing any arithmetic: if a rise of *p* is followed by a fall of *q*, the round trip is neutral exactly when q = p/(1+p) — and here p = 25%, so the cancelling fall is 20%. The question was constructed to reward the candidate who derives the rule rather than the one who subtracts the rates.

#### Worked item 2 — the decimal-point item

**Question.** A solution is made by dissolving 4.5 g of solute in 250 mL of water. What is the concentration in grams per litre?

**Step 1 — convert volume first.** 250 mL = 0.250 L. Convert before computing, never after.

**Step 2 — divide.** 4.5 g ÷ 0.250 L = 18 g/L.

**Step 3 — sanity-check the order of magnitude.** Four and a half grams in a quarter of a litre must land near 18, not near 0.018 or 1800.

> ⚡ **Examiner Trap.** Computing 4.5 ÷ 250 first gives 0.018, then "fixing" it by multiplying by 1000 at the end invites a second error. Converting the unit once, up front, removes the class of mistake entirely. Options in this item type are typically 0.018, 1.8, 18 and 1,800 — they are one decimal shift apart on purpose, and a single lost shift selects a neighbour.

#### Worked item 3 — the weighted average

**Question.** A student's three subject scores are 70, 80 and 90, weighted 1, 2 and 3 respectively. What is the weighted average?

**Step 1 — multiply by the weights.** 70×1 = 70; 80×2 = 160; 90×3 = 270.

**Step 2 — sum.** 70 + 160 + 270 = 500.

**Step 3 — divide by the sum of the weights, not by three.** 500 ÷ (1+2+3) = 500 ÷ 6 = 83.3.

The plain mean of the three scores is 80. The weighted answer is higher, because the highest score carries the largest weight — the discrepancy between 80 and 83.3 is the whole point of the item.

> ⚡ **Examiner Trap.** Dividing 500 by 3 instead of by 6 gives 166.7, which is not even on the 0–100 scale the scores came from. Always check that a weighted average returns a value inside the range of its inputs; when it does not, the divisor is wrong.

#### Estimating versus calculating — when to stop estimating

| Signal in the question | Correct move | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| "Approximately" / "closest to" | Estimate, select, move on | Precision is not what is being tested |
| Two options in the same decade | Calculate properly | Estimation cannot separate them |
| Options spread over decades | Estimate only | The calculator work buys nothing |
| A formula with named variables | Calculate, then estimate-check | The formula is the tested skill |
| Units stated in the question | Convert first, always | A late conversion loses a power of ten |

The decision rule is narrow and worth memorising: **estimate to choose between decades, calculate to choose within one.** Twenty of forty items in a typical sitting fall into the first category, and those are the items that fund the time spent on the second.

#### ⚡ Quick Exam Checklist

- Rounded to one significant figure before any arithmetic
- Order of magnitude computed and matched against the option spread
- Percentage applied as a multiplier, never added to the base
- Ratio split by finding one part, not by guessing which part
- Units converted *before* substituting, and re-checked on the answer line
- Weighted averages divided by the sum of the weights
- Round-trip percentages tested against q = p/(1 + p)
- No correct answer recomputed for a cleaner-looking number
