# MM2 Sequences and Series

MM4 on the specification's numbering sits behind MM3, but it is one of the four families with the least content and the most pattern. The specification asks for four things: sequences given either by a formula for the $n$th term or by a simple recurrence of the form $x_{n+1} = f(x_n)$; arithmetic series including the sum of the first $n$ natural numbers; the sum of a finite geometric series and the sum to infinity when it converges; and the binomial expansion of $(1+x)^n$ and of expressions like $(a + f(x))^n$, together with the notations $n!$ and $\binom{r}{n}$.

Read the last one carefully. Positive integer $n$ only, and *simple* $f(x)$. That is a much narrower promise than "the binomial theorem", and it is what tells you how hard the questions will be.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> Nothing in MM2 needs invention. Each strand has one formula, and the skill is recognising which formula.

| Situation | The formula | Condition to check |
|---|---|---|
| $n$th term given | Substitute $n$ | Make sure you start at $n=1$ |
| $x_{n+1} = f(x_n)$ given | Work forward one term at a time | Keep a table; do not try to find a pattern too early |
| Arithmetic series, $n$ terms | $S = \tfrac{n}{2}\big[2a + (n-1)d\big]$ | Constant difference |
| Sum of first $n$ naturals | $1+2+\cdots+n = \tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$ | The $d = 1$ case of the above |
| Geometric series, $n$ terms | $S = \dfrac{a(1 - r^n)}{1 - r}$ | Constant ratio, $r \ne 1$ |
| Geometric, to infinity | $S = \dfrac{a}{1-r}$ | Converges only when $\lvert r \rvert < 1$ |
| Binomial | $\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}a^{n-r}b^r$, $\binom{n}{r} = \dfrac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}$ | $n$ a positive integer |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Sequences: two descriptions, not one

A sequence can be given to you as a **position-to-term** rule or a **term-to-term** rule, and the two describe the same object from opposite ends. If $u_n = n^2 + 1$, then $u_1 = 2$, $u_2 = 5$, $u_3 = 10$, $u_4 = 17$ — write out a few terms and the differences ($3, 5, 7$) tell you it is not arithmetic, while the second differences ($2, 2$) tell you it is quadratic.

If instead you are given $x_{n+1} = f(x_n)$, there is nothing to derive. You are handed a starting value and a machine. With $x_{n+1} = 3x_n - 1$ and $x_1 = 2$: $x_2 = 5$, $x_3 = 14$, $x_4 = 41$, $x_5 = 122$. Keep a table.

That example hides something worth seeing. The differences are $3, 9, 27, 81$ — themselves geometric with ratio 3. Whenever you meet $x_{n+1} = ax_n + b$ with $a \ne 1$, the differences are geometric with ratio $a$, and the terms have a closed form $x_n = \frac{b}{1-a} + \left(x_1 - \frac{b}{1-a}\right)a^{n-1}$. Here that is $\frac{-1}{-2} + \left(2 - \tfrac12\right)3^{n-1} = \tfrac12 + \tfrac32 \cdot 3^{n-1}$, and $n=3$ gives $\tfrac12 + \tfrac{27}{2} = 14$ ✓.

#### Arithmetic series, and a derivation worth being able to reproduce

The sum of the first $n$ natural numbers is $\tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$, and the derivation is three lines. Write $S = 1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots + n$, then write it again reversed, $S = n + (n-1) + \cdots + 2 + 1$, and add the two columns: every column totals $n+1$, and there are $n$ columns, so $2S = n(n+1)$ and $S = \tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$. Paper 2 asks candidates to construct and analyse arguments (Prf1, Prf3), so a five-line proof you can reproduce is worth more than a formula you half-remember.

The general arithmetic series with first term $a$ and common difference $d$ has $n$ terms with last term $l = a + (n-1)d$, and

$$S = \frac{n}{2}(a + l) = \frac{n}{2}\big[2a + (n-1)d\big]$$

Both forms are worth having: $S = \tfrac{n(a+l)}{2}$ when you are given the last term, $S = \tfrac{n}{2}[2a+(n-1)d]$ when you are given $a$ and $d$.

#### Geometric series, finite and infinite

Multiplying $S = a + ar + ar^2 + \cdots + ar^{n-1}$ by $r$ and subtracting cancels everything except the ends:

$$rS - S = ar^n - a \implies S = \frac{a(r^n - 1)}{r - 1} = \frac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}$$

For $r = 1$ that formula divides by zero, correctly: every term is $a$, so $S = na$. Handle that case before reaching for the fraction.

**The sum to infinity** is the limit of the finite sum as $n \to \infty$. It exists only when $|r| < 1$, because the term $r^n$ must tend to zero. The specification phrases this as $r < 1$; the honest condition is $|r| < 1$, and the distinction is exactly the trap. A ratio of $-2$ satisfies $r < 1$ yet produces the divergent series $1 - 2 + 4 - 8 + \cdots$, whose partial sums $1, -1, 3, -5$ run away from everything. So:

$$\text{Sum to infinity} = \frac{a}{1-r}, \quad \text{valid only for } |r| < 1$$

Worked: $1 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac19 + \cdots$ has $a = 1$, $r = \tfrac13$, so the sum is $\dfrac{1}{1-\frac13} = \tfrac32$. One line, and it is a whole question.

#### The binomial expansion

$$\left(1+x\right)^n = 1 + nx + \binom{n}{2}x^2 + \cdots + x^n$$

For $n=5$ that gives $1 + 5x + 10x^2 + 10x^3 + 5x^4 + x^5$. The coefficients $1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1$ are read off Pascal's triangle, or from $\binom{n}{r} = \dfrac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}$, and they are symmetric because $\binom{n}{r} = \binom{n}{n-r}$.

For the general case $(a + f(x))^n$, with $\binom{n}{r} = \dfrac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}$:

$$\left(2+3x\right)^4 = 16 + 96x + 216x^2 + 216x^3 + 81x^4$$

Check each coefficient: $\binom40 2^4 = 16$; $\binom41 2^3 (3) = 4\cdot 8\cdot 3 = 96$; $\binom42 2^2(3^2) = 6\cdot 4\cdot 9 = 216$; $\binom43 2(27) = 4\cdot 2\cdot 27 = 216$; $\binom44 81 = 81$. A question asking only for *the coefficient of $x^2$* is one binomial coefficient and one multiplication — that is the shape most TMUA items take.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (3mo+)

#### The two sum formulae are one picture

Arithmetic series and geometric series are the discrete analogues of a straight line and an exponential under a measure called volume. Both formulae come from multiplying and subtracting, and in both cases the trick is that the middle terms cancel. Once you have seen that mechanism once you will never again need to be told which formula to use: if the *ratios* of terms are constant you are geometric, if the *differences* are constant you are arithmetic.

#### Partial sums of a geometric series are exact, not approximate

Finite partial sums let you do things a calculator would otherwise do. If $S_m$ and $S_n$ are the sums of the first $m$ and first $n$ terms of a geometric series with $|r|<1$, then $S_\infty - S_m = \dfrac{ar^m}{1-r}$ — the tail. So $\dfrac{S_{10}}{S_\infty} = 1 - r^{10}$ exactly, with no approximation error at all. Questions that give a truncated sum and ask you to infer $r$ are working in this space.

#### Where the routine runs out

Three things break the pattern. A sequence whose terms are neither arithmetic nor geometric needs the $n$th-term formula rather than any sum rule — $u_n = \dfrac{n}{n+1}$ has to be summed term by term with a partial-fraction trick or the answer is given. A recurrence like $x_{n+1} = x_n^2$ looks trivial and grows explosively, so the answer is usually $x_2 = 1$, $x_3 = 1$ and then constant, and candidates who assume growth miss it. And a ratio that is very close to $1$ makes the sum to infinity enormous — $\tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac18 + \cdots$ totals $1$, but the same series starting with ratio $\tfrac9{10}$ totals $10$, and rounding $r$ too early destroys the answer.

### Common mistakes

Applying an arithmetic formula to geometric terms, or the reverse.

Using $r < 1$ instead of $|r| < 1$ and accepting a divergent series with a negative ratio.

Ignoring the $r = 1$ case, where $S = na$ and the fraction is undefined.

Off-by-one in the last term: $n$ terms of an arithmetic sequence ending at $l$ means $l = a + (n-1)d$, not $a + nd$.

Reading a recurrence $x_{n+1} = f(x_n)$ as though it were an $n$th-term formula.

Confusing $\binom{n}{r}$ with $n!/r!$ alone and forgetting the $(n-r)!$.

Dropping a power in $(a + f(x))^n$ — remember the $r$th term carries $a^{n-r}$.

Rounding a ratio before using the sum-to-infinity formula.
