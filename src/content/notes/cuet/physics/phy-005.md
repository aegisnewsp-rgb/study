# Work Energy Power

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Work is the scalar transfer of energy by a force acting through a displacement, measured in joules (J). Energy is the capacity to do work, and power is the time-rate of doing work, measured in watts (W = J/s). The three ideas are tied together by the work–energy theorem: net work equals the change in kinetic energy.

- **Work:** $W = F d \cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between **F** (N) and **d** (m). Sign depends on direction: positive when force aids motion, zero when perpendicular (e.g., centripetal force), negative when opposing motion (e.g., friction, gravity on a rising body).
- **Kinetic energy:** $K = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2$ (J), with $m$ in kg and $v$ in m/s.
- **Work–energy theorem:** $W_{net} = \Delta K = K_f - K_i$.
- **Power:** $P_{avg} = W/t$; instantaneous $P = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v}$. Commercial unit of electrical energy: 1 kWh = $3.6 \times 10^6$ J.

**High-yield CUET pointers:** sign of work, conservation of mechanical energy in spring/vertical-loop problems, and power of vehicles climbing inclines.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Definitions and Sign Convention

Work is defined only when a body undergoes a displacement under a force. $W = F d \cos\theta$ gives +ve, −ve, or zero work depending on whether $\theta$ is acute, obtuse, or $90°$. A student holding a heavy bag while walking on a flat road does zero work on the bag because the applied force is vertical and displacement is horizontal.

Kinetic energy $K = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ is always non-negative and frame-dependent. Potential energy is defined only for **conservative forces** (gravity, spring force, electrostatic). For these, $W_{conservative} = -\Delta U$, where $U$ is potential energy.

#### Work–Energy Theorem

The theorem states $W_{net} = \Delta K$. Crucially, **only the net work** of all forces equals $\Delta K$, not the work of any single force. If you want the work of one specific force, write $W_i = \Delta K - \sum_{j\neq i} W_j$.

#### Energy Conservation

Total mechanical energy $E = K + U$ stays constant when no non-conservative force (friction, air drag, muscular push) does net work. On a frictionless incline of length $L$ and vertical height $h$, the gravity work is $mg \cdot h$, **not** $mg \cdot L$, because $\Delta U = mg\Delta h$ is path-independent.

| Concept | Formula / Rule | Key point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Work | $W = F d \cos\theta$ | Zero if $d = 0$ or $\theta = 90°$ |
| Kinetic energy | $K = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2$ | Always ≥ 0 |
| Gravitational PE | $U_g = m g h$ | Reference level is arbitrary |
| Spring PE | $U_s = \tfrac{1}{2} k x^2$ | $x$ measured from natural length |
| Work–energy theorem | $W_{net} = \Delta K$ | Uses net work, not individual |
| Average power | $P_{avg} = W/t$ | SI unit: watt (J/s) |
| Instantaneous power | $P = F v \cos\theta$ | Time-varying work rate |

#### Typical CUET Patterns

- Find work done by gravity over a curved path given only height change.
- Apply $W_{net} = \Delta K$ to a block pulled across a surface with friction.
- Compute power of a pump lifting water at a given mass flow rate.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Conservative vs Non-Conservative Forces

A force is conservative if the work done between two points is path-independent. Gravity and the spring force are conservative; kinetic friction is the textbook example of a non-conservative force because $W_{friction} = -\mu_k N d$ depends on the actual path length, not just endpoints. Non-conservative forces convert mechanical energy into internal energy (heat, sound, deformation), so $E_{mechanical}$ drops by exactly the magnitude of work done against them.

#### Worked Numerical

A 2 kg block slides down a frictionless incline from height $h = 5$ m. Using energy conservation, $K_{bottom} = m g h = 2 \times 9.8 \times 5 = 98$ J. Equating to $\tfrac{1}{2} m v^2$ gives $v = \sqrt{2 g h} = \sqrt{98} \approx 9.9$ m/s. Power delivered by gravity at the instant speed is $v$ is $P = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v} = m g v \cos 0° = 2 \times 9.8 \times 9.9 \approx 194$ W.

#### Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
| --- | --- |
| Saying "gravity does work $mgL$ down an incline of length $L$" | Gravity work is $mgh$, where $h$ is vertical height |
| Treating work–energy theorem per force | Only $W_{net} = \Delta K$; individual $W_i$ need not |
| Setting $U_s = \tfrac{1}{2} k x$ | Correct expression is $\tfrac{1}{2} k x^2$ |
| Using kWh and joule interchangeably | 1 kWh = $3.6 \times 10^6$ J; convert before substituting |
| Taking power as $W \times t$ | Power is $W/t$, dimensionally J/s |

#### Edge Cases and Adjacent Links

1. Variable force: when **F** is not constant along the path, $W = \int \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{s}$. The spring force $F = -kx$ is the standard worked case, giving $W_{spring} = \tfrac{1}{2} k (x_i^2 - x_f^2)$.
2. Vertical circular motion: combine $W_{net} = \Delta K$ with centripetal condition $mv^2/r = T + mg\cos\theta$ to solve rope-tension problems at top and bottom.
3. Power of vehicles: maximum climb angle satisfies $P_{engine} = m g v \sin\theta + F_{resistance} v$.

**Exam strategy:** CUET UG Physics Section B carries 40 questions, of which Work-Energy-Power contributes 1–2 MCQs and 2–3 numericals, often fused with springs or circular motion. Solve energy-conservation problems first when friction is absent — they bypass the need for detailed kinematics.

#### Practice Prompts

1. A 1500 kg car climbs a 10° incline at constant 36 km/h against a resistive force of 500 N. Find the engine power output.
2. A spring ($k = 200$ N/m) is compressed by 0.1 m and releases a 0.5 kg ball. Using energy conservation, find the launch speed.
