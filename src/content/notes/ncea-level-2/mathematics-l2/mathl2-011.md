# Evaluate a statistically based report (91266) — NCEA Level 2 Mathematics Notes

Achievement Standard 91266 is a small internal standard (2 credits) that tests critical evaluation of a statistical report. Students are given a published report (from a newspaper, magazine, government publication, or website) and asked to assess the source, sampling, data quality, statistical techniques used, and the validity of the conclusions. The standard is a bridge between doing statistics and reading statistics — essential for media literacy.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **2 credits**, internally assessed by the school, NZQA moderated.
- Topics: source credibility, sampling method, data collection, summary statistics used, appropriateness of statistical techniques, validity of conclusions, identification of bias, recognition of misleading graphs.
- **Merit** requires relational thinking — linking the methodology to the conclusion's validity.
- **Excellence** requires extended abstract thinking — comparing alternative methodologies, reflecting on the broader implications of the report's findings.

#### Examiner traps

- Accepting the report at face value — the whole point is critical evaluation, not description.
- Confusing the sampling method with the data collection method.
- Skipping the limitations section of the report (if any).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Source credibility

A credible report:

- Identifies the source (organisation, author, date).
- Has been peer-reviewed or published in a reputable outlet.
- Discloses funding sources and conflicts of interest.
- Provides enough detail for the reader to evaluate the methodology.

Question the report if:

- The source is anonymous or unidentified.
- The funding source has a clear stake in the outcome.
- The report is on a website with no editorial oversight.
- The report does not provide enough detail to evaluate the methodology.

#### Sampling method

The sampling method determines whether the sample is representative of the population. Evaluate:

- Was the sampling method appropriate for the research question?
- Was the sample size large enough to support the conclusions?
- Was there selection bias (e.g., the report only surveyed people who volunteered)?
- Were subgroups (e.g., age, gender, region) represented in the sample?

#### Data collection

- Was the data collection method appropriate (survey, observation, measurement, experiment)?
- Were the questions unbiased?
- Was the response rate high enough to avoid non-response bias?
- Were the measurements calibrated and reliable?

#### Statistical techniques

- Were the summary statistics appropriate for the data type (e.g., median for skewed data, mean for symmetric data)?
- Were the displays appropriate (e.g., bar chart for categorical, histogram for continuous)?
- Were any statistical inferences made (confidence intervals, hypothesis tests)? If so, were the assumptions met?
- Was correlation mistaken for causation?

#### Conclusions

- Are the conclusions supported by the data?
- Are the conclusions limited to the population from which the sample was drawn?
- Do the conclusions acknowledge limitations (sample size, methodology, generalisability)?
- Are the conclusions presented with appropriate uncertainty (e.g., confidence intervals)?

#### Misleading graphs

Common tricks:

- Truncated y-axis (the bar starts at 50 instead of 0, exaggerating the difference).
- Inappropriate scale (log scale presented as linear, or vice versa).
- 3D pie charts (the perspective distorts the angles).
- Cherry-picking (showing only the data that supports the conclusion).

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### The "so what" test

A report passes the "so what" test if its conclusions have practical implications or change a decision. A report that simply describes the sample without connecting to a broader population or a decision has limited value.

The Excellence move is to identify whether the report's conclusions have practical implications, and whether those implications are supported by the data.

#### Comparative evaluation

For Excellence, compare the report's methodology with an alternative:

- "The report used a convenience sample of 200 students. A stratified sample of 1000 students across year groups and schools would have given a more representative picture of the population."
- "The report presented the median income. The mean would have been more appropriate if the data was symmetric; if it was skewed (which is typical for income), the median was the right choice."

#### Bias in the data

Bias can creep in at every stage:

- Selection bias (who is in the sample).
- Measurement bias (how the data is collected).
- Response bias (how respondents answer).
- Publication bias (only significant or interesting results are published).
- Survivorship bias (only the successes are counted).

The Excellence move is to identify which biases are present in the report and how they affect the conclusions.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Any published report is credible." No — evaluate the source, the methodology, and the conclusions.
- "Statistics always tell the truth." Statistics can be manipulated, misrepresented, or misinterpreted.
- "Sample size doesn't matter." It does — small samples give imprecise estimates.
- "A conclusion that matches my prior is correct." Confirmation bias is the enemy of critical evaluation.
