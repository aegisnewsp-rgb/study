# Elasticity

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Elasticity of demand** measures how quantity demanded responds to a change in price, income, or the price of a related good. It is expressed as a **ratio of percentage changes**, so the unit of measurement cancels out.

The three core variants are:

- **Price Elasticity of Demand (PED or Ep)** = (%ΔQ) / (%ΔP). Always read as a positive number; the law of demand makes Ep negative in raw form.
- **Income Elasticity (YED or Ey)** = (%ΔQ) / (%ΔY). Positive for normal goods, negative for inferior goods.
- **Cross Elasticity (XED or Ec)** = (%ΔQx) / (%ΔPy). Positive for substitutes, negative for complements.

Five canonical bands classify PED: **perfectly elastic (∞), elastic (>1), unitary (=1), inelastic (<1), perfectly inelastic (0)**. UPPSC PCS economics questions frequently test these bands alongside the **Total Revenue Test** (when Ep > 1, TR rises as price falls; TR peaks where Ep = 1).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Definition and Formula

Economists define elasticity as the ratio of the percentage change in the dependent variable (quantity demanded) to the percentage change in the independent variable. For price elasticity, the standard formula is:

> **Ep = (% change in Q) / (% change in P) = (ΔQ/Q) × (P/ΔP)**

Here Q is original quantity demanded (in units), P is original price (in ₹), ΔQ is the change in quantity, and ΔP is the change in price. The negative sign from the inverse demand relationship is dropped by convention, so we read Ep as an absolute value.

#### Point vs Arc Elasticity

**Point elasticity** is calculated at a specific point on a continuous demand curve using calculus: Ep = (dQ/dP) × (P/Q). **Arc elasticity** is used for discrete jumps between two price-quantity combinations and uses averages to avoid base-year bias:

> **Arc Ep = [(Q₂ − Q₁)/(Q₂ + Q₁)] / [(P₂ − P₁)/(P₂ + P₁)]**

This distinction matters in UPPSC numerical problems where a price change of more than a few per cent is involved.

#### Classification Table

| Demand Type | Ep Value | Slope characteristic |
|---|---|---|
| Perfectly elastic | ∞ | Horizontal demand curve |
| Elastic | > 1 | Relatively flat curve |
| Unitary | = 1 | TR at maximum |
| Inelastic | < 1 | Relatively steep curve |
| Perfectly inelastic | = 0 | Vertical demand curve |

#### TR–MR–Elasticity Linkage

The **Total Revenue Test** ties elasticity directly to firm revenue. Total revenue is TR = P × Q (₹). Marginal revenue relates to price by:

> **MR = P [1 − (1/Ep)]**

When Ep > 1, MR > 0 and TR rises as price falls; when Ep < 1, MR < 0 and TR falls as price falls; TR is maximised when Ep = 1. UPPSC questions often present a TR schedule and ask the candidate to identify the elasticity band at each price.

#### Determinants and Policy Use

The determinants of PED include the availability of close substitutes, the proportion of income spent on the good, whether it is a necessity or luxury, the time horizon (long-run elasticity exceeds short-run elasticity), the number of uses, and habit formation. Government policies on **taxation, subsidies, price ceilings, public utility pricing, agricultural MSP, and import-export tariffs** all rest on elasticity assumptions.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Income and Cross Elasticity in Depth

Income elasticity classifies goods precisely. **Ey > 1** defines luxury goods (demand rises faster than income); **0 < Ey < 1** defines necessity goods (Engel curve flatter than 45°); **Ey < 0** defines inferior goods. Cross elasticity distinguishes substitutes (Ec > 0, e.g., tea and coffee) from complements (Ec < 0, e.g., cars and petrol). A common trap is treating all inferior goods as having Ey < 0 — by definition that is true, but examiners may ask for the supplementary classification of **negative income elasticity** versus the contrapositive "Engel aggregation".

#### Edge Cases and Curve Derivation

The Marshallian demand curve is derived from the **Price Consumption Curve (PCC)** traced across an indifference map, holding income, tastes, and related prices constant. Similarly, the **Income Consumption Curve (ICC)** maps the bundle chosen as income changes at constant prices, and rotating its coordinates yields the **Engel curve** whose slope encodes Ey. For linear demand Q = a − bP, point elasticity Ep = (P/Q) × (−1/b), which varies along the curve — it equals unity at the midpoint and shifts towards zero or infinity at the axes, illustrating why elasticity is preferable to slope.

#### Common Mistakes in UPPSC Answers

1. Conflating elasticity with slope: a linear demand curve has constant slope but variable elasticity except in the special isoelastic case Q = k/P.
2. Dropping the absolute-value convention and reporting negative Ep.
3. Confusing TR movement with profit or total cost in MR–Ep problems.
4. Choosing the wrong formula for small versus large price changes.
5. Assuming perfectly inelastic demand means quantity demanded is zero — it only means Q is unresponsive to P.

#### Practice Prompts

- **Numerical:** Price falls from ₹40 to ₹30, quantity rises from 200 to 260 units. Compute arc Ep and predict whether TR rises or falls.
- **Descriptive:** "Examine how elasticity of demand guides the government in fixing agricultural procurement prices." Support with at least three reasoned points.
