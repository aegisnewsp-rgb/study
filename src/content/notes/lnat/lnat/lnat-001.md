# Section A — Reading the Passage for What It Says — LNAT Notes

Section A of the LNAT gives you 42 multiple-choice questions drawn from 12 argumentative passages — three or four questions on each — to answer in 95 minutes. Each passage argues a position rather than narrates an event, so the sentence that settles any given question is a claim, a concession or a piece of evidence, not a description. The 95-minute wall across 12 passages means you cannot read all of them carefully; the students who score well work the passages instead of studying them. The fastest way to lose a Section A mark is to answer from memory of the passage after skimming it, because an option that is true in the world but unsupported by the printed words is still wrong.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### What the passage actually is

- **Argumentative, not narrative.** Each passage argues a position. Sentences do work: claim, support, concession, counter-argument.
- **Three or four questions per passage.** The 42 questions cluster tightly. A wrong read on one passage costs you three or four marks, not one.
- **Four answer options per question.** Confirmed by the LNAT Consortium on the live practice test page at lnat.ac.uk.

#### The reading protocol in one minute

- **Stem first.** Read the question stem before the passage. The stem names what the question is hunting.
- **Find the load-bearing sentence.** After the stem, read with one task: locate the single sentence the question turns on. For an "according to the passage" question it is the sentence that states the fact. For an inference it is the line that contains the gap the question wants you to bridge.
- **Answer from the printed words.** If an option needs information the passage never gave you, it is wrong, even if it sounds sensible in the world.

#### Time budget that actually fits

- **Roughly 95 minutes ÷ 12 passages ≈ 7–8 minutes per passage** including its three or four questions.
- **Spend under 90 seconds per question** on average. If you are over 90 seconds, mark the option you would defend and move on — you can review during the 95-minute window.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### The three sentence-types you hunt for

Most Section A questions resolve around one of three sentence-types in the passage. Learning to recognise them changes the read from a hunt through prose into a hunt through a structure.

**The claim.** The single sentence that says what the author is arguing. The topic sentence of the first or second paragraph. Sometimes stated in softer language ("It may be that…", "There is a case for…") — softer language does not mean a weaker claim; the test still treats it as the position.

**The concession.** A sentence that acknowledges the strongest point against the claim. Introduced by "of course", "admittedly", "to be sure". A concession is not a refutation; the author returns to the claim immediately after. Section A questions frequently ask what the author concedes, or whether a candidate counter-argument is granted.

**The evidence.** A sentence that supplies an example, a statistic, an analogy or a piece of reasoning the claim rests on. Evidence sentences carry the most marks in "according to the passage" questions because the answer is literally in those lines.

Once you can classify each sentence as claim, support, concession, or counter, the questions reduce to: which sentence does the stem point at, and what does that sentence actually say.

#### Stem-first, not passage-first

Reading the passage first is the single most expensive habit a Section A student can keep. Two problems follow from it.

First, the 12 passages are long and the 95-minute budget is short. A full careful read burns four or five minutes per passage and leaves nothing for the questions that need rereading.

Second, the read is unfocused. Without the stem you are storing every claim, concession and example because you do not know which one the question will turn on. Half of what you remembered you will not need, and the half you did need — the sentence the question turns on — is the part your memory is least likely to have preserved cleanly.

The protocol that fixes both: read the stem, name what the stem is hunting (a stated fact, an inference, an assumption, the author's concession), then read the passage once with that hunt in mind. The right sentence announces itself because you know what you are looking for.

#### Worked mini-passage and questions

Read it once with the protocol: stem first, then the passage. Allow about three minutes.

**Passage A.** *Many cities have introduced low-traffic neighbourhoods, where through-traffic is restricted and residential streets are returned to people on foot and bicycles. The policy is often framed as a clean gain: less pollution, safer streets, stronger neighbourhood ties. In practice the displacement effect is well documented — traffic does not vanish, it relocates to boundary roads, where residents of those roads absorb the noise and fumes that the protected streets no longer see. Proponents respond that boundary roads are a smaller total share of the street network, so the aggregate harm is lower. That claim assumes the harm a boundary resident suffers is comparable to the harm a previously through-trafficked resident used to suffer, which is plausible but not measured. What is missing from the debate is any account of who already lives on the boundary roads. In cities where the boundary roads run through poorer districts, the displacement effect is, in plain terms, a transfer of pollution from richer streets to poorer ones.*

**Q1.** According to the passage, low-traffic neighbourhoods have been criticised for

A. failing to reduce total traffic in a city.
B. shifting traffic to streets that may be in poorer areas.
C. raising the share of boundary roads in the street network.
D. producing cleaner air in protected streets than was expected.

**Reasoning.** Stem hunts for what the passage says the criticism is. The displacement paragraph and the final paragraph both name the criticism: traffic relocates to boundary roads, and where boundary roads run through poorer districts the policy transfers pollution from richer streets to poorer ones. Option B states exactly that. Option A misreads "displacement" as "no reduction at all". Option C is a misreading of "smaller total share" as "larger share". Option D contradicts the passage — protected streets did get cleaner air. Answer: **B**.

**Q2.** The author describes the proponents' response ("That claim assumes…") as

A. a refutation supported by new evidence.
B. a concession whose underlying assumption is left unmeasured.
C. a counter-argument that the author endorses.
D. a flawed analogy between two kinds of harm.

**Reasoning.** Stem hunts for how the author frames the proponents' response. The author calls the response plausible but says the underlying assumption has not been measured. Option B says the same thing. Option A invents "new evidence" the passage never supplied. Option C misreads the author's framing as endorsement. Option D invents the word "analogy" — the passage compares two cases but does not call the response an analogy. Answer: **B**.

#### What to do when two options both look defensible

This is the most common place to lose time. Two options survive your first pass. The mistake is to re-read the passage from the top. The right move is to name what each option is doing relative to the passage.

- **One restates, one goes further than the passage can carry.** Pick the one that stays inside the printed words. If an option would still be defensible without the passage, it is probably too far.
- **Both restate, but one matches a stronger word than the passage uses.** Pick the one with the weaker word. "Often" beats "always" when the passage says "often".
- **Both go further.** Pick the one that names the smallest leap. Two small leaps are safer than one large one.

The naming step is what stops the 90-second re-read loop. If you can say which option overreaches and why, you do not need to read the passage again to confirm.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Why this skill transfers

Section A is, structurally, a reading test disguised as a multiple choice. The Consortium's stated purpose is to test the intellectual skills that matter in a law degree: following an argument, weighing evidence, separating what an author claims from what they assume. The reading protocol above trains those skills directly, because each step (stem first, classify the sentence, answer from the printed words, name the overreach) maps onto a single underlying habit — answer from the passage, not from your view of the world.

That habit transfers beyond the LNAT into case reading, statute reading and the kind of dense academic prose first-year undergraduates meet. The skill is not "answer 42 questions in 95 minutes"; the skill is reading an argument under time pressure without substituting your own views for what the writer actually said.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- **"I should read the passage first because I need to understand it before I can answer."** The stem tells you what understanding you need. Reading without the stem is reading for context you will not use.
- **"Faster readers always score higher."** Speed without focus is wasted. Students who score highest on Section A tend to read at average speed but know exactly which sentence they are hunting for.
- **"An option that sounds reasonable must be the right one."** Section A is marked against the passage, not against the world. An option that is reasonable but unsupported by the printed words costs the mark.
- **"Concession sentences are weaknesses in the argument."** A concession is a marker that the author has acknowledged the strongest objection. Section A frequently asks what the author concedes, and the right answer is the concession, not a sentence that ignores it.
- **"If I run out of time I should guess at random."** Unanswered questions score zero; an uninformed guess also scores zero. Mark your best option for every question as you go, and use the review window inside the 95 minutes to revisit the ones you were unsure about.

#### Practice routine that builds the protocol

A useful week of Section A practice looks like this. Sit one timed passage per day — three or four questions, eight minutes — and after marking write one line for each wrong option explaining which sentence-type it mishandled (claimed, support, concession, counter). After five passages you will see the same failure types repeat. The repetition is the lesson. Move on to two passages a day when the eight-minute target is comfortable, then three. The goal is not to do more passages faster; it is to do each passage with the same protocol under heavier time pressure.

Once a week, sit a full Section A under the real clock — 12 passages, 42 questions, 95 minutes, no pauses. Mark it out of 42 and write one line on each wrong answer. The number you start from is the only baseline you will get. Re-check current timings, essay counts and access arrangements on https://lnat.ac.uk/ before committing to a plan.
