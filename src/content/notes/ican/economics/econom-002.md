# Demand and Supply

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your ICAN Economics paper.

**Demand** is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at various prices over a period, ceteris paribus. **Supply** is the quantity producers are willing and able to offer at various prices, ceteris paribus. The two forces meet at **market equilibrium**, fixing the equilibrium price (Pe) and equilibrium quantity (Qe).

- **Law of demand:** inverse price–quantity relationship → downward-sloping curve.
- **Law of supply:** direct price–quantity relationship → upward-sloping curve.
- **Key formulas:** Qd = a − bP ; Qs = c + dP ; Pe = (a − c) ÷ (b + d).
- **PED = |%ΔQd / %ΔP|** — elastic (>1), unit elastic (=1), inelastic (<1).
- **Trap:** a movement along the curve ≠ a shift; only non-price factors shift the curve.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students working through the ICAN Economics syllabus across weeks.

#### Demand, Supply and the Market Mechanism

Demand and supply jointly determine how resources are allocated in a market economy. The ICAN syllabus treats this as the gateway to microeconomic analysis, so mastery here unlocks elasticity, utility, cost, and market-structure questions later in the paper.

#### Equilibrium Calculation

Setting Qd = Qs gives the equilibrium price. For Qd = 80 − 4P and Qs = 10 + 2P, solving 80 − 4P = 10 + 2P yields **Pe = ₦11.67/unit** (Naira) and **Qe = 33.33 units**. Substitute back into either equation to verify. ICAN short-theory questions often supply a paired schedule and ask you to derive Pe and Qe algebraically.

#### Determinants of Shifts vs. Movement

A change in **own price** causes a movement along the curve. Any other change causes the whole curve to shift.

| Determinant | Shifts Demand | Shifts Supply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Income (normal good) | Rightward | — |
| Income (inferior good) | Leftward | — |
| Price of substitute | Same direction as good's price | — |
| Price of complement | Opposite direction | — |
| Input / production cost | — | Opposite direction |
| Technology improvement | — | Rightward |
| Excise tax / subsidy | Leftward (tax) / rightward (subsidy) | — |

#### Price Controls and Disequilibrium

A price ceiling below Pe creates a **shortage**; a price floor above Pe creates a **surplus**. Nigeria's fuel-subsidy debate and minimum-wage policy are standard ICAN contextual applications.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students with months to spare, including edge cases and Nigerian policy links.

#### Elasticity in Depth

Price elasticity of demand is read as a magnitude: |PED| > 1 elastic, = 1 unit elastic, < 1 inelastic. On a linear demand curve, elasticity falls as you move down the curve, so the top-left segment is elastic and the bottom-right segment is inelastic. Cross elasticity (XED) is **positive** for substitutes (e.g. rice and garri, Pure Water and bottled water) and **negative** for complements (e.g. cars and petrol, printers and cartridges).

#### Worked Micro-Example

A Lagos baker faces Qd = 200 − 5P and Qs = 20 + 3P. Equilibrium requires 200 − 5P = 20 + 3P ⇒ 8P = 180 ⇒ **Pe = ₦22.50**. Qe = 200 − 5(22.5) = **87.5 loaves**. Consumer surplus = ½ × (40 − 22.5) × 87.5 = **₦765.63**. Producer surplus = ½ × (22.5 − 6.67) × 87.5 = **₦692.71**. Always state units (Naira, loaves) in your answer to earn full marks.

#### Common Mistakes and Practice Prompts

> **Trap to avoid:** Ceteris paribus is non-negotiable — write it explicitly whenever you state the Law of Demand or Law of Supply on the exam script.

1. Treating "demand increased" as a movement along the curve; it must shift rightward.
2. Ignoring the effect of naira devaluation on import-dependent supply (e.g. refined petrol, rice).
3. Forgetting that elasticity is dimensionless, so units cancel in the ratio.

#### Practice prompts:

1. With Qd = 150 − 3P and Qs = 30 + 2P, calculate Pe, Qe, CS, and PS, and comment on the effect of a ₦5 excise tax on consumers.
2. Using a fully labelled diagram, show how a fuel subsidy removal shifts supply and discusses the short-run vs long-run elasticity implications for Nigerian households.
