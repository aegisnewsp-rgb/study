# Tanzanian History: Pre-Colonial Era to Independence — ACSEE (Tanzania)

This General Knowledge topic traces Tanzania from the Swahili coast trading towns of the first millennium through the German colonial period (1885–1919), the British mandate (1919–1961), and the political road to independence under Tanganyika African National Union (TANU). It also covers the 1964 union of Tanganyika with the People's Republic of Zanzibar to form the United Republic of Tanzania. Exam questions test dates, named actors (Sultan Seyyid Said, Karl Peters, Julius Nyerere, Abeid Karume), key documents (the 1961 Tanganyika Independence Act, the 1965 Interim Constitution, the 1964 Articles of Union), and the major turning points (the Maji Maji uprising 1905–1907, the Tanganyika independence of 9 December 1961, the Zanzibar Revolution of January 1964, the Union of 26 April 1964).

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)
> The minimum you need to recognise names and dates on the paper.

**Pre-colonial political units worth naming on the exam:**
- Swahili city-states along the coast: Kilwa, Zanzibar, Bagamoyo, Dar es Salaam, Mafia, Sofala (further south).
- Interior kingdoms and chiefdoms: Buganda, Bunyoro, Toro, Ankole (Great Lakes region — present political Uganda); Nyamwezi and Chagga polities on the trade route to the lakes; Hehe under Chief Mkwawa (southern highlands); the Sukuma, the Luo around Lake Victoria.
- Zanzibar: came under Omani rule from the 17th century; the Busaidi dynasty of Oman transferred its seat to Zanzibar under Sultan Seyyid Said (who moved from Muscat in 1840). Zanzibar controlled clove trade and a coastal commercial empire reaching into the interior.

**Caravan trade:** Nyamwezi porters from Tabora carried ivory, slaves and later cloth across to Bagamoyo and other coastal points. The route was central to the 19th-century ivory and slave trade that brought Swahili-Arab merchants deep into the interior.

**The German colonial period (Deutsch-Ostafrika):**
- Karl Peters and the German East Africa Company (1884) signed "treaties" with local chiefs (later widely described as fraudulent); the German state took over direct administration in 1891.
- Major rebellion: **Maji Maji** (1905–1907), a multi-ethnic uprising against forced cotton cultivation, headed by Kinjikitile Ngwale. The war ended after the German counter-insurgency under Governor Albrecht von Rechenberg; estimates of deaths from the war and subsequent famine range from 75,000 to 300,000.
- World War I: German forces under Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck fought a guerrilla campaign across the territory and into Mozambique; the armistice ended the campaign on 25 November 1918.

**The British mandate period:**
- Tanganyika became a British League of Nations mandate in 1919, administered initially through the colonial office (then as a UN Trust Territory after 1946).
- The groundnut scheme (Tanganyika Groundnut Corporation, 1946) was a British colonial project to grow peanuts at Kongwa; it failed on soils, machinery, and management and lost large sums.
- Nationalism: Tanganyika African Association (TAA, founded 1929) merged with the more radical Tanganyika African National Union (TANU) in 1954 under Julius Kambarage Nyerere. TANU won a strong position in the 1958–59 elections and negotiated independence.

**Independence and union:**
- Tanganyika became independent on **9 December 1961**, with Nyerere as Prime Minister.
- Republic declared on 9 December 1962; Nyerere became President.
- Zanzibar Revolution: **12 January 1964**, overthrew Sultan Jamshid bin Abdullah and his government; the Afro-Shirazi Party (ASP) under Abeid Karume and the Umma Party formed a revolutionary government.
- Tanganyika army mutiny of January 1964 was suppressed with British help.
- **Articles of Union** signed 22 April 1964; the United Republic of Tanzania was formed on **26 April 1964**, with Nyerere as President of the union and Karume as First Vice President.
- The 1965 Interim Constitution entrenched the union; the union Constitution was revised in 1977 and remains the basis of the present constitutional order.

⚡ **Exam tip:** For any "Who/When/What" question on this topic, write the answer as "Name (Year) — what they did". Many students drop the date or the role and lose one of the two marks.

**Causes and consequences of Maji Maji.** The causes were a mixture of grievances — German forced-labour policies (the *kiboko* or whip), heavy taxation in money or in labour, displacement for cotton plantations, and the takeover of fertile land. The war began in the southeastern highlands (Matumbi hills) and spread to Ngoni, Bena, Hehe, and other groups. The leader Kinjikitile Ngwale distributed *maji* (holy water) which he said had been blessed by the spirit Hongo and would turn German bullets to water — hence the name. The German response used scorched-earth tactics and a famine followed. The aftermath is widely credited with prompting German reforms under Governor Rechenberg and influencing later policy on indirect rule.

**Tanganyika's road to independence.** Why 1961 and not 1956 or 1964? The 1950s saw three forces converging: (1) post-WWII anti-colonial sentiment across Africa (Ghana 1957, Nigeria 1960); (2) the UN Trusteeship Council's push for self-government; (3) TANU's organising across regions and ethnic groups, with Nyerere's repeated emphasis on national unity (the *ujamaa* ideology, although fully developed later, began here). The British granted internal self-government in 1960 and full independence on 9 December 1961 with Nyerere as Prime Minister.

**Zanzibar and the union.** The Zanzibar Revolution overthrew a government dominated by the Zanzibar Nationalist Party (ZNP), which was supported by the Arab and Asian communities; the revolutionary coalition included the ASP (mainly African) and the Umma Party. After the revolution, the new government sought protection against counter-revolution and outside interference; Nyerere's Tanganyika offered a union, which Karume accepted. The Articles of Union created a single state with two governments — the union government for shared matters and the Zanzibar Revolutionary Council for Zanzibar's internal affairs. Karume was assassinated in 1972 and was succeeded by Aboud Jumbe.

**Worked example — explain why Tanganyika and Zanzibar united in 1964.** A 6-mark essay would mention: (1) shared fear of external interference, particularly from the remaining settler communities and Cold War pressures; (2) shared Swahili language and East African coastal culture; (3) the political alignment between TANU and the ASP leadership; (4) economic complementarity — mainland food production and Zanzibar's clove exports; (5) Karume's need for protection against counter-revolution after the January revolution. A strong answer links the immediate trigger (revolution aftermath) with the longer-running aspiration (East African unity that Nyerere had supported since the late 1950s).

**Practice set:**
1. Who moved his capital from Muscat to Zanzibar, and when?
2. Name two economic grievances that contributed to the Maji Maji uprising.
3. What were the Articles of Union, and when were they signed?
4. State two reasons why Tanganyika became independent in 1961 rather than earlier.
5. Briefly distinguish TANU from ASP.

Answers:
1. Sultan Seyyid Said, in 1840.
2. Forced labour on cotton plantations; heavy taxation; displacement from fertile land; the whip (*kiboko*).
3. The agreement between Tanganyika and Zanzibar forming the United Republic of Tanzania, signed 22 April 1964 (effective 26 April 1964).
4. International context: UN trusteeship push and the wave of African independence in 1957–1960; domestic organisation: TANU's pan-ethnic mobilisation under Nyerere; British post-war willingness to disengage from territories that could pay their own way.
5. TANU (Tanganyika African National Union) was the mainland independence movement founded in 1954 under Nyerere; ASP (Afro-Shirazi Party) was the Zanzibar party founded in 1957, dominant in the Zanzibar revolution. They were separate parties but shared anti-colonial politics.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (1mo+)
> Build the historiographical and analytical layer that an essay-grade question demands.

**The historiography of Maji Maji.** German colonial archives describe the war as a brutal religious uprising suppressed with overwhelming force; the post-1961 Tanzanian national narrative (especially Nyerere's *Freedom and Unity / Uhuru na Umoja*, 1966, and the scholarly literature it shaped) framed it as the first major anti-colonial war in Tanganyika and a foundation of national identity. Later revisions (e.g. John Iliffe, *A Modern History of Tanganyika*, 1979; Marcia Wright and others) emphasised multiple causes, ethnic diversity of the participants, and the role of ecological and economic factors over the religious one. The same war can be told as a German tragedy, a Tanganyikan origin story, or a regional rebellion — the question's framing matters.

**Indirect rule and the British in Tanganyika.** The British initially tried indirect rule through existing chiefs, mirroring Nigeria; this worked best where there were strong traditional authorities (Buganda, Toro, Bunyoro were, however, in Uganda not Tanganyika — Tanganyika's chiefly structures were weaker, especially after German rule had weakened some). The 1926 Native Authority Ordinance formalised indirect rule; by the 1950s, the demand for independence made the policy unworkable, and TANU absorbed or replaced the Native Authorities.

**The "race question" and Nyerere's political theory.** Nyerere's writings (*Ujamaa: Essays on Socialism*, 1968) argued for a specifically African socialism built on the extended family and on rejecting both capitalism and doctrinaire Marxism. The one-party state from 1965 (TANU) and the 1967 Arusha Declaration set out self-reliance, nationalisation of key industries, and villagisation. On the exam, "ujamaa" questions usually expect a short definition plus a description of two policies it produced — villagisation (*vijiji vya ujamaa*) and nationalisation of banks and industry. Avoid over-claiming: ujamaa was not Marxist-Leninist, but it was also not free-market.

**Zanzibar in the long view.** Zanzibar's identity is a layered product — Swahili, Omani, African mainland, British colonial, and post-revolutionary. The revolution is sometimes treated as a single ethnic event; in fact the ASP was internally diverse and the revolutionary coalition split within a year, leading to the one-party state under Karume. The clove-based economy collapsed after the revolution when many Arab landowners left; the implications for union are still debated — was the union a takeover of Zanzibar by the mainland, or a protectorate from outside powers? Stronger answers acknowledge both readings and note that the structure has shifted over time (1992 multi-party, 2010 Government of National Unity arrangements).

**The Union Constitution's working.** The 1977 Union Constitution divides power between union matters (foreign affairs, defence, police, currency, immigration, macroeconomic policy) and Zanzibar's exclusive matters (most domestic policy, including education below degree level, internal security, the Zanzibar President). The union President is also President of Zanzibar but the union Vice President is the President of Zanzibar — these arrangements have been contested. The 1992 amendments legalised multi-party politics (subject to Zanzibar's consent). Subsequent reforms (2000, 2005, 2010, 2012) modified representation and government formation in Zanzibar.

**Long-running themes an essay could pick up:**
1. **Contested sovereignty:** Was Tanganyika independent from the British in 1961, or only when the Union was formed in 1964? The constitution treats the former as the foundational date; political historians continue to debate.
2. **Unity vs plurality:** Nyerere's national project required a single language (Kiswahili) and a single party; the effect was to suppress regional and ethnic identity.
3. **Revolutionary and constitutional state-building:** The Zanzibar Revolution was violent; the Articles of Union were negotiated; the 1965 Interim Constitution and the 1977 Union Constitution were written under TANU one-party rule. Each step shifted the basis of authority.

### Common traps
1. **Misattributing the date of independence.** Tanganyika became independent on 9 December 1961, and a republic on 9 December 1962. Zanzibar became independent from Britain on 10 December 1963 and had its revolution on 12 January 1964. The union came on 26 April 1964. Confusing these dates loses marks on a 4-mark "timeline" question.
2. **Calling the union a "merger" of equals.** It was a union between a much larger Tanganyika and a small Zanzibar, with the mainland dominating the union government; describing it as a merger of two equals misreads the constitutional structure.
3. **Treating TANU and ASP as the same party.** They were separate parties on either side of the union. ASP ruled Zanzibar alone; it later merged with TANU to form CCM (Chama Cha Mapinduzi, 1977) after the union.
4. **Saying "Nyerere became President at independence".** He was Prime Minister at independence (9 December 1961); he became President when Tanganyika became a republic on 9 December 1962. Examiners reward the distinction.
5. **Calling the Maji Maji war a religious war in isolation.** The religious element (maji, Hongo) is real but the war had material causes (forced labour, taxation, cotton policy). An answer that only says "maji turned bullets to water" loses the analytical marks.
6. **Forgetting the East African context.** Tanganyika's independence was negotiated alongside Kenya and Uganda; the East African common services arrangement shaped Tanganyika's post-independence economy. Mentioning only Tanganyika in isolation makes an essay answer read narrow.
