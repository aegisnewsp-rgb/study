# Sociology and Social Institutions

A social institution is a stable pattern of social roles, rules and practices that meets a social need. Family, education, religion, economy and government are the main institutions examined in this topic. Each connects individual behaviour to structures that existed before the individual arrived and will continue after the individual leaves.

The key is to resist two simplifications. One treats institutions as neutral containers. The other treats every outcome as the free choice of individuals. Sociology asks how norms are formed, enforced and challenged; how institutions allocate resources and power; and how people reproduce or change them.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

**What makes an institution an institution.**

- A **norm** is a shared expectation about conduct.
- A **sanction** is a reaction to conformity or deviance. Positive sanctions reward conformity; negative sanctions punish deviance.
- Formal sanctions come from an organisation with authority, such as a court, school or workplace. Informal sanctions come from social approval, disapproval or exclusion.
- Socialisation is the process through which people learn the values and behaviours expected of them.
- Legitimacy is the accepted right to exercise authority.
- Power is the ability to influence the behaviour of others.

**The major institutions and their core functions.**

| Institution | Primary functions | Sociological issues |
|---|---|---|
| Family | Socialisation of children, care, emotional support, economic cooperation | Household forms, gender roles, kinship, care work |
| Education | Transmission of knowledge and skills, socialisation, sorting and selection | Inequality, credentialism, hidden curriculum, tracking |
| Religion | Shared meaning, ritual, group identity, moral orientation | Sacred and profane, legitimacy, conflict, secularisation |
| Economy | Production, distribution and exchange of goods and services | Property, labour, markets, class, consumer behaviour |
| Government | Authoritative allocation, law, coordination, protection | Legitimacy, power, bureaucracy, representation, rights |

**The family as an institution.** The family transmits culture, provides emotional and economic support, socialises children and places limits on sexual and reproductive conduct. Its forms vary: nuclear, extended, single-parent, blended, adoptive and same-sex households all perform these functions in different arrangements. A functional account asks what the family contributes to society. A conflict account asks who controls its resources, labour and decision-making. Feminist analysis focuses on the unequal distribution of unpaid care and domestic labour.

**Education as an institution.** Functions include transmission of knowledge, preparation for work, socialisation into citizenship, social integration and sorting individuals by role. The **hidden curriculum** consists of the unofficial norms, expectations and hierarchies learned in school. **Credentialism** occurs when qualifications carry more value than the work they certify. Conflict theory treats education as a means of reproducing class inequality; functionalism treats it as a fair selection mechanism if the procedure is open and the criteria relate to performance.

**Religion as an institution.** Durkheim described religion through the division of the **sacred** and the **profane**, with ritual gathering people into a moral community. Marx treated religion as part of the superstructure that soothed the effects of material hardship. Weber studied the influence of religious ethics on economic action. Functionalism stresses social cohesion and shared meaning; conflict theory stresses the power to define legitimate belief.

**Economy as an institution.** The economy coordinates production, distribution and consumption. Property rights determine who owns productive assets. Markets allocate through prices, while firms organise labour and production. Institutional economics adds that markets work inside rules: contract enforcement, property titles, currency and competition policy. Weber's **rationalisation** describes the shift toward calculation, efficiency, predictable rules and bureaucracy. Marx centres ownership of the means of production. Bourdieu adds that economic position interacts with cultural and social capital.

**Government as an institution.** Government claims legitimate authority to make and enforce binding rules. Weber's ideal types of authority are traditional, charismatic and legal-rational. Modern states draw chiefly on legal-rational authority: legitimacy rests on enacted rules and offices rather than on a particular person. Government allocates resources through taxation, spending and regulation. Democratic government combines representation, accountability and participation; authoritarian government concentrates power and limits competition.

**Three classical theoretical lenses.**

| Lens | Main question | Family | Education | Religion | Economy | Government |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Functionalism | What work does the institution do for social stability? | Socialisation and support | Skill transmission and role allocation | Shared meaning and cohesion | Efficient allocation and adaptation | Coordination and order |
| Conflict theory | Who benefits, and who controls scarce resources? | Unequal care work and property | Reproduction of inequality | Legitimation of power | Ownership and class conflict | Power and domination |
| Symbolic interactionism | How do meanings arise in interaction? | Roles and identities | Labels, streaming and classroom interaction | Symbols and ritual | Consumption and status meanings | Definitions and negotiated legitimacy |

**Norms, sanctions and deviance.** Social control ranges from informal disapproval to formal punishment. **Deviance** is behaviour that violates social norms, not an inherent property of an act. A behaviour can be deviant in one setting and ordinary in another. Functionalists view deviance as serving boundary and cohesion functions; conflict theorists focus on the unequal power to define deviance; interactionists examine how labels produce deviant careers.

**Social movements.** A movement emerges from shared grievances, resources, organisation and a collective identity. Its outcomes range from a changed norm to institutional reform. **Institutionalisation** means a movement's practices become routine. **Social change** can proceed through reform, revolution or the gradual diffusion of new expectations.

**Memorise for the Lite tier.**

- Institution: stable roles, rules and practices.
- Norm: shared expectation. Sanction: reaction to conformity or deviance.
- Family socialises and supports; education transmits and sorts; religion creates shared meaning; economy coordinates exchange; government exercises legitimate authority.
- Functionalism asks for social function; conflict theory asks who benefits; symbolic interactionism asks how meaning is made.
- Deviance is defined by a social response, not by the act alone.
- Legal-rational legitimacy rests on rules and offices.

### 🟡 Standard — Full Coverage (1–2d)

#### Worked example: why one school has a wider achievement gap

**The observation.** Students at a well-resourced school achieve more examination results than students at a school with fewer experienced teachers and fewer books. The school's guide says that students choose whether to study.

**The conflict account.** The guide converts an institutional pattern into an individual choice. A conflict account asks how the school sorts students, who receives experienced teachers, and how prior advantage helps families navigate admissions, tutoring and application. The wider gap appears when students do not enter on equal terms and when schools reinforce rather than reduce those differences.

**The functional account.** Functionalism asks whether the school is performing its stated job efficiently. If selection is based on attainment, the gap between schools after selection is not evidence that selection failed. The account predicts that differences should narrow once prior attainment and relevant preparation are held constant, and that unequal outcomes remain if the procedure creates new group differences.

**The interactionist account.** Classroom interaction, teacher expectations and labels can affect how students see themselves. A school-wide description does not identify that mechanism, but a study of classroom practice could.

**The data problem.** The observation does not show that the better-resourced school caused the gap. Students select schools, and families with more resources have more school choices. The school gap and prior family resources are associated. A longitudinal study, a matched comparison or a natural experiment would carry more causal weight.

**What the item rewards.** State which level of explanation the claim uses. If the claim explains a group pattern through individual choices, the strongest challenge is to ask whether choices were constrained by institutional arrangements.

#### Worked example: competing interpretations of a strike

Workers stop work over pay and conditions. One explanation says that shared injustice produced a collective identity and organisation. Another says that a small group of leaders manipulated workers who lacked accurate information. Another says that the action occurred because a new agreement gave strikers a way to organise.

The first account is a **resource mobilisation** explanation: grievances become effective through organisation and collective identity. The second is a **bias** explanation that treats the participants as victims of misleading information. The third is an **opportunity** explanation focused on a political opening. A good answer says which evidence would distinguish them: membership records and organisational history for the first, communication and information flow for the second, and changes in the political environment for the third.

This example is a useful pattern for the whole topic. A single event has several explanations, and the item asks which mechanism the evidence fits rather than which account sounds deepest.

#### Worked example: interpreting a case study without generalising

A researcher studies one rural community where community councils allocate a shared water reserve. Interviews show that council members rotate positions of responsibility, decisions are minuted publicly, and disputes are heard before allocation. The researcher concludes that democratic institutions always produce fair resource distribution.

The evidence supports a detailed account of this community. It does not support "always". The case lacks comparison, and the sample was selected for interesting governance. The **ecological fallacy** also lurks: individual council members support collective decisions, yet that tells us little about the distribution of power across the whole society. The safe conclusion is that this community's practices combine participation, accountability and conflict resolution. A survey across several communities would be needed for a population claim.

#### Norms in everyday institutions

Norms differ in strength. Laws are formal rules backed by state sanction. Professional codes are formal but enforced by an occupational body. Conventions, such as queueing, are usually informal yet strongly expected. Fashions and preferences carry little social pressure. An item may ask whether a rule is a norm at all, or which institution has the authority to enforce it.

Norms become institutions through repetition, shared understanding and linked roles. Enforcement need not be harsh. Everyday institutions rely on approval, shame, exclusion and the expectation that others will cooperate. This helps explain why a technically illegal practice can persist inside a group: the group can control information, offer rewards and make reporting costly.

**Socialisation continues throughout life.** Family begins it, school extends it, peers reshape it, and workplace and media institutions add new roles. **Agents of socialisation** include parents, teachers, peers, workplaces, mass media and religious organisations. **Resocialisation** occurs when an institution replaces earlier patterns, as in military training, monastic life or some rehabilitation programmes.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (3–5d)

#### Reading an institution through its comparisons

For each institution, ask four questions:

1. **What need does it address?** The answer reveals the functions.
2. **Who occupies its key roles?** This reveals status, authority and gatekeeping.
3. **What resources does it control?** Money, knowledge, legitimacy, coercion and information have different power effects.
4. **What rules define legitimate conduct?** The rules distribute opportunities and sanctions.

Compare two answers rather than accepting a single label. If a policy expands school access, functionalism predicts greater skill development and mobility; conflict theory asks whether the expansion weakened a gatekeeper's control or changed credential supply; interactionism asks how students interpret the change and what new identities it makes available.

#### Institutions and power

Weber's definition of state power rests on legitimate violence, but social institutions rarely need physical force. The person who controls a credential, decides admission or shapes a workplace rule can influence conduct without force. This is why Bourdieu's **symbolic power** matters: a group is successful when its categories of value are treated as natural and its leaders define legitimate taste. Feminist analysis shows how apparently personal decisions about work and care are organised by institutions. Foucault analyses how institutions create categories of the normal and abnormal through routines of examination and correction.

**Recognition versus redistribution.** Public debate can focus on equal recognition while leaving unequal resources untouched. Other disputes focus on redistribution of resources while treating prevailing status hierarchies as settled. An item that contrasts these positions is testing which mechanism the author treats as primary.

#### Common mistakes

**Treating family as one fixed form.** The functions are more durable than the household arrangement, and the family form changes across societies and history.

**Confusing socialisation with indoctrination.** Socialisation transmits more than one value system, and people interpret and resist what institutions teach.

**Calling any unequal outcome evidence of discrimination.** A group difference needs comparison with relevant alternatives before the cause can be assigned.

**Assuming cohesion proves legitimacy.** Consensus can reflect shared belief, power, dependence or a lack of organised opposition.

**Treating law and government as identical.** Law is a set of rules; government makes, administers and enforces public decisions through institutions.

**Using "society" as a unified actor.** Groups within a society have unequal resources and conflicting interests.

#### Exam traps

A passage says that religious attendance supports social cohesion but also that religious institutions have opposed social change. The best answer names different functions and consequences rather than forcing a single verdict.

A school introduces mixed-ability classes and achievement gaps continue. The functional account may say selection and hidden curriculum persist; the conflict account may say the school's stated aim has not changed who controls knowledge. The item asks for the best-supported interpretation, so name the mechanism the described evidence supports.

A survey finds that people who attend religious services report stronger community ties. The study establishes association. Alternative explanations include age, family background and pre-existing community membership.

A case of a person refusing an official rule because a peer group rejects it shows conflict between institutions. The peer group is acting as a norm-enforcing body, and the choice is a site of institutional tension rather than a purely personal decision.
