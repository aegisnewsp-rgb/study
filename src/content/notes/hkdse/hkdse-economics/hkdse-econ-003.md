# C. Market and Price — HKDSE Economics Notes

This topic covers Topic C of the Compulsory Part of HKDSE Economics. It addresses perfect competition (short-run and long-run equilibrium, shut-down condition); the firm as a price taker; consumer surplus and producer surplus; market efficiency; the effects of taxation and subsidies on equilibrium output and welfare; and price controls (price ceilings and price floors).

> Verify the live HKDSE Economics Assessment Framework on https://www.hkeaa.edu.hk/ before planning revision.

---

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Perfect competition**: many buyers and sellers, homogeneous product, perfect information, free entry and exit. Firms are price takers.
- **Short-run equilibrium**: P = MC (where MC is rising).
- **Long-run equilibrium**: P = MC = min ATC (firms earn zero economic profit; normal profit included).
- **Shut-down condition**: P < AVC in the short run; firm shuts down.
- **Exit condition**: P < ATC in the long run; firm exits.
- **Consumer surplus (CS)**: the difference between what consumers are willing to pay and what they actually pay. Area under the demand curve and above the equilibrium price.
- **Producer surplus (PS)**: the difference between what producers receive and the minimum they would accept. Area above the supply curve and below the equilibrium price.
- **Total surplus**: CS + PS. Maximised at competitive equilibrium.
- **Deadweight loss**: the loss of total surplus due to a market distortion (tax, quota, monopoly).
- **Taxes**:
  - **Specific tax**: a fixed amount per unit. Shifts supply curve up by the tax amount.
  - **Ad valorem tax**: a percentage of price. Rotates the supply curve up by the tax percentage.
- **Tax incidence**: how the tax burden is shared between buyers and sellers. Depends on the relative elasticities of demand and supply.
- **Subsidy**: a payment from the government to producers (or consumers). Shifts the supply curve down (or demand curve up). Increases total surplus but at a cost to the government.
- **Price ceiling (maximum price)**: a legal maximum price. Effective if below equilibrium. Causes shortages, black markets, and quality deterioration.
- **Price floor (minimum price)**: a legal minimum price. Effective if above equilibrium. Causes surpluses and deadweight loss.

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing CS with "consumer welfare" — consumer welfare is broader (includes PS, government revenue, etc.).
- Forgetting that a price ceiling above the equilibrium has no effect.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Perfect competition

Assumptions: many buyers and sellers; homogeneous product; perfect information; free entry and exit; perfect factor mobility.

**Short-run equilibrium**:

- P > ATC: positive economic profit (supernormal profit). Attracts new firms in the long run.
- P = ATC: normal profit (zero economic profit). Long-run equilibrium.
- P < ATC but P > AVC: loss, but continue operating in the short run.
- P < AVC: shut down.

**Long-run equilibrium**:

- P = MC = min ATC. Economic profit = 0.
- All firms earn normal profit. No incentive for entry or exit.

#### Welfare analysis

- **Consumer surplus (CS)**: ∫(D - P*) dQ from 0 to Q* (area under demand curve, above P*).
- **Producer surplus (PS)**: ∫(P* - S) dQ from 0 to Q* (area above supply curve, below P*).
- **Total surplus (TS)**: CS + PS. Maximised at competitive equilibrium.

#### Taxes and subsidies

**Specific tax (per-unit) on producers**:

- Supply curve shifts up by the tax amount.
- Equilibrium price rises (less than the tax); equilibrium quantity falls.
- Tax revenue = tax × new quantity.
- CS falls; PS falls; government revenue rises; deadweight loss = triangle between old and new equilibria.

**Tax incidence**:

- If demand is more inelastic than supply, consumers bear more of the tax.
- If supply is more inelastic than demand, producers bear more of the tax.

**Subsidy**:

- Reduces the effective cost of production.
- Supply curve shifts down (or demand curve shifts up if to consumers).
- Equilibrium price falls (if subsidy to producers); equilibrium quantity rises.
- Government cost = subsidy × new quantity.
- CS rises; PS rises; government cost is the deadweight loss to society (resource cost).

#### Price controls

**Price ceiling**:

- Maximum price set by the government.
- If below equilibrium: shortage = Qd - Qs at the ceiling price.
- Effects: queues, black markets, quality deterioration, reduced supply in the long run.
- Examples: rent control in some cities; fuel price control in some countries.

**Price floor**:

- Minimum price set by the government.
- If above equilibrium: surplus = Qs - Qd at the floor price.
- Effects: excess supply, government purchases or storage, deadweight loss.
- Examples: minimum wage (a price floor for labour); agricultural price supports.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Cross-topic links

- **D. Competition and Market Structure**: welfare losses from monopoly.
- **E. Efficiency, Equity and the Role of Government**: market failure.
- **J. International Trade and Finance**: tariffs and quotas as international price controls.

#### Exam technique

- **For equilibrium questions**: set Qd = Qs and solve for P and Q.
- **For welfare questions**: compute CS, PS and total surplus before and after the distortion.
- **For tax/subsidy questions**: identify who bears the burden; compute the deadweight loss.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Taxes always raise revenue without other effects." Taxes create deadweight losses and affect behaviour.
- "Price ceilings help consumers." Price ceilings help consumers who can buy at the lower price but harm those who cannot find the good at all.
- "Subsidies are free." Subsidies cost the government money and may distort markets.
