# Overview of the MUET Writing Paper

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review

The MUET Writing paper is a **90-minute academic writing paper** divided into two tasks.

| Feature | Task 1 | Task 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Information transfer | Argumentative or discursive essay |
| Input and format | Graph, table, chart or diagram → formal letter | Topic or statement → formal essay |
| Word count | **150–200 words** | **350–400 words** |
| Recommended time | About **30 minutes** | About **60 minutes** |
| Approximate share | **30%** of Writing marks | **70%** of Writing marks |

**Task 1** requires accurate interpretation and description of trends, figures and visual information. Organise the material logically and adapt the letter to its purpose, such as a complaint, application, request for information or letter to the editor. Describe the information rather than arguing a position.

**Task 2** requires a clear stance or balanced argument supported by relevant reasons and examples. Develop the response in organised paragraphs and use academic vocabulary and tone.

Use the time deliberately. Read both questions carefully, prepare a brief outline for each task, remain within both word limits and reserve time to check spelling, grammar and punctuation. Remember that the recommended writing times already consume the full paper duration, so planning and checking must be built into your personal schedule.

Common mistakes include misreading the prompt, giving opinions in Task 1, writing Task 1 informally, ignoring word limits, going off-topic in Task 2 and neglecting Task 1 because Task 2 carries more marks.

Both tasks are assessed through four criteria:
1. **Task fulfilment**
2. **Language use**
3. **Text organisation**
4. **Mechanics**

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study


The Malaysian University English Test (MUET) Writing paper is designed to assess your ability to write in English for academic purposes. It is a 90-minute paper divided into two distinct tasks, and it carries significant weight in your overall MUET band score. Understanding the structure, requirements, and expectations of this paper is the essential first step to performing well.

#### The Two Tasks at a Glance

#### Task 1: Information Transfer (Recommended: 30 minutes)

Task 1 requires you to write a formal letter based on input provided in the form of a graph, table, chart, or diagram. You must transfer information from the visual stimulus into a written response of **150–200 words**. This task assesses your ability to:

- Interpret and describe trends, data, and visual information accurately
- Write in an appropriate formal letter format
- Organise information logically (e.g., from general to specific, or chronologically)

The letter type varies — it can be a complaint letter, an application letter, a request for information, or a letter to the editor. The key is that you are *describing* information, not arguing a position.

**Typical prompt example:**
> The graph below shows the percentage of Malaysian students pursuing higher education abroad from 2015 to 2020. Write a formal letter to the Director of Education requesting more details about government scholarship programmes.

#### Task 2: Argumentative Essay (Recommended: 60 minutes)

Task 2 is a full argumentative or discursive essay based on a given topic. You must write **350–400 words** in response to a statement or question that invites debate. This task assesses your ability to:

- Present a clear stance or balanced argument
- Support your views with relevant reasons and examples
- Organise ideas into well-developed paragraphs
- Use appropriate academic vocabulary and tone

**Typical prompt example:**
> "Social media does more harm than good to young people." Do you agree? Discuss.

#### Paper Structure Summary

| Feature | Task 1 | Task 2 |
|---|---|---|
| **Question Type** | Information transfer (graph/table → letter) | Argumentative/discursive essay |
| **Format** | Formal letter | Formal essay |
| **Word Count** | 150–200 words | 350–400 words |
| **Time Allocation** | ~30 minutes | ~60 minutes |
| **Marks Weight** | ~30% of Writing paper | ~70% of Writing paper |

#### Timing Strategy

With 90 minutes total, time management is critical:

1. **Read both questions carefully** — spend 3–5 minutes understanding exactly what each task asks. Many candidates lose marks simply because they misread the question.
2. **Plan before writing** — spend 2–3 minutes on a brief outline for each task. This investment pays off in clearer, more coherent writing.
3. **Stick to word counts** — writing too little (under 150 for Task 1 or under 350 for Task 2) limits your score potential. Writing too much wastes time and increases the chance of errors.
4. **Leave 5 minutes for checking** — always reserve time to review your work for spelling, grammar, and punctuation errors.

#### Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Answering Task 1 with opinions** — Task 1 is purely informational. You describe the data, not argue about it.
- **Ignoring the word count** — falling significantly short or exceeding the range signals poor task adherence to examiners.
- **Writing an informal letter for Task 1** — the register must be formal, regardless of the letter's purpose.
- **Writing off-topic for Task 2** — every sentence should relate to the question. Going off-topic immediately limits your score.
- **Neglecting Task 1 because Task 2 carries more marks** — Task 1 is easier to score well in and should not be rushed or treated as an afterthought.

#### What Examiners Look For

Both tasks are marked on four criteria:

1. **Task fulfilment** — Have you answered the question as asked?
2. **Language use** — Is your vocabulary appropriate, accurate, and varied?
3. **Text organisation** — Are your ideas logically structured with good paragraphing?
4. **Mechanics** — Is your spelling, punctuation, and grammar correct?

Understanding these criteria is the foundation for everything else. Each subsequent topic in this guide breaks down these skills in detail.

### 🔴 Extended — Mastery

#### Exam traps across the two tasks

- **Treating Task 1 as an essay:** A request for scholarship details does not permit a long argument about government policy. Describe the relevant visual information and make the requested request.
- **Abandoning Task 1 because Task 2 has the larger share:** The approximate 70% share does not cancel task fulfilment in the 150–200-word letter. An unfinished or inaccurate Task 1 cannot be repaired by extra Task 2 prose.
- **Assuming that “balanced” means no position:** A discursive response can acknowledge different views while still maintaining a clear, reasoned stance.
- **Using difficult language for its own sake:** Formal writing requires control and precision, not inflated sentences or unnecessary jargon.
- **Exceeding a word range:** An answer of 149 or 201 words falls outside the stated range for that task. Cutting repeated material is normally more useful than adding a decorative final sentence.
- **Writing both tasks to a word limit rather than to the prompt:** Correct length does not repair an irrelevant letter or an essay that answers a different question.

#### Worked mini-example: planning Task 1

A practice graph shows the percentage of Malaysian students pursuing higher education abroad rising from 18% in 2015 to 30% in 2020. The prompt asks you to request details about scholarship programmes from the Director of Education.

A workable content plan is:

1. **Purpose:** State that you are requesting information about scholarship programmes.
2. **Overall pattern:** Identify the rise in overseas study across the period.
3. **Key figures:** Select the starting figure, an intermediate figure and the final figure.
4. **Trend:** Note where growth became sharper.
5. **Request:** Ask for eligibility, application procedures and funding information.

A concise descriptive section can read:

> The graph shows that the percentage of Malaysian students pursuing higher education abroad rose from 18% in 2015 to 30% in 2020. Growth was gradual at first, reaching 23% in 2018, before becoming faster during the final two years. The sharpest rise occurred between 2019 and 2020. Overall, the percentage increased throughout the period shown.

The request for scholarship information belongs in the letter because it matches the prompt. A speculative explanation of why students left Malaysia does not.

#### Worked mini-example: planning Task 2

For the prompt, “Social media does more harm than good to young people. Do you agree?”, avoid listing unconnected advantages and disadvantages. A workable plan is:

- **Thesis:** Social media expands communication and access to information, but its overall effect depends on patterns of use and safeguards.
- **First main idea:** Constant comparison and attention-seeking can affect well-being.
- **Second main idea:** Online networks can support learning, friendship and access to information.
- **Counter-consideration:** The same platform can be beneficial for one purpose and harmful in another.
- **Conclusion:** Evaluate the conditions of use rather than treating every form of social media as identical.

This structure gives you reasons to develop rather than a list with equal word counts. Each paragraph should perform a distinct job.

#### Timing trap: build the schedule backwards

The recommended allocations of approximately 30 and 60 minutes already total 90 minutes. If you add three to five minutes for reading, several minutes for outlining and five minutes for checking, you must reduce the actual drafting time. Treat these figures as planning recommendations, not extra time to place on top.

In personal practice:

- Build a brief outline into each task block.
- Draft only after identifying what each answer must achieve.
- Reserve the final checking period rather than relying on spontaneous corrections.
- Rehearse cutting a weak final paragraph before adding a new one.
- Check whether Task 1 still answers the request and Task 2 still answers the debate when time pressure increases.

This is a practice method, not an official timetable.

#### Common wrong reasoning

- **“More advanced vocabulary compensates for an incorrect visual description.”** It cannot. The first claim must match the graph and prompt.
- **“A formal letter should sound impersonal.”** It should be formal, but it can still make the stated request clearly.
- **“A full essay means several long paragraphs.”** Development matters more than paragraph length.
- **“Task 2 allows free choice because it is argumentative.”** You must respond to the exact statement or question.
- **“Checking is optional if the essay feels finished.”** Errors in spelling, punctuation and grammar remain errors even in a well-organised response.

#### Safe draft versus strong draft

A safe draft can contain roughly 180 words for Task 1 and 380 for Task 2 while still misreading one task or presenting only a list of ideas. A strong draft answers each prompt directly, selects meaningful information, maintains a clear line of reasoning and leaves enough time for a final accuracy check. Length alone does not distinguish them.
