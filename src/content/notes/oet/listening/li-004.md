# Listening for Attitude, Modality and Register


### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review

The OET Test Handbook 2026 puts three parts and 42 questions inside roughly 40 minutes of audio, with each item carrying one mark before the raw total is converted onto the 0 to 500 scale. The same handbook is explicit that the paper is identical for every candidate whatever their profession — so nothing here depends on knowing clinical vocabulary.

**Modality is the item.**

- A softened request form asks the listener to act
- The same form read as a question about capability asks whether the listener is able to
- A politeness frame wrapped around that request is still a request, not a suggestion
- *Must*, *should* and *may* issue three separate instructions rather than three strengths of one

**Stress carries the difference.**

- Unstressed *should* and *must* can sound identical, because speakers reduce them
- When one of the two carries emphasis it is marking an exception — overriding a default rather than restating it
- That is why this sub-test is audio

**Register names the role before you process the content.**

- A request relayed down a ward, an announcement of what a doctor wants reviewed before a round, and a patient struggling to describe something they cannot name are three different speech acts
- The register identifies which one is happening before any content arrives

**Advice and instruction are different speech acts.**

- An opening that leaves the decision with the listener may be declined
- A bare instruction naming the preparation and the starting point may not

**Reported speech reports.**

- A recommendation relayed in the third person establishes that a recommendation exists
- It does not establish that anyone has decided anything

**Two habits.**

- Predict the slot the question needs before the answer arrives
- Answer on the words you heard rather than the conclusion you drew from them — inference is Reading's job, not this part's

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study

The official OET Test Handbook 2026 sets out a Listening sub-test of approximately 40 minutes, three parts and 42 questions, with one mark per correct answer and the score out of 42 converted to the 0 to 500 scale. It also states something that should shape how you prepare: "Listening and Reading are the same for all candidates, regardless of profession."

That means the Listening sub-test is not a reading comprehension exercise with medical nouns in it. It is an exercise in tracking what a speaker is doing, and the distinctions below are where the marks are lost.

#### Modality is the single most-missed feature
English speakers signal certainty and obligation with small grammatical words, and they mark them with stress when they matter. Strip those out and the meaning changes.

"Could you increase the dose?" is a request. "Can you increase the dose?" is a question about capability. "Would you mind increasing the dose?" is a more polite request that is still a request. In a healthcare setting these three carry different amounts of instruction, and an item that tests a change in the plan turns on which one you heard.

The same applies to other modals. "The patient must stop the medication" and "the patient should stop the medication" and "the patient may stop the medication" are three different clinical instructions.

Stress changes it further. In ordinary conversation, *should* and *must* can sound identical when unstressed, because English speakers reduce them. A speaker who stresses *must* is overriding something; a speaker who reduces it is offering a default. This is why audio, not text, is the medium — the distinction does not survive being read.


#### Register tells you who is speaking and what they want
A consultant, a junior doctor, a pharmacist, a patient and a telephone operator use different vocabulary and different sentence structures for the same event, and the register tells you which is which before you process the content.

"Could you bring Ms Adeyemi's notes forward, please?" — administrative, deferential, a request passed along.

"I'd like to review Ms Adeyemi before the ward round." — a doctor issuing an instruction.

"I went in the night and she was just... I don't know, she wasn't right." — a patient reporting, with vagueness, hedging and an unfinished sentence that carries the emotional weight.

Candidates who try to process every word miss the register cue and then lose the content. Use register to predict what comes next, then listen for the content.


#### Advice versus instruction
"Something you might consider is a lower-dose preparation" is advice the listener may decline. "Start on the lower-dose preparation" is an instruction.

A frequent OET item gives you a plan change and asks which speaker expressed it. The distractor is a phrase that sounds informative but carries no instruction, or an instruction the speaker attributed to someone else — "the pharmacist said I should…" is a report of advice, not advice itself.

Watch for reported speech too. "Dr Ahmed recommends a repeat scan" tells you a recommendation exists, not that a decision has been made. The distinction matters clinically and it is exactly what these items test.


#### Two habits that fix most of it
Predict before the answer arrives. Having read the question stem, you know what kind of information is coming, so you are listening for that slot rather than for everything at once. The audio is dense and the questions are not; the questions are your filter.

Answer on the words you heard, not the meaning you inferred. If the item asks what the speaker advised and you concluded something different from what was said, choose the one that was said. Inference belongs to the Reading sub-test; here the transcript is the authority.


#### Common mistakes
Reading the options while the speaker is still talking. You miss the modality word that changes the answer.

Treating reported speech as instruction. "The registrar said to increase the dose" is a report; the decision belongs to whoever says it.

Confusing a nurse\'s polite request with a doctor\'s instruction. Register signals the role, and the role decides how binding the words are.

Letting the emotional content of a patient\'s speech disrupt your listening. Panic in a speaker\'s voice does not change the information being conveyed.

Assuming hedges are filler. "It might possibly be worth reviewing" is doing real work.

Answering from clinical plausibility rather than from the audio. Plausible alternatives are deliberately included, and they are frequently more clinically obvious than the correct one.

### 🔴 Extended — Mastery

#### Why the stress cue has to be contrastive

Unstressed *should* and *must* can sound identical, because speakers reduce them, and the reduction is normal speech rather than carelessness. That makes stress a lexical event rather than an intonational one: a speaker drawing the contrast is overriding a competing default, and a speaker letting the word pass unstressed is accepting the default. An item built on that distinction was produced with the contrast audible on purpose, so treat the stress as item data rather than delivery noise. It also explains why transcription practice does not prepare you for this part — a transcript keeps the words and drops the cue.

#### Attribution depth, and who actually holds the decision

Reported speech comes in depths, and the depth tells you where the decision sits. An instruction that arrives third-hand, attributed to a pharmacist you never hear, is advice reported by the person who received it. A third-hand recommendation from a named consultant is still only a recommendation. A first-person statement of what will happen is the decision itself. An item asking which speaker expressed a plan change is testing exactly this chain: the more attributions deep, the further the decision sits from the voice you are hearing. Hedge chains — a course of action whose origin nobody can name — compound the same uncertainty.

#### Register as a predictive model

Five roles in a hospital talk about the same event with different vocabulary and different sentence structures: the senior clinician who decides, the junior clinician who carries out, the allied-health professional who dispenses, the patient who reports, and the telephone operator who routes the call. Once you have named the role you can predict the content slot the speaker is about to fill, and you are no longer trying to catch every word. The patient turn is the clearest test: vague vocabulary, hedging, false starts and an unfinished sentence, with the emotional weight carried by the interruption rather than by volume.

#### Hedges are load-bearing, not padding

Hedges carry load rather than padding. A three-word stack of softening adverbs around a proposal does two jobs at once: it softens the face threat of giving an instruction, and it signals that the speaker lacks the standing to give one. Where a patient is speaking, the same hedging usually signals fear as well. Candidates who discard those phrases as filler lose the register information sitting inside them.

#### Spending 42 one-mark items

One mark per correct answer, no partial credit, so there is nothing to gain from an item you cannot place. Predicting the slot before the answer arrives is also what keeps the item budget intact: the dense audio is filtered by a short question stem, and a candidate listening to everything hears nothing. The expensive error is reaching for the option that fits the clinical picture, because the distractors here are built to be more medically obvious than the recording itself.

#### What this part is not

Part C of Reading asks you to license an answer with a sentence in the text. Here there is no text to license from and the transcript is the authority: if the audio did not say it, a correct-sounding conclusion still scores zero.
