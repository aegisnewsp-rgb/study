# Fundamental Rights (Articles 12-35)

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Fundamental Rights form the bedrock of constitutional democracy enshrined in Part III (Articles 12–35) of the Constitution of India, described as the Magna Carta of India. These rights guarantee civil liberties against arbitrary state encroachment and are directly enforceable under judicial review. Originally, the Constitution provided seven fundamental rights, but the 44th Constitutional Amendment Act (1978) removed the Right to Property from Article 19(1)(f) and Article 31, reclassifying it as a statutory constitutional right under Article 300A.

| Fundamental Right Cluster | Constitutional Articles | Fundamental Core Protection | Primary Constitutional Restriction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Right to Equality** | Articles 14–18 | Equality before law; non-discrimination; equal opportunity in public posts | Special provisions for SC/ST/OBC/EWS; military/academic title exceptions |
| **Right to Freedom** | Articles 19–22 | Six democratic freedoms; protection against retrospective criminal laws; life and personal liberty | Reasonable restrictions under 19(2)–(6); sovereignty, public order, decency |
| **Right against Exploitation** | Articles 23–24 | Absolute prohibition of human trafficking, *begar*, and child labour in hazardous industries | State compulsory public service without religious or caste discrimination |
| **Right to Freedom of Religion** | Articles 25–28 | Freedom of conscience, profession, practice, propagation, and religious institution management | Subject to public order, morality, health, and social welfare reform laws |
| **Cultural & Educational Rights** | Articles 29–30 | Preservation of distinct language, script, culture; minority right to establish educational institutions | Non-discrimination in state aid; regulatory academic standards |
| **Constitutional Remedies** | Articles 32 | Guaranteed direct access to Supreme Court for enforcement via prerogative writs | May be suspended during National Emergency under Article 359 (except 20 & 21) |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### 1. Scope of "State" (Article 12) and Judicial Review (Article 13)

Article 12 defines the "State" against whom Fundamental Rights are primarily enforceable:
1. The Government and Parliament of India (Union executive and legislative organs).
2. The Government and Legislature of each of the States.
3. All local authorities (Municipalities, Panchayats, District Boards, Improvement Trusts).
4. Other authorities within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India.

In *Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib* (1981) and *Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. Indian Institute of Chemical Biology* (2002), the Supreme Court laid down the structural test for "other authorities": if a body is financially, functionally, and administratively dominated by the government, it constitutes an instrumentality or agency of the State (e.g., LIC, ONGC, SAIL, UGC, ISRO).

Article 13 establishes the supremacy of Fundamental Rights over ordinary legislation:
- **Article 13(1)**: Pre-constitutional laws inconsistent with Part III are void to the extent of inconsistency (**Doctrine of Severability** and **Doctrine of Eclipse**).
- **Article 13(2)**: The State shall not make any post-constitutional law that takes away or abridges Part III rights.
- In *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973), the Supreme Court ruled that constitutional amendments under Article 368 cannot abrogate the **Basic Structure** of the Constitution, which includes Fundamental Rights.

#### 2. The Five Prerogative Writs: Comparative Analysis

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar termed Article 32 the "heart and soul" of the Constitution because it provides an enforceable constitutional remedy. The table below delineates the five prerogative writs issued under Article 32 (Supreme Court) and Article 226 (High Courts).

| Writ Name | Literal Latin Meaning | Primary Purpose and Scope | Target Entities Subject to Writ | Locus Standi Exception |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Habeas Corpus** | "To have the body" | Securing release of a person unlawfully or arbitrarily detained | Both public authorities and private individuals | Relaxed; friend, relative, or public-spirited citizen may petition |
| **Mandamus** | "We command" | Compelling performance of a mandatory public or statutory duty | Public officials, statutory bodies, subordinate tribunals | Strict personal interest; cannot issue against President or Governor |
| **Prohibition** | "To forbid" | Preventing an inferior court from exceeding its legal jurisdiction | Judicial and quasi-judicial tribunals only | Strict; issued while proceedings are still actively pending |
| **Certiorari** | "To be certified" | Quashing an illegal order passed without jurisdiction or in violation of natural justice | Judicial, quasi-judicial, and administrative authorities | Issued after final order or adjudication has been pronounced |
| **Quo-Warranto** | "By what authority" | Challenging the legal validity of a person\x27s claim to a public substantive office | Usurpers of public substantive statutory or constitutional offices | Completely waived; any citizen may initiate action |

#### 3. Article 32 vs. Article 226 Jurisdictional Contrast

| Dimension | Article 32 (Supreme Court) | Article 226 (High Courts) |
|---|---|---|
| **Constitutional Nature** | A Fundamental Right in itself; Supreme Court cannot refuse relief | A constitutional remedy; discretionary relief |
| **Substantive Scope** | Strictly limited to enforcement of Part III Fundamental Rights | Enforces Fundamental Rights **and** any other legal/statutory right |
| **Territorial Reach** | Pan-India jurisdiction across all states and union territories | Confined to territorial borders of the state (or cause of action arose therein) |

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Fundamental Rights Dynamics During Proclamation of Emergency

When a National Emergency is proclaimed under Article 352:
1. **Article 358**: Automatically suspends the six democratic freedoms under Article 19. However, this automatic suspension operates **only** when the Emergency is declared on grounds of war or external aggression, never on grounds of armed rebellion (44th Amendment, 1978).
2. **Article 359**: Empowers the President to suspend by executive order the right to move any court for the enforcement of specified Fundamental Rights.
3. **The Invariant Non-Suspendable Core**: The 44th Constitutional Amendment Act (1978) expressly prohibited the suspension of the enforcement of **Article 20** (Protection in respect of conviction for offences) and **Article 21** (Protection of life and personal liberty) under any circumstances, even during active wartime emergencies.

#### Karnataka State Public Administration Context: Article 371J

In KPSC KAS examinations, candidates must master the intersection of fundamental equality (Articles 15 and 16) with regional affirmative action under Article 371J:
- **98th Constitutional Amendment Act (2012)**: Inserted Article 371J into the Constitution granting special provisions to the **Kalyana-Karnataka** (formerly Hyderabad-Karnataka) region covering six backward districts: Bidar, Kalaburagi, Yadgir, Raichur, Koppal, and Ballari (plus newly carved Vijayanagara).
- **Reservation Mechanisms**: Under the Karnataka Public Employment (Requirement as to Residence) Order, Article 371J permits reservation of local cadre posts ($75\%$ to $85\%$ in local cadres and $8\%$ in state-level cadres) and educational seats for persons hailing from this region, overriding general Article 16(2) residence restrictions.

#### Worked KPSC KAS Model Answer

**Question (KPSC KAS Mains GS2):**  
*"Analyze how the expansion of Article 21 by the judiciary has enriched human dignity in India. Illustrate your answer with milestone Supreme Court judgments." (250 words, 15 marks)*

**Model Answer Scaffold:**

**1. Introduction (35 words):**  
Article 21 guarantees that *"no person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law."* Through transformative constitutionalism, the Supreme Court evolved this negative injunction into an expansive charter of positive human rights.

**2. Judicial Evolution and Milestone Doctrines (120 words):**  
- **Procedural to Substantive Due Process**: In *A.K. Gopalan* (1950), the Court adopted a pedantic literal reading. In *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978), the Court overruled Gopalan, establishing that procedure must be "just, fair, and reasonable", introducing American substantive due process.
- **Inter-relationship of Rights (Golden Triangle)**: Articles 14, 19, and 21 do not operate in silos; an infringement under Article 21 must also satisfy the arbitrariness test of Article 14 and the reasonableness test of Article 19.
- **Derivative Rights**:
  - *Right to Clean Environment*: *M.C. Mehta v. Union of India* (1987).
  - *Right to Livelihood*: *Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation* (1985).
  - *Right to Privacy*: *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017), holding privacy to be an intrinsic facet of dignity.
  - *Right to Free Legal Aid*: *M.H. Hoskot v. State of Maharashtra* (1978).

**3. Application in Administrative Governance (50 words):**  
The expansion of Article 21 imposes affirmative obligations upon the executive to ensure institutional accountability, humane prison conditions, and protection against custodial violence (*D.K. Basu guidelines*).

**4. Conclusion (35 words):**  
By interpreting "life" as meaningful existence rather than mere animal survival (*Francis Coralie Mullin*), the Indian judiciary transformed Article 21 into the spiritual core of Indian constitutional jurisprudence.
