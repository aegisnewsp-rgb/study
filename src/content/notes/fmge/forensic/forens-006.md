# Forensic Psychiatry — Insanity Defense, Indian Evidence Act, McNaghten Rules

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

- **Section 84 IPC (Section 22 BNS 2023)** is the insanity defense: a person is not criminally liable if, at the time of the act, by reason of **unsoundness of mind**, they were incapable of knowing the **nature of the act** or that it was **wrong or contrary to law**.
- **McNaghten Rules (1843)** codify the same test — "defect of reason from **disease of the mind**" causing inability to know nature/quality or wrongness of the act. Adopted verbatim into Indian law.
- **Burden of proof** lies on the accused, only on **preponderance of probabilities** (Section 105 IEA / Section 106 BSA), not beyond reasonable doubt.
- **Legal insanity ≠ medical insanity** — the court decides legal capacity, not the psychiatrist.
- **Irresistible impulse** is NOT an independent defense under Section 84 IPC.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Statutory Foundation

Section 84 IPC (replicated as Section 22 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023) embodies the **McNaghten cognitive test**. The accused must be shown to have suffered, "at the time of doing it," a defect of reason from disease of the mind that destroyed either (a) knowledge of the nature and quality of the act, or (b) knowledge that the act was wrong or contrary to law.

#### McNaghten Rules — Two Limbs

| Limb | Test | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| First | Nature & quality | Did the accused understand what they were physically doing? |
| Second | Wrongness | Did the accused know the act was legally wrong (R v Windle, UK — moral wrongness is insufficient)? |

Both limbs presuppose a **disease of the mind** — schizophrenia, manic episode, delirium, severe epilepsy-related automatism. Excluded: voluntary intoxication, which is governed separately under Section 85 IPC / Section 23 BNS, which demands involuntary intoxication causing full loss of capacity.

#### Burden & Procedure

| Question | Answer | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Who must prove insanity? | The accused | Sec 105 IEA / Sec 106 BSA |
| Standard of proof? | Preponderance of probabilities (not BRD) | Sec 105 IEA |
| Is expert opinion binding on court? | Relevant, not binding | Hari Singh Gond v State of MP |
| Lucid interval? | Presumption of sanity revives | State of Maharashtra v Sindhiya |
| Court procedure if accused is of unsound mind? | Magistrate's inquiry, possible mental-hospital custody, suspension of trial | Sec 328–339 CrPC / Sec 329 BNSS |

#### Exam-Style Pointers
- FMGE frequently tests the **exact wording** of Section 84 IPC and McNaghten's original formulation.
- Image-based MCQs on Section 84 vs Section 85 vs Section 86 (cognate provisions) are routine.
- One-liners ask: "Legal insanity is judged by the court, not the doctor" — a classic differentiator from medical insanity.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Where Indian Law Diverges from Anglo-American Practice

Indian courts have refused to import **irresistible impulse** as a stand-alone defence, holding that volitional incapacity falls within "disease of the mind" only when it destroys cognitive understanding. The cognitive test under McNaghten remains paramount. By contrast, several US jurisdictions have adopted the **Model Penal Code "substantial capacity" test** and the **Durham "product" test** — neither has statutory force in India.

#### Diminished Responsibility vs Full Insanity

Indian criminal law has no formal "diminished responsibility" doctrine equivalent to English homicide law (Homicide Act 1957). Mitigation flows through sentencing discretion or Exception 1 to Section 300 IPC (grave and sudden provocation), not partial-exculpation pleadings. FMGE candidates must not equate mental abnormality (a psychiatric concept) with Section 84 acquittal.

#### Lucid Interval & Automatism

A **lucid interval** is a period of apparent rationality during a psychotic illness, sufficient to revive the presumption of sanity if the offence is committed within it (State of Maharashtra v Sindhiya). **Automatism** — involuntary movement without conscious control (e.g., hypoglycaemic episode, reflex epilepsy) — is conceptually distinct from insanity; Indian courts evaluate it factually under Section 84 since Section 84 itself is broad enough to swallow non-insane automatism.

#### Common Exam Traps

- Quoting "burden beyond reasonable doubt on accused" — wrong. Standard is **preponderance of probabilities**.
- Citing Section 45 Indian Evidence Act as binding — wrong. It is now **Section 39 BSA**, merely **relevant**.
- Treating any **psychiatric diagnosis** as automatic acquittal — wrong. Only legal incapacity under McNaghten qualifies.

#### Practice Prompts
1. A man with paranoid schizophrenia stabs his neighbour believing him to be a demon. Under Section 84 IPC, would he satisfy the **second limb** of McNaghten? (Hint: defence fails if he knew the killing was legally wrong, even if morally justified.)
2. After voluntary consumption of alcohol, a driver kills a pedestrian. Can he claim Section 84? (Answer: No — Section 85 requires **involuntary** intoxication causing full incapacity.)
