# Part C: Inference, Attitude and Gist


### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review

The OET Test Handbook 2026 gives this sub-test 60 minutes across three parts and 42 questions, each correct answer worth one mark, with the raw total converted onto the 0 to 500 scale. Part A is allotted 15 of those minutes; Parts B and C share the other 45. Every candidate sits the same paper, whatever their profession.

Part C contains no specialist vocabulary. Every difficulty in it is calibration.

**Plausible is not supported.**

- Locate the sentence that licenses your answer
- Without one you are selecting between distractors, and confidence is not evidence

**Attitude is evidenced, not felt.** Read what the writer chooses to emphasise, how the paragraph concedes, and what is conspicuously absent.

**Concession marks the real position.** A case that opens strongly and then pivots — with "although", with "Nevertheless", with a claim that something remains unclear — is not a strong case, and the pivot is what the item wants.

**Tone is not stance.**

- Level delivery can carry a hostile conclusion
- Warm delivery can carry a friendly one
- Stance lives in what is argued and how, not in how it sounds

**Gist is proportion.**

- The main idea is the claim you could reconstruct afterwards from memory
- Too narrow if it needs the text's own vocabulary to be recognisable
- Too broad if any comparable piece on the subject could say the same

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study

The official OET Test Handbook 2026 sets out a Reading sub-test of 60 minutes across three parts, with 42 questions and the note that "Part A takes 15 minutes. Parts B and C take 45 minutes." It also states that Reading is the same for every candidate regardless of profession, and that one mark is awarded per correct answer with the score out of 42 converted to the 0 to 500 scale.

Part C is where inference lives, and it is the part candidates find hardest despite containing no specialist vocabulary — the difficulty is entirely about calibration, not language.

#### The calibration problem
A plausible answer is not a supported answer. Part C items are built so that one option is what you would say if you were inferring from general knowledge, one is what you would say if you were guessing from the tone, and one is what the text actually supports.

The discipline is to be able to point at the sentence that licenses your answer. If you cannot, you are choosing between distractors, however confident you feel.

Consider a text that notes a hospital trust introduced an electronic referral system and that referral times fell. An option saying the trust believed the new system would reduce waiting lists is not supported — nothing in the text reports anyone's belief, only the outcome. An option saying referrals completed faster after the system was introduced is supported, and it says no more than the text says.


#### Attitude is read from evidence, not impression
Part C asks what the writer thinks or how a described person feels. Read it from what they choose to emphasise and what they decline to say.

Three tells. Vocabulary: a writer who uses loaded or qualifying language is signalling something, and a writer who uses flat factual language and then concedes a limitation in the same sentence is signalling scepticism. Concession structure: a paragraph that states a strong case and then ends with "although", "Nevertheless", "it remains unclear whether" is not a strong case, and the concession is what the question wants. Detail selection: what a writer chooses not to mention is informative, but only when the omission is conspicuous — an article about a treatment failure that never mentions risk is making a point by omission.

Beware the pitfall of inferring stance from tone alone. A writer can be neutral in tone and highly sceptical in content.


#### Gist questions are about proportion
Gist items ask for the main idea, which is not the same as the title-like topic and not the first paragraph. The main idea is the claim a reasonable reader would write down afterwards.

Test a candidate by paraphrase: if it needs the text's vocabulary to be recognisable, it is probably too narrow. If it would be true of half the articles in the field, it is too broad. Main ideas are claims about what the text establishes, not subjects it mentions.


#### Common mistakes
Choosing the most medically plausible option. Plausibility is exactly what the distractors trade in, and a clinical prior has no authority over the text.

Reading the whole part before attempting items. Part C sits inside the 45 minutes shared with Part B, and reading order should be set by the questions.

Inferring a writer's opinion from tone. Attitude is carried by what is argued and how, not by how warmly it is written.

Mistaking a stated limitation for an implied criticism. "The trial was too small to detect a difference" is a limitation; calling it a failure is an interpretation the text may not support.

Answering a "main idea" item with a subheading. The main idea is the claim the whole text supports; subheadings are sections of it.

Skipping an item because two options look defensible. In Part C the ambiguity resolves on close reading of one word, so reading the stem again is faster than guessing.

### 🔴 Extended — Mastery

#### The licensing-sentence protocol

Make the discipline mechanical. Locate the sentence in the text that licenses your chosen option. Quote it back to yourself. Then test the option against it in both directions: does the sentence say this, and does it say only this? An option that adds a belief nobody reported — that a trust thought its new system would reduce waiting lists, when the text reports only the outcome — fails the first direction even when it sounds like what a sensible person would conclude. An option that stops short of what the sentence supports is right on the first direction and wrong on the second. Part C items are constructed with exactly these two traps: inference from general knowledge, and inference from tone.

#### Concession is the strongest attitude signal

Where the question asks what a writer thinks, look first at how the paragraph ends. Strong claim, then a pivot — "although", "Nevertheless", "it remains clear that" — signals that the writer's position is the weaker half. Writers who want agreement front-load the concession; writers who want resistance bury it after the evidence. Structure carries more reliable attitude signal than any adjective in the text.

#### Omission only counts when it is conspicuous

Detail selection is informative, but only when the absence is loud. An account of a treatment failure that stays silent on risk is arguing something through its silence; an account that omits something incidental is not. Ask whether a reader would notice the gap, not whether a gap exists.

#### Proportion arithmetic for a gist item

Two tests, both cheap. Paraphrase it: if your candidate is only recognisable by borrowing the text's own vocabulary, it is a sub-section rather than the main idea. Generalise it: if it would hold of half the work published on the same subject, it names a topic instead of making a claim. A main idea is a claim the text establishes; a subject is merely something the text touches on — which is also why the opening paragraph is a poor answer key on a text whose argument develops late.

#### A stated limitation is not a criticism

A trial that reports its own sample as too small to detect a difference is describing a limitation of its own evidence. Reading that as a failure imports a verdict the writer never reached. Limitation and interpretation are different speech acts in much the way that advice and instruction are different speech acts in Listening, and the items are built on that difference.

#### Where the time goes

Parts B and C share 45 minutes, so reading order is set by the questions rather than by the part boundaries. Part C is where two options can both look defensible; in that case a single word in the text settles it, and re-reading the stem costs less than a guess.
