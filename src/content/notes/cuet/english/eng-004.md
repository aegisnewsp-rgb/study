# Reading Comprehension

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your CUET UG English paper.

Reading Comprehension (RC) tests how accurately you extract meaning from unseen passages. In CUET UG English you face 5–7 passages — factual, literary, argumentative, narrative — with 4–5 objective questions each, and there is no negative marking on factual items. The skill rests on four moves: skim for gist, locate the central idea, decode vocabulary from context, and separate factual statements from inferences.

#### Must-know question types:
- Main idea / central theme of the whole passage
- Vocabulary-in-context (synonym or antonym of a highlighted word)
- Author's tone and purpose
- Inference (what the passage *implies*, not what it *states*)
- Fact-based or referential questions (who/what/where/why)

#### High-yield pointers:
- Read the first and last sentences of each paragraph first to map structure.
- Eliminate any option whose words appear in the passage but twist the meaning.
- Watch qualifiers in the stem: *except*, *NOT*, *most likely* often flip the answer.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months of preparation.

#### How a CUET RC passage is built

Each unseen passage is 300–500 words and is followed by four or five MCQs that move from literal recall toward inference. Roughly 60% of the questions are factual or vocabulary-based, and the remaining 40% demand inference, tone identification, or summarising. The passage type — narrative, descriptive, argumentative, or literary — dictates the question pattern.

#### Core skill: separating explicit from implicit

An explicit answer is *stated* in the passage and you can point to the line. An inferential answer is *implied*: the passage gives clues but the conclusion requires one logical step. A common CUET trap is to give an option that contains passage vocabulary but reverses a polarity word like *not* or *although*, turning a true statement false.

#### Tone, purpose, and vocabulary in context

Tone refers to the author's attitude — critical, appreciative, sarcastic, neutral, or reflective. Purpose is the wider goal: to inform, persuade, entertain, or analyse. Vocabulary-in-context questions give a highlighted word; the correct option matches how that word functions *in that specific sentence*, not its general dictionary meaning.

#### Strategy checklist
- Skim for the central idea in 30–45 seconds before reading the questions.
- Underline discourse connectors (*however*, *therefore*, *although*, *consequently*) — they mark argument turns.
- Eliminate options that are extreme (*always*, *never*, *completely*) when the passage uses moderate language.
- Pick the answer supported by the largest span of the passage, not a single sentence.

| Question type | What it tests | How to spot it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main idea | Central theme of the whole passage | "The passage is mainly about…" or "The central theme is…" |
| Vocabulary in context | Word meaning inside passage | "The word X is closest in meaning to…" |
| Inference | Logical conclusion from clues | "It can be inferred that…" / "The author implies…" |
| Tone / purpose | Attitude and goal | "The author's tone is…" / "The primary purpose is…" |
| Fact / detail | Direct recall | "According to the passage, who/what/why…" |

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students targeting 99+ percentile in CUET UG English.

#### Edge cases and traps specific to CUET RC

Literary passages — poems, memoir excerpts, reflective essays — appear every year. Here, the **speaker** may hold a view different from the **author**, and a frequent trap is to attribute the speaker's emotion to the writer. Another edge case is the *NOT/EXCEPT* stem: many aspirants miss the negation and pick a statement that *is* true instead of the one that is *not*. In argumentative passages, an option may quote a *counter-argument* as if it were the author's claim — reject it.

#### Connecting RC to adjacent CUET English skills

RC feeds directly into **Verbal Ability** and **Rearrangement of Sentences**: paragraphs follow the same discourse-connector logic that passages use. Practising RC also strengthens your handling of **literary devices** (metaphor, irony, synecdoche), which CUET tests both inside passages and as standalone items.

#### Common mistakes that cost marks
- Choosing a paragraph-level idea as the passage-level main idea.
- Relying on outside knowledge about a topic (climate, history, sports) instead of the given passage.
- Selecting the option that *sounds* most academic or longest, when the passage supports a shorter, simpler statement.

#### Practice prompts
1. Read any 400-word editorial from *The Hindu* or *Indian Express*. Without looking back, answer: (a) central theme in one sentence, (b) author's tone with one justification, (c) the meaning of any underlined phrase in context.
2. Take a 20-line poem (e.g. from NCERT Class 12 *Flamingo*). Identify the speaker vs the author, then answer a "what is the poet implying about…" question. Time yourself: under 6 minutes per passage.
