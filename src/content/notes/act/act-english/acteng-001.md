# Production of Writing: Topic Development and Unity — ACT English Notes

Topic development questions test whether a proposed sentence reinforces the paragraph focus or introduces an irrelevant digression. In the official ACT examination, this topic carries a weight rating of 5 out of 5 and appears consistently across testing administrations. Candidates preparing for high percentiles must master both theoretical fundamentals and rapid practical problem-solving strategies.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Weight & Frequency**: Rated 5/5 in priority; this topic appears reliably in every sitting.
- **Timing Goal**: Aim for disciplined pacing per item to leave sufficient buffer for complex multi-step problems at the end of the section.
- **Core Principle**: Evaluate sentence additions and deletions against the core purpose of the paragraph.
- **Common pitfall**: If a proposed addition is factually true but off-topic, delete it to preserve essay cohesion.
- **Systematic Strategy**: Always eliminate obviously incorrect distractors before attempting detailed calculations or granular textual analysis.
- **Answer Verification**: Cross-check final values or textual line references against the explicit wording of the prompt to avoid answering the wrong question.

#### Examiner traps

- Misreading key constraint words in the prompt (such as *except*, *not*, *least*, *integer*, *constant*).
- Falling for attractive partial answers that represent an intermediate calculation step rather than the final required quantity.
- Basing textual answers on outside background knowledge rather than direct evidence supplied within the passage.
- Spending excessive time struggling on a single difficult problem instead of flagging it, making an educated guess, and maintaining forward momentum.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Advanced variations and high-difficulty edge cases

Sentence-level additions go wrong in specific ways, and each has its own repair:

- **Claim Focus**: Underline the writer's main claim, then mark each supporting sentence as claim, evidence, or irrelevant to expose drift and misplaced material within the draft.
- **Scope Check**: Compare the opening promise with the ending's final position, cutting any paragraph that serves examples rather than clearly advancing the central idea.
- **Development Test**: For every retained point, ask what new reader understanding it supplies, then sharpen vague statements into a claim, reason, and relevant example.

#### Systematic drill schedule

Run three passes, each aimed at one of this topic's edge cases, before you attempt full essays:

- **Baseline read-through**: Take an unreviewed essay and underline its central claim in the first paragraph. Then mark every later sentence as developing that claim, illustrating it, or wandering. Delete the wanderers before you mark anything else.
- **Off-topic collection**: Collect sentences you would happily see in a well-written essay on any topic. That is what off-topic looks like, and the test works even when the sentence is perfectly grammatical.
- **Final paragraph edit**: For each remaining sentence ask what claim it advances. One that answers none gets cut regardless of how well it is written.
