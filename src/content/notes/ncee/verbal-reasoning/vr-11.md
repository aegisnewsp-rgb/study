# Simple Logic and Deduction

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Simple Logic and Deduction** tests whether you can pull a **necessarily true conclusion** out of a set of given statements. The engine is the **syllogism** — two premises, one conclusion. Master four categorical forms: **All A are B** (universal affirmative), **No A is B** (universal negative), **Some A are B** (particular affirmative), **Some A are not B** (particular negative). In NCEE Verbal Reasoning (4% weight), you will see statements, followed by conclusions labelled I, II, III, IV — pick only the ones that **must** follow. Watch the quantifier "some" — it never upgrades to "all". Conditional logic uses **modus ponens**: *If P then Q; P is true; therefore Q is true*. Use a quick Venn diagram to test any syllogism in under 30 seconds.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Syllogistic Reasoning

A **syllogism** is a three-part argument: a **major premise**, a **minor premise**, and a **conclusion**. The classic form chains overlapping categories:

- All mammals are animals.
- All dogs are mammals.
- **Therefore, all dogs are animals.**

This pattern — *All A are B; All B are C; ∴ All A are C* — is the **Barbara** figure and is always valid. Break the chain (e.g., *Some A are B; All B are C; therefore All A are C*) and the conclusion collapses.

#### Categorical Propositions

| Form | Name | Example |
|---|---|---|
| All A are B | Universal Affirmative (A) | All squares are polygons. |
| No A is B | Universal Negative (E) | No triangle is a circle. |
| Some A are B | Particular Affirmative (I) | Some students are athletes. |
| Some A are not B | Particular Negative (O) | Some birds are not flyers. |

The square of opposition governs how these relate: A and O are contradictories; E and I are contradictories; A implies I (but I does NOT imply A).

#### Immediate Inference

From a single premise you can derive:
- **Conversion** (swap subject and predicate): *Some A are B → Some B are A*.
- **Obversion** (change quality, replace predicate with its complement): *All A are B → No A is non-B*.
- **Contraposition** (convert the obverse): *All A are B → All non-B are non-A*.

#### Venn-Diagram Method

Draw two (or three) overlapping circles, label regions for A, B, C, and shade what each premise rules out. If the area representing the conclusion is fully shaded or fully empty, the conclusion is **valid**; otherwise it is only **possible**, not certain.

#### Common NCEE Format

Expect a short passage of 2–4 statements, then conclusions I–IV. Choose:
- (a) if only I follows
- (b) if only III follows
- (c) if both I and II follow
- (d) if none follows

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Modus Ponens and Modus Tollens

Conditional (If–Then) reasoning has two valid forms and several fallacies you must recognise:

- **Modus ponens** (valid): If P → Q; P; ∴ Q.
- **Modus tollens** (valid): If P → Q; ¬Q; ∴ ¬P.
- **Affirming the consequent** (invalid): If P → Q; Q; ∴ P. — a classic trap.
- **Denying the antecedent** (invalid): If P → Q; ¬P; ∴ ¬Q. — equally invalid.

#### Necessary vs Sufficient Conditions

"P is **sufficient** for Q" means P guarantees Q. "P is **necessary** for Q" means Q cannot occur without P. Confusing these is a frequent distractor: rain is sufficient for wet ground but not necessary (a sprinkler also works).

#### Ranking and Coding Sub-Types

NCEE also frames logic through **order/ranking** puzzles (A is taller than B, B is taller than C, so A is taller than C — use **transitivity**) and **coding** (item X is coded as 3-2-1, Y as 3-1-4; deduce the relationship). Build a chain diagram rather than guessing.

#### Edge Cases and Traps

- "Some" means **at least one**, not "most" or "few".
- A statement can be **true** in the real world yet **invalid** as a deduction — validity is about logical form, not facts.
- Never accept a conclusion that introduces new information not present in the premises.
- Two particular premises (I, O) generally yield no valid conclusion.

#### Practice Prompts

1. **Statements:** All pens are tools. Some tools are red. **Conclusions:** I. Some pens are red. II. Some red things are tools. — Answer: only II follows.
2. **Conditional:** If the gate is locked, the dog cannot escape. The dog escaped. **Conclusion?** The gate was not locked (modus tollens, valid).
