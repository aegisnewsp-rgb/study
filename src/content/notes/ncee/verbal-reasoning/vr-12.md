# Cause and Effect Relationships

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A cause makes something happen; an effect is what happens because of that cause. In NCEE Verbal Reasoning, you read a short passage and decide which event **triggered** which. Signal words such as *because, since, therefore, as a result, consequently, due to* point you straight to the direction of the link.

- **Cause** = the action or event that comes first and produces the outcome.
- **Effect** = the outcome that follows directly from the cause.
- **Correlation ≠ causation** — two things happening together does not mean one produced the other.
- **Immediate cause** is the closest trigger; **remote cause** sits further back in the chain.

Watch for inverted sentences where the effect is stated first, then the cause. The NCEE regularly tests whether you can flip the order back correctly.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### How a Cause-Effect Link Works

A cause-effect relationship is a one-directional connection: event A produces event B, not the reverse. In NCEE passages, the relationship is in most keys signalled by a connector word. Without that signal, two events listed in sequence can merely be coincidental, not causal.

#### Signal Words That Show Direction

| Signal word | Function in sentence |
| --- | --- |
| because, since, due to | Introduces the cause (reason) |
| therefore, hence, consequently | Introduces the effect (result) |
| as a result, so, thus | Introduces the effect after the cause |
| leads to, results in, brings about | Connects cause directly to effect |

#### Spotting the Chain

Causes in many papers form a chain rather than a single pair. Example: *Heavy rain* → *flooding on the road* → *traffic jam* → *late arrival at school*. The NCEE can ask for the **immediate cause** (flooding) or the **final effect** (late arrival). Always re-read the question stem to see which link it targets.

- The cause always comes before its effect in time, even if the sentence lists it second.
- A cause must be **sufficient** to produce the effect, not merely associated with it.
- Remote causes explain *why* the immediate cause happened; they are not wrong, but they are in most keys not the answer the NCEE wants.

#### Common Traps in NCEE Items

- Pairing two events that share timing but no causal link (correlation trap).
- Picking an effect that can happen but is not forced by the stated cause.
- Reversing cause and effect when the passage uses passive voice.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Immediate vs Remote Cause

When a passage describes several events, the **immediate (proximate) cause** is the one that directly triggers the effect, while the **remote cause** is the earlier event that set the situation up. NCEE questions in standard papers reward the proximate cause unless the stem asks "the underlying reason." Read the qualifier carefully.

#### Distinguishing Correlation from Causation

Two events rising together (ice-cream sales and swimming-pool visits) does not prove one causes the other; a third factor (hot weather) drives both. In cause-effect items, reject any answer whose link is only temporal or associative. The correct option must show that the cause **produces** the effect, not merely **accompanies** it.

#### Worked Micro-Example

Passage: *Because the power supply failed, the factory's machines stopped running, and as a result, daily output fell sharply.*

1. Identify the cause: **power supply failed**.
2. Identify the immediate effect: **machines stopped running**.
3. Identify the final effect: **daily output fell sharply**.

If asked "What was the immediate cause of the machines stopping?" — answer is the power failure, not the drop in output. If asked "What resulted from the power failure?" — both stopping and the output drop are valid, but the *most direct* effect is the machines stopping.

#### Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. Treating a sequence of events as causal when only timing is shared.
3. Choosing the remote cause when the question demands the immediate cause.
3. Reversing cause and effect because the sentence is in passive voice.

#### Practice Prompts

- "The match was cancelled **because** the pitch was waterlogged." Identify the cause and the effect, then rewrite the sentence flipping their order.
- A passage mentions both *more revision hours* and *higher test scores*. Decide whether revision causes the higher scores, the higher scores cause more revision, or a third factor (motivation) drives both.
