# Cell Biology — DU Unit B Admission (Science)

Cell Biology is the foundation on which every later Biology topic in the DU Unit B paper is built. Whether the question is about genetics (DNA replication happens in the nucleus), respiration (mitochondria), photosynthesis (chloroplast), or neural function (membrane potential), the cell is the stage on which the action occurs. The chapter, drawn from NCTB HSC Botany Chapter 1 and the matching Zoology sections, covers cell theory and its exceptions, prokaryotic versus eukaryotic organisation, plant and animal organelles, the fluid-mosaic membrane, transport across membranes, and mitotic and meiotic division. Three MCQ patterns dominate: (1) identify an organelle from a description of its function, (2) distinguish between mitosis and meiosis by counting divisions or by chromosome behaviour, and (3) compare diffusion, osmosis, and active transport across the plasma membrane. A student who can sketch a plant cell and label twelve structures correctly, then explain plasmolysis, will get nearly every Cell Biology MCQ the paper throws.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

**Cell theory (three statements and one famous exception).**

1. The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.
2. All living organisms are composed of cells and cell products.
3. All cells arise from pre-existing cells (*Omnis cellula e cellula* — Rudolf Virchow).

**Exception:** Viruses are not cells — they are acellular infectious agents that require a host cell to replicate. The DU paper occasionally asks whether viruses are "living" — the textbook answer is "no, because they cannot reproduce or carry out metabolism outside a host".

**Two organisational types.**

| Feature | Prokaryotic | Eukaryotic |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | Bacteria, blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) | Plants, animals, fungi, protists |
| True nucleus | No (DNA in nucleoid) | Yes (membrane-bound) |
| Membrane-bound organelles | No | Yes |
| Cell wall | Peptidoglycan (bacteria) | Cellulose (plants), chitin (fungi), none (animals) |
| Ribosomes | 70S (smaller) | 80S (larger, in cytoplasm); 70S in mitochondria/chloroplasts |
| DNA | Circular, no introns | Linear, with introns and histones |
| Cell size | 1–10 µm | 10–100 µm |

The 70S / 80S distinction is a frequent viva question. The 70S ribosome in chloroplasts and mitochondria is a key piece of evidence for the endosymbiotic theory — these organelles descended from free-living prokaryotes engulfed by an ancestral eukaryote.

**Animal cell organelles (the minimum memorise list).**

| Organelle | Function | Distinctive feature |
|---|---|---|
| Nucleus | Stores DNA; site of transcription | Double membrane with pores; nucleolus inside |
| Mitochondrion | ATP synthesis via Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation | Double membrane; inner folded into cristae; own DNA and ribosomes |
| Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) | Rough ER: protein folding and transport. Smooth ER: lipid synthesis, detoxification | Continuous with nuclear envelope |
| Ribosome | Protein synthesis | Two subunits (60S + 40S in eukaryotes); not membrane-bound |
| Golgi apparatus (dictyosome) | Modifies, sorts, packages proteins for secretion or delivery | Stacked flattened sacs (cisternae); receives from ER |
| Lysosome | Intracellular digestion; hydrolytic enzymes at acidic pH | Membrane-bound; absent in plants |
| Centrosome (with centrioles) | Microtubule organising centre; spindle formation in animal cells | Two perpendicular centrioles |
| Cytoskeleton | Cell shape, intracellular transport, cell movement | Microfilaments (actin), microtubules (tubulin), intermediate filaments |

**Plant cell organelles (extra vs animal).**

| Organelle | Function | Distinctive feature |
|---|---|---|
| Cell wall | Rigid support outside the plasma membrane | Cellulose + pectin; primary and secondary wall |
| Chloroplast | Photosynthesis (light reactions + Calvin cycle) | Double membrane; thylakoids stacked into grana; own DNA and ribosomes |
| Large central vacuole | Storage of water, ions, pigments; turgor pressure | Often occupies > 80% of cell volume |
| Plasmodesmata | Cytoplasmic channels connecting adjacent plant cells | Through cell walls |
| Plastids | Storage (leucoplasts) and pigmentation (chromoplasts) | Family of organelles including chloroplasts |

Plastids interconvert: chloroplasts in unripe fruit become chromoplasts (carotenoid storage) as the fruit ripens; leucoplasts in roots store starch.

**Fluid-mosaic membrane model (Singer and Nicolson, 1972).** A phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins. Phospholipids have hydrophilic heads (face water) and hydrophobic tails (face inward). The membrane is *fluid* (lipids and proteins move laterally) and *mosaic* (proteins are scattered, varied in shape and function).

**Membrane transport — three modes to distinguish.**

| Mode | Energy | Direction | Carrier needed | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Diffusion | None | High → low concentration | No | O₂ into cells; CO₂ out |
| Osmosis | None | High water → low water (across semipermeable membrane) | No (water via aquaporins) | Water uptake by plant roots |
| Facilitated diffusion | None | High → low | Yes (channel or carrier protein) | Glucose into red blood cells via GLUT1 |
| Active transport | ATP | Low → high (against gradient) | Yes (pump protein) | Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase; proton pumps in stomach lining |

**Tonicity terms.** A solution is **hypertonic** to a cell if its solute concentration is higher (cell shrinks / undergoes plasmolysis in plant cells). **Hypotonic** means lower solute (cell swells / becomes turgid in plant cells). **Isotonic** means equal solute (no net water movement). Plant cells prefer isotonic or slightly hypotonic surroundings; animal cells prefer isotonic.

**Mitosis vs meiosis (memorise the differences).**

| Feature | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Number of divisions | 1 | 2 (Meiosis I and II) |
| Daughter cells | 2, genetically identical to parent | 4, genetically different |
| Chromosome number | Same as parent (diploid → diploid) | Halved (diploid → haploid) |
| Where it happens | Somatic cells | Germ cells (in gonads) |
| Crossing over | No | Yes (in Prophase I) |
| Homologous pairing | No | Yes (synapsis in Prophase I) |
| Function | Growth, repair, asexual reproduction | Production of gametes; genetic variation |

**Mitosis phases to know in order.** Prophase (chromatin condenses into chromosomes, nuclear envelope breaks down), Metaphase (chromosomes align at the equator), Anaphase (sister chromatids separate to opposite poles), Telophase (chromosomes decondense, nuclear envelope reforms). Cytokinesis (division of cytoplasm) overlaps with telophase.

**Meiosis I phases.** Prophase I (with sub-stages: leptotene, zygotene — synapsis, pachytene — crossing over, diplotene — chiasmata visible, diakinesis), Metaphase I (homologous pairs at equator), Anaphase I (homologous chromosomes separate; sister chromatids stay together), Telophase I. Meiosis II resembles mitosis but with haploid cells.

### 🟡 Standard — Exam Prep (3d-3w)

#### Mitochondria and chloroplasts as semi-autonomous organelles

Both organelles have a double membrane, their own circular DNA, and 70S ribosomes. They grow and divide independently of the cell. This is the basis of the **endosymbiotic theory** (Lynn Margulis, 1967): an ancestral archaeal host engulfed an aerobic proteobacterium (becoming the mitochondrion) and later a cyanobacterium (becoming the chloroplast). Evidence: double membrane (consistent with engulfment), circular DNA without histones, 70S ribosomes, binary fission as the division mechanism, and the existence of modern free-living relatives of each (e.g., *Paracoccus* for the mitochondrion, cyanobacteria for the chloroplast).

A DU Unit B MCQ occasionally tests this — "which organelle has its own DNA?" — answer: nucleus, mitochondria, and chloroplast (plus, in plants, the small amount in plastids generally). The mitochondria and chloroplast DNA encodes some of their own proteins and RNAs; the rest is encoded in the nuclear genome and the proteins are imported.

#### Endomembrane system and protein sorting

Proteins destined for secretion, the plasma membrane, or lysosomes are synthesised on the rough ER, folded with the help of chaperones (e.g., BiP), and packaged into COPII-coated vesicles for transport to the Golgi. In the Golgi, sugars are trimmed and modified; proteins are sorted into clathrin-coated vesicles destined for the plasma membrane, lysosomes, or secretion. The signal hypothesis (Blobel, 1999 Nobel Prize) holds that an N-terminal signal peptide directs the ribosome to the ER.

**Protein sorting rules of thumb:**
- N-terminal signal peptide + stop-transfer anchor → integral membrane protein (ER).
- N-terminal signal peptide cleaved after entry → secreted or lumenal protein.
- Mannose-6-phosphate tag → lysosome.
- Nuclear localisation signal (NLS) — short stretch of basic residues — → nucleus via importin.

This level of detail is more than DU Unit B usually tests, but the rough ER → Golgi → secretion pathway is a common MCQ.

#### The fluid-mosaic membrane in more detail

The phospholipid bilayer is asymmetric: phosphatidylserine and phosphatidylethanolamine concentrate in the inner leaflet, phosphatidylcholine and sphingomyelin in the outer leaflet. Flip-flop between leaflets is rare (requires flippase enzymes); lateral diffusion is fast.

**Membrane proteins** come in two main types:
- **Integral (intrinsic)** — embedded in the bilayer; often span it (transmembrane). Detergents are required to extract them.
- **Peripheral (extrinsic)** — attached to the membrane surface or to integral proteins; released by mild treatments like pH or ionic strength changes.

Membrane protein functions include receptors, transporters, enzymes, anchors for the cytoskeleton, and cell-adhesion molecules. The glycophorin A in red blood cells is a classic single-pass transmembrane protein with a hydrophobic α-helix spanning the bilayer.

**Cholesterol** modulates membrane fluidity: at high temperatures it restrains phospholipid motion, reducing fluidity; at low temperatures it prevents tight packing, increasing fluidity. Animal cells in cold climates tend to have more unsaturated phospholipids (more kinks, looser packing) to maintain fluidity.

#### Transport across membranes — quantitative

For simple diffusion, **Fick's law** says the rate of diffusion $J$ across a membrane is
$$J = -D \frac{\Delta C}{\Delta x},$$
where $D$ is the diffusion coefficient, $\Delta C$ is the concentration difference, and $\Delta x$ is the membrane thickness. Larger surface area and steeper gradient → faster diffusion.

For osmosis, the **water potential** formalism is used in plant physiology:
$$\Psi = \Psi_s + \Psi_p,$$
where $\Psi_s$ is solute potential (always ≤ 0; more solute → more negative) and $\Psi_p$ is pressure potential (often turgor pressure in plant cells; positive when the cell presses on the wall). Water flows from higher $\Psi$ to lower $\Psi$. Pure water has $\Psi = 0$. A cell in equilibrium has $\Psi_{\text{cell}} = \Psi_{\text{outside}}$.

In a hypertonic solution, $\Psi_{\text{outside}}$ is very negative (lots of solute), so water leaves the cell; the plant cell undergoes **plasmolysis** — the protoplast shrinks away from the cell wall. In a hypotonic solution, water enters; the cell becomes turgid. Animal cells lack a wall, so in a hypotonic solution they swell and may burst (lysis); in a hypertonic solution they shrivel (crenation).

**Active transport: the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase** is the textbook example. It pumps 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per ATP hydrolyzed, against their concentration gradients. This maintains the resting membrane potential of animal cells (≈ −70 mV inside relative to outside) and consumes a large fraction of a typical cell's ATP budget. The Na⁺ gradient it creates is also the energy source for secondary active transport (e.g., glucose uptake by SGLT in the intestine).

#### Cell cycle and mitosis in detail

The cell cycle has four phases: G₁ (growth, organelle production), S (DNA replication), G₂ (further growth, preparation for mitosis), M (mitosis). G₁, S, and G₂ together are **interphase**. Non-dividing cells exit the cycle into **G₀**.

**Checkpoints** ensure proper progression:
- **G₁/S checkpoint** — checks DNA integrity and cell size before committing to replication.
- **G₂/M checkpoint** — checks that DNA has been fully and correctly replicated before entering mitosis.
- **Spindle assembly checkpoint** — at metaphase, ensures all kinetochores are attached to spindle fibres before anaphase proceeds.

Cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) and cyclins drive the transitions. p53 is a key tumour suppressor that arrests the cycle at G₁/S when DNA is damaged; loss of p53 is implicated in many cancers. The DU paper rarely asks about specific cyclins, but the existence of checkpoints is fair game.

#### Meiosis and genetic variation

The three sources of genetic variation generated by meiosis:
1. **Crossing over** in Prophase I — homologous chromosomes exchange segments at chiasmata, producing new allele combinations on each chromatid.
2. **Independent assortment** of homologous pairs at Metaphase I — the orientation of each pair is random, so with $n$ pairs there are $2^n$ possible combinations of maternal vs paternal chromosomes in each gamete. Humans have 23 pairs → $2^{23} \approx 8.4$ million combinations from independent assortment alone.
3. **Random fertilisation** — which sperm meets which egg is independent, multiplying the combinations further.

A common DU MCQ: "in Prophase I of meiosis, the event that produces new allele combinations is..." Answer: crossing over (also accepted: recombination). Don't confuse with "synapsis" (which is the pairing itself, not the exchange).

#### Worked example

**Problem.** A plant cell with solute potential $\Psi_s = -0.6$ MPa and pressure potential $\Psi_p = 0.4$ MPa is placed in a solution with $\Psi_s = -0.2$ MPa and $\Psi_p = 0$ MPa. Determine the direction of net water movement and what happens to the cell.

**Solution.** Compute water potentials:
- Cell: $\Psi_{\text{cell}} = -0.6 + 0.4 = -0.2$ MPa.
- Solution: $\Psi_{\text{soln}} = -0.2 + 0 = -0.2$ MPa.

The water potentials are equal — this is **isotonic** for the cell. There is no net water movement, and the cell is at equilibrium.

Now move the cell to a solution with $\Psi_s = -0.8$ MPa and $\Psi_p = 0$. New $\Psi_{\text{soln}} = -0.8$ MPa. Since $\Psi_{\text{cell}} > \Psi_{\text{soln}}$, water moves from cell to solution. The cell loses water, $\Psi_p$ drops to 0, and the protoplast shrinks away from the cell wall: **plasmolysis**. If the cell sits in the hypertonic solution long enough, it dies.

Conversely, moving the cell to pure water ($\Psi_s = 0$, $\Psi_p = 0$) means $\Psi_{\text{soln}} = 0 > \Psi_{\text{cell}} = -0.2$ MPa. Water flows into the cell; $\Psi_p$ rises; the cell becomes **turgid**. Because of the rigid cell wall, the cell does not burst — the wall pushes back until $\Psi_p$ balances $\Psi_s$ and equilibrium is restored at a higher turgor.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (1mo+)

#### Membrane potentials and the Nernst equation

A membrane potential arises when ion concentrations differ across a semipermeable membrane. The **Nernst equation** gives the equilibrium potential for one ion:
$$E = \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{[\text{ion}]_{\text{out}}}{[\text{ion}]_{\text{in}}},$$
where $R$ is the gas constant, $T$ absolute temperature, $z$ the ion charge, and $F$ Faraday's constant. At body temperature (37 °C, 310 K), $RT/F \approx 26.7$ mV. For K⁺ in a typical neuron, $[\text{K}]_{\text{in}} = 140$ mM and $[\text{K}]_{\text{out}} = 5$ mM, giving $E_K \approx -90$ mV. The resting potential is set by the weighted contributions of all permeant ions, computed via the Goldman equation.

#### Signal transduction across the membrane

A signalling molecule (e.g., a hormone) binds a transmembrane receptor; the receptor undergoes a conformational change; an intracellular signalling cascade amplifies the signal. Three common classes:
- **G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs)** — activate a G-protein, which activates an effector (e.g., adenylyl cyclase → cAMP). cAMP activates protein kinase A (PKA), which phosphorylates target proteins.
- **Receptor tyrosine kinases (RTK)** — ligand binding causes dimerisation and cross-phosphorylation of intracellular tyrosine residues, recruiting downstream signalling proteins.
- **Ion-channel receptors** — ligand-gated channels open directly (e.g., the acetylcholine receptor at the neuromuscular junction).

The cAMP second messenger system lets a single extracellular signal amplify into many intracellular events — one epinephrine molecule can produce millions of cAMP molecules.

#### Cancer and cell-cycle regulation

Cancer cells evade the checkpoints that normally restrain division. Key mutations:
- Loss of **p53** (the "guardian of the genome") — many cancers, including ~50% of all solid tumours.
- Overexpression of **cyclin D** or **CDK4** — drives G₁/S progression.
- Loss of **Rb** (retinoblastoma protein) — releases E2F transcription factor and pushes the cycle forward.
- Activation of **telomerase** — maintains telomere length, allowing unlimited divisions.

The DU paper occasionally asks what p53 does; the answer is "arrests the cell cycle at G₁/S in response to DNA damage" or "triggers apoptosis if damage is irreparable".

#### Apoptosis vs necrosis

- **Apoptosis** is programmed cell death — cell shrinks, chromatin condenses, membrane blebs, the cell breaks into apoptotic bodies that are phagocytosed. No inflammation. Caspase enzymes execute the program.
- **Necrosis** is uncontrolled death from injury — cell swells, membrane ruptures, contents spill out, inflammation follows.

Apoptosis is essential in development (webbing between fetal fingers is removed by apoptosis) and in immune tolerance (self-reactive lymphocytes are eliminated). Defective apoptosis contributes to cancer and autoimmunity.

### Common traps

- **Conflating chloroplasts and mitochondria.** Both have double membranes and their own DNA, but only chloroplasts have thylakoids/grana and only mitochondria have cristae. The DU paper tests this distinction.
- **Forgetting that plant cells have lysosome-equivalent function.** Plants do not have lysosomes; the equivalent function is carried out by the **vacuole** and by ER-derived vesicles. Saying "plant cells have lysosomes" is wrong.
- **Confusing plasmolysis with haemolysis.** Plasmolysis is the shrinking of a plant cell protoplast away from the wall in hypertonic solution. Haemolysis (or cytolysis) is the bursting of a red blood cell in hypotonic solution. Different organisms, different mechanisms.
- **Treating osmosis as diffusion of solutes.** Osmosis is the diffusion of *water* (the solvent) across a semipermeable membrane, not the diffusion of the solute. Solutes that cross the membrane freely are not "osmosed".
- **Misstating chromosome behaviour in meiosis I vs II.** In Anaphase I, homologous chromosomes separate (sister chromatids stay together). In Anaphase II, sister chromatids separate. Saying "sister chromatids separate in Meiosis I" is a classic wrong answer.
- **Calling the cell wall a "rigid barrier that prevents all transport".** Plasmodesmata allow cytoplasmic continuity between adjacent plant cells. The cell wall is permeable to water and small solutes; only the plasma membrane is selective.
- **Confusing "active transport" with "any transport that uses energy".** Active transport specifically moves solutes *against* their concentration gradient using ATP (or another ion gradient). Facilitated diffusion uses a carrier but no energy, and goes down the gradient.
