# Skills Breakdown and Section Weighting — MCAT CARS

Every planning decision in CARS starts with three numbers. The section is **53 exam questions in 90 minutes**, all passage-based. Those questions draw from **three** skills: Foundations of Comprehension (about 30%), Reasoning Within the Text (about 30%), and Reasoning Beyond the Text (about 40%). Passage content splits roughly **50% humanities / 50% social sciences**. Nothing else about CARS is a weighting decision, and these are the only numbers worth building a schedule around.

AAMC adds an important caveat, and it cuts against over-fitting: the percentages "have been approximated to the nearest 5% and will vary by test from one test to another," because test forms control for difficulty, group items that depend on a single passage, and include unscored field-test questions. Treat 30/30/40 as a study-priority signal, not a scoring guarantee.

### The "CARS Skill 4" myth

You will meet study material — and generated study material — claiming CARS has a fourth skill called Quantitative Reasoning, usually described as reading tables and graphs inside CARS passages. It does not. AAMC publishes three CARS skills and the overview page enumerates exactly those three. Quantitative Reasoning is a content category in a *different* MCAT section — the Psychological, Social, and Biological Foundations of Behavior section, alongside Psychological and Social Foundations and Biological and Biochemical Foundations. CARS, by design, asks no subject-content questions: "no additional coursework or specific knowledge is required."

If a note, a timetable, or a coaching video tells you to drill a fourth CARS skill, it is describing a section of the exam you are not taking. The real fourth-number problem is 40%, and it belongs to Reasoning Beyond the Text.

### Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

#### The three numbers

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Questions | 53, all passage-based |
| Time | 90 minutes |
| Foundations of Comprehension | about 30% |
| Reasoning Within the Text | about 30% |
| Reasoning Beyond the Text | about 40% |
| Passage content | Humanities about 50%, Social Sciences about 50% |
| Passage length | typically 500–600 words |

#### What each share means for your practice

- **Skill 1 at 30%** — you must be able to state the passage's claim after one reading. If you cannot, nothing else is answerable.
- **Skill 2 at 30%** — argument work: strengthen, weaken, assumption, evidence. The most learnable of the three, because the moves repeat across passages.
- **Skill 3 at 40%** — application to new situations and outside knowledge. The largest slice and the one candidates most often under-train.
- **Passage content 50/50** — you cannot skip "the boring humanities passage" as a category and expect the math to hold. About half the section will not look like your science background.

#### One-pass drill

1. Take one timed CARS passage set. Do not check answers first.
2. For every wrong answer, tag it: Skill 1, Skill 2, or Skill 3.
3. Tally. A candidate who is weak at Skill 3 will see it immediately — and that is the one worth the time.

### Standard — Regular Study (2d-2mo)

#### Turning the weights into a schedule

If you have two weeks and no CARS baseline, the weighting argues for roughly even attention on Skills 1 and 2 (they are prerequisites) with the largest block on Skill 3.

- **Weeks 1–2:** Skill 2 drilling. Strengthen/weaken/assumption recognition is rule-governed and gives the fastest score movement.
- **Weeks 3–4:** Skill 3 drilling. Infer, analogy, hypothetical application, outside-knowledge application.
- **Skill 1 is maintained, not studied.** Keep it warm by reading one passage per set for meaning; it is a prerequisite that improves passively.
- **Split your passage practice 50/50** between humanities and social sciences content. Your instinct to avoid one of them costs you in the section that is 50% the other.

#### Reading the section's own structural facts

AAMC's overview describes passages as "relatively short, typically between 500 and 600 words," but "complex, often thought-provoking pieces of writing with sophisticated vocabulary and, at times, intricate writing styles." That combination is why a 500-word passage can take four minutes and still yield six questions. The length is not your difficulty signal — the writing style is.

Two further constraints shape practice:

- **Everything is self-contained.** No outside knowledge is needed, which means time spent pre-reading background is time lost from the 90 minutes.
- **Content spans ethics, philosophy, studies of diverse cultures, population health, and a wide range of social sciences and humanities disciplines.** You are practising argument analysis, not subject recall.

### Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo-6mo)

#### How the weights interact

The three skills are sequential inside every passage, not parallel buckets of questions. Skill 1 makes the passage legible; Skill 2 lets you evaluate the argument as built; Skill 3 asks what follows beyond it. A 40% Skill 3 share is only answerable if the first 60% is already secure — which is why Skill 1 and Skill 2 drilling has a multiplier effect rather than a linear one.

The 50/50 passage split has a similar interaction. Humanities passages are conversational and opinionated, so tone carries the argument; social sciences passages are factual and scientific in tone, so trend and cause carry it. A candidate trained on only one register systematically misreads the other's tone, and misreading tone produces exactly the "the author must agree with me" errors that Reasoning Within the Text items are built to catch.

#### Common errors and the correction

- **"Skill 3 is 40%, so I should do Skill 3 drills."** Only after Skill 1 and 2 are stable. The weights describe the target distribution, not the training order.
- **"The percentages are exact."** AAMC says they are approximated to the nearest 5% and vary per test form. Never compute a predicted score from them.
- **"I'll skip humanities passages to save time."** About half of passage content is humanities. Skipping them costs more than they cost in reading.
- **"CARS has a quantitative skill."** It does not. See the myth section above.

#### Exam technique

- Budget from the 53/90 ratio: nine passages in ninety minutes is roughly ten minutes per passage, questions included.
- Read for claim and structure on the first pass, not for detail. Detail is re-readable; the skeleton is not recoverable later.
- Track which skill you are answering as you go — the tags come free and make a post-test analysis possible.
- Use the platform's calculator and flag-for-review sparingly. CARS needs neither; the time goes to reading.

#### Specification reference

The CARS section is 53 exam questions in 90 minutes, drawn from Foundations of Comprehension (about 30%), Reasoning Within the Text (about 30%), and Reasoning Beyond the Text (about 40%), with passage content split about 50% humanities and 50% social sciences. AAMC states these percentages are approximated to the nearest 5% and vary from test to test. There is no fourth CARS skill.
