# The Scientific Method

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> The single fact that decides the answer: a hypothesis must be **falsifiable**, not just "guessable."

The scientific method is a step-by-step procedure for turning an observation into reliable knowledge. It runs in a loop: Observation → Question → Hypothesis → Experiment → Analysis → Conclusion → Replication. Reviewers in the UAE University CAT in most keys test three micro-skills here: spotting the independent variable, naming the next step in the cycle, and deciding whether a claim counts as science.

| Term | One-line meaning |
|---|---|
| **Hypothesis** | A **falsifiable** statement that predicts an outcome |
| **Theory** | A repeatedly tested, well-substantiated **explanation** |
| **Law** | A description of a consistent **pattern** in nature, not a proof of why |
| **Peer review** | Independent experts checking the work before publication |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook — "OHECAR":** Observation, Hypothesis, Experiment, Conclusion, Analysis, Replication. Walk through these letters in order when a passage asks "what comes next?"

At 3% weight, one well-rehearsed MCQ is the realistic payoff. Skip the history; practise the variables.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### The cycle, in usable detail
Start with an **observation** — something your senses or instruments detect. Convert it into a **question**, then write a **hypothesis** as an *if–then* statement that can, in principle, be shown wrong. Design an **experiment** in which you change one thing, measure one thing, and hold everything else steady. **Analyse** the numbers or descriptions, draw a **conclusion**, and let other people **replicate** the result. Failure to replicate forces a revised hypothesis — the loop is the point.

#### Variables, in plain language
| Variable type | What the researcher does | Example (plant growth study) |
|---|---|---|
| **Independent** | Manipulates it | Amount of sunlight per day |
| **Dependent** | Measures it | Final height in cm |
| **Controlled** | Holds it constant | Water volume, soil type, pot size |

Independent goes in the method column; dependent goes in the results column. Controlled variables go in *both* — same value throughout. A confounder is any uncontrolled variable that can explain the result; examiners plant it deliberately.

#### Concept comparison matrix
| Concept | Looks like | But is actually |
|---|---|---|
| Hypothesis | A question or a guess | A **testable, falsifiable prediction** |
| Theory | An untested idea | A **body of tested explanations** supported across many studies |
| Law | A proven fact | A **description** of regularity; does not explain mechanism |
| Correlation | Causation | Two variables can move together with **no causal link** |
| Observation | Inference | Observation is what the senses record; inference is the **interpretation** |
| Replication | Same experiment once | Independent teams repeating under varied conditions |

> 📌 **Formula Check (proportions, not equations):** Required sample size scales with expected effect size and desired statistical power; the CAT rarely tests the maths, but expect a question asserting "n = 30 is always enough" — it is not. Power analysis is the proper check, and a small CAT item has been known to mark "unscientific" any conclusion drawn from an n of 3.

#### Worked scenario
A magazine reports that students who drink more coffee score higher on memory tests. Students choose: *"This proves coffee improves memory."* The correct response is that **correlation is not causation**: third variables (study habits, sleep, motivation) can explain both. A scientific test would add a control group, randomise participants to coffee vs. no-coffee arms, blind the testers, and pre-register the hypothesis.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** "Proves" is the giveaway word. Experiments **support** hypotheses; they never absolutely prove them in a finite sample. Pick the answer containing *supports*, *suggests*, or *is consistent with*.

#### Deductive vs. inductive reasoning
**Induction** builds a general rule from specific cases (every swan seen is white → all swans are white). **Deduction** draws a specific prediction from a general rule (all swans are white → this bird is a swan, therefore white). Scientific work uses both: induction to formulate hypotheses from data, deduction to test predictions against them. A statement that cannot be tested by either route is outside science.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Where the method breaks down
Edge cases are where CAT distractors earn their keep. Keep these six traps in your peripheral vision:

1. **Black swan problem.** Induction cannot reach universal certainty — one counter-example overturns a "law" derived only from cases seen.
2. **Theory-laden observation.** Even raw data is filtered through prior theory; two researchers can record different "observations" of the same event.
3. **Underdetermination.** Multiple hypotheses can fit the same dataset; the data alone never force a single conclusion.
4. **Publication bias.** Positive results publish more in many papers than null results, so the literature overstates effects.
5. **p-hacking.** Running many comparisons, then reporting only the significant ones, inflates false-positive rates. Preregistration of hypotheses is the modern defence.
6. **Reproducibility crisis.** A non-trivial share of published findings in psychology and biomedicine have failed independent replication (the **Open Science Collaboration, 2015** estimated roughly 35–50% for psychology findings, though the figure is debated and the CAT will not require memorising the percentage).

#### Adjacent topics that share vocabulary

| Adjacent field | Overlapping word | What it actually means there |
|---|---|---|
| Statistics | Hypothesis | A **statistical hypothesis** (H₀: no effect) is one species of the broader scientific hypothesis |
| Philosophy of science | Falsifiability | Popper's **demarcation criterion**: science = statements that can in principle be refuted by evidence |
| Engineering | Design of experiments | DOE uses Taguchi/RSM methods that formalise the same independent/dependent logic |
| Medicine | RCT | Randomised controlled trial = the medical expression of the experimental step |

#### Quantitative vs. qualitative data
Numbers come from instruments (height, absorbance, counts). Words come from interviews, open-ended surveys, field notes. Both are scientific when collected systematically, but their analysis differs — descriptive and inferential statistics for the former; coding, thematic analysis, or triangulation for the latter. A CAT question can list a study type and ask which data form fits it.

#### Two advanced practice prompts
- **Prompt A.** A study reports a correlation of *r* = 0.62 between ice-cream sales and drowning deaths. Design two competing scientific hypotheses that can explain it and sketch a single experiment that distinguishes them.
- **Prompt B.** A "memory water" brand cites a single before–after trial of 8 customers. Identify at least four departures from the scientific method in the study design, and rewrite the conclusion using language that the published paper should have used.

> 🧭 **Field Note:** When the distractors get long, the correct answer is almost always the shortest one that names a *mechanism* (controlled variable, replication, falsifiability) rather than a *feeling* (obvious, intuitive, widely believed).
