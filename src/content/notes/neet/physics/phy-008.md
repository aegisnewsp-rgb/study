# Mechanical Properties

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Mechanical Properties of Solids describes how rigid bodies deform and recover when external forces act on them. NEET UG tests this unit with roughly 1 MCQ per paper (3% weight in Physics), usually a numerical on Young's modulus or a conceptual question on the stress–strain curve.

| Term | Definition | SI unit |
|---|---|---|
| Stress (σ) | Restoring force per unit area inside the body, σ = F/A | Pa (N m⁻²) |
| Strain (ε) | Relative deformation, ε = ΔL/L (longitudinal), ΔV/V (volumetric), θ (shear) | Dimensionless |
| Young's modulus (Y) | σ/ε in the longitudinal direction | Pa |
| Bulk modulus (B) | −ΔP / (ΔV/V), measures volume elasticity | Pa |
| Shear modulus (G) | (F/A)/θ for tangential deformation | Pa |
| Poisson's ratio (σ) | Lateral strain ÷ longitudinal strain | Dimensionless, 0.2–0.5 for metals |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** "**Y B G P** = **Y**ou **B**end, **G**et **P**oissoned" — the four elastic constants (Young's Y, Bulk B, Shear G, Poisson's σ). And remember **YBG = 3B(1−2σ) = 2G(1+σ)** links them all.

Within the **elastic limit**, stress is directly proportional to strain (Hooke's law). Beyond it the body shows plastic deformation and never returns to its original shape.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Stress, Strain and Hooke's Law

When a deforming force F acts on a body, internal restoring forces develop so that total internal force per unit area balances F. This internal intensity is **stress** (σ = F/A). The corresponding relative change in dimension is **strain** (ε = ΔL / L for a wire stretched by ΔL).

Hooke's law states that within the elastic limit, σ ∝ ε, so σ/ε equals a constant called the modulus of elasticity. Three moduli handle three independent deformation modes:

- **Young's modulus (Y)** — longitudinal stretching or compression along one axis.
- **Shear modulus (G or η)** — tangential force producing angular shear θ.
- **Bulk modulus (B)** — uniform pressure change ΔP producing volumetric strain ΔV/V.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Modulus | Deformation type | Formula | Typical magnitude (steel) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Young's Y | Length change | Y = (F/A) / (ΔL/L) | ~200 GPa |
| Bulk B | Volume change | B = −ΔP / (ΔV/V) | ~160 GPa |
| Shear G | Shape change | G = (F/A) / θ | ~80 GPa |
| Poisson's σ | Lateral vs axial strain | σ = (Δd/d) / (ΔL/L) | ~0.28 for steel |

#### Inter-Relations Between Moduli

The three moduli and Poisson's ratio are not independent. Two key relations appear in NEET assertion-reason items:

- **Y = 3B(1 − 2σ)** — links longitudinal, volume and lateral responses.
- **Y = 2G(1 + σ)** — links longitudinal and shear responses.

Elastic potential energy per unit volume stored in a stretched wire is U/V = ½ × stress × strain = ½ Y ε² (SI unit J m⁻³).

#### Stress–Strain Curve for a Ductile Metal

A typical ductile metal shows (in order): **proportional limit**, **elastic limit**, **yield point**, **plastic region**, **ultimate tensile stress** and finally **fracture point**. Brittle materials such as glass break shortly after the elastic limit with little plastic flow.

> 📌 **Formula Check:** All moduli have SI unit pascal (Pa) and dimension [M L⁻¹ T⁻²]. Strain is dimensionless, so any ratio of modulus to strain yields stress.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** A steel wire of length 2.0 m and cross-sectional area 4.0 × 10⁻⁶ m² is stretched by 0.20 mm under a load. Given Young's modulus of steel Y = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa, find (a) the stress, (b) the strain, and (c) the deforming force. Which of the following is correct?

(a) σ = 2.0 × 10⁷ Pa, F = 80 N  
(b) σ = 2.0 × 10⁸ Pa, F = 800 N  
(c) σ = 4.0 × 10⁷ Pa, F = 160 N  
(d) σ = 1.0 × 10⁸ Pa, F = 400 N

#### Solution:

Step 1 — Strain:
ε = ΔL / L = (0.20 × 10⁻³ m) / 2.0 m = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ (dimensionless).

Step 2 — Stress from Hooke's law:
σ = Y × ε = (2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa) × (1.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 2.0 × 10⁷ Pa.

Step 3 — Force from σ = F/A:
F = σ × A = (2.0 × 10⁷ Pa) × (4.0 × 10⁻⁶ m²) = 80 N.

Correct option: **(a) σ = 2.0 × 10⁷ Pa, F = 80 N.**

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students often convert 0.20 mm incorrectly (use 0.20 × 10⁻³ m, not 0.20 × 10⁻⁶ m) or forget to multiply stress by area when finding force. Always re-state every length in metres before substituting.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Boundary Conditions

The idealised Hookean region is short. Real materials deviate because of microstructural defects, temperature rise, and rate of loading. Several boundary conditions deserve attention:

1. **Negative sign in bulk modulus** — B = −ΔP / (ΔV/V). The minus sign is mandatory: an increase in pressure must decrease volume, so ΔV is negative and B stays positive. Dropping the minus sign makes B negative, a nonsense value examiners love to test.
2. **Poisson's ratio limits** — theoretical bounds are −1 < σ < 0.5 for isotropic stable materials. Cork has σ ≈ 0 (almost no lateral expansion), while rubber approaches 0.5.
3. **Wire vs rod area** — for a uniform wire, A is constant; for a tapered or composite rod, integrate σ(x) dA or use equivalent cross-section.
4. **Shear area choice** — in G problems, A is the area parallel to the tangential force, not the face receiving the force vector.
5. **Thermal stress** — a rod clamped between rigid walls develops σ = Y α ΔT, where α is the linear expansion coefficient. This combines Thermal Physics with elasticity and is a frequent NEET assertion-reason question.
6. **Breaking stress vs breaking force** — breaking stress is a material property (Pa); breaking force depends on cross-section. Two wires of the same material but different radii break under different loads.

#### Advanced Traps and Exceptions

| Trap | Why it happens | Correct approach |
|---|---|---|
| Treating pressure and stress as identical | Both have SI unit Pa | Pressure is external; stress is the internal restoring intensity |
| Using Y = stress × strain | Misread Hooke's law as product | Y = stress / strain |
| Applying Hooke's law past the elastic limit | Curve appears nearly linear | Switch to plastic-region analysis; Y is no longer constant |
| Confusing G with coefficient of viscosity η | Both use Greek letter η | G is shear modulus (Pa); η is dynamic viscosity (Pa·s) |
| Assuming rubber is "weak" | Low Y ≈ 5 MPa | Low modulus means high elasticity, not low strength |

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics

Mechanical Properties links directly to **Mechanical Properties of Fluids** (Pascal's law, hydraulic stress), **Gravitation** (elastic tidal deformation), **Oscillations** (Y determines the effective spring constant k = YA/L of a wire), and **Thermal Physics** (thermal stress). In **Oscillations**, a mass m hanging from a wire gives time period T = 2π√(mL/(YA)), so Y controls stiffness.

#### Practice Prompts

1. A copper rod of length 1.5 m, area 2 cm², supports a 2000 N load. If Y = 1.2 × 10¹¹ Pa, compute elongation and the elastic PE stored. (Answer: ΔL = 1.25 × 10⁻⁴ m; U ≈ 0.125 J.)
2. A spherical steel ball of radius 5 cm is lowered into the ocean to depth 4 km. Given B = 1.6 × 10¹¹ Pa and seawater density 1030 kg m⁻³, find the fractional decrease in volume. (Answer: ΔV/V = ρgh/B ≈ 2.5 × 10⁻⁴.)

> 💡 **High-Yield Revision Hook:** Memorise the SI chain — 1 Pa = 1 N m⁻², and 1 GPa = 10⁹ Pa. Most NEET numericals in this unit give Y in GPa; convert to Pa before substituting into ΔL = FL/(AY).
