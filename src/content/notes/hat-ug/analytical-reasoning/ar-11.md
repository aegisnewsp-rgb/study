# Cause and Effect

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Cause and Effect** questions in HAT-UG Analytical Reasoning present a scenario followed by two statements (I and II); your task is to assign the direction of the logical link between them. The cause is the event that **produces or contributes to** another event; the effect is the outcome produced by that cause. Every question demands a single verdict from a fixed menu of options.

- **Statement I is the cause, Statement II is its effect**
- **Statement I is the effect, Statement II is its cause**
- **Both are independent causes** of the same (unstated) effect
- **Both are independent effects** of the same (unstated) cause
- **Neither** statement is cause or effect of the other

Pick the **strongest immediate cause** — never a remote precondition, an enabling factor, or a symptom pretending to be the cause. Expect **1–3 questions** in the HAT-UG Analytical Reasoning section; each is a quick elimination job once the direction is fixed.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Anatomy of a Cause-Effect Question

A typical HAT-UG item shows a short scenario (e.g., "The city reported a sharp rise in waterborne diseases last month"), then Statement I and Statement II offer two plausible explanations. The hidden job is to decide whether I triggers II, II triggers I, both stem from a shared root, or they are unrelated.

#### Classifying the Link

| Verdict | What it means | Trigger cue in the options |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I → II | I is the cause, II is its effect | "I is the cause and II is the effect" |
| II → I | II is the cause, I is its effect | "I is the effect and II is the cause" |
| Both causes | Two independent causes of an unstated effect | "Both are independent causes" |
| Both effects | Two visible results of an unstated cause | "Both are independent effects" |
| Neither | No causal link between them | "Neither I nor II is the cause/effect" |

#### The Four-Pass Method

1. **Read the scenario first** and identify the central event you must explain.
2. **Translate each statement** into event form and ask: does this *produce* the other, or *result from* it?
3. **Check for a common cause** — do both statements sound like parallel symptoms of a missing root event?
4. **Eliminate the weakest options** (remote cause, symptom, mere correlation) before selecting the strongest immediate cause.

#### High-Yield Traps

- A **precondition** (e.g., "the monsoon arrived early") is not the **immediate cause** of an outbreak — pick the direct trigger if offered.
- **Correlation ≠ causation**: two events that co-occur may share a hidden common cause, making them independent effects rather than cause-and-effect of each other.
- **Symptoms are effects, not causes**: fever, for example, is an effect of infection, not the cause.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Remote vs Immediate vs Contributory Cause

Examiners deliberately stack options at different depths of the causal chain. The **immediate cause** is the last event that directly produces the effect; a **remote cause** is a background condition that set the stage; a **contributory cause** is necessary but not alone sufficient. When two options both seem plausible, ask: *which one directly produces the effect, and which one merely enabled it?* If both are contributory, the stronger immediate cause wins.

#### The Independence Pattern

The "independent causes / independent effects" verdicts test whether you recognise **parallel structure** rather than a chain. For example, "I. Sales of umbrellas rose. II. Sales of raincoats rose." — both are parallel effects of an unstated cause (heavy rainfall), not cause-and-effect of each other. Conversely, "I. The company cut prices. II. A rival launched a discount campaign." are independent causes of the same unstated effect (market share drop).

#### Worked Micro-Example

**Scenario:** City X reported a rise in respiratory ailments.
**I.** Construction activity in the area increased sharply.
**II.** Air quality monitors recorded a rise in PM2.5 levels.

Step 1 — Central event: rise in respiratory ailments.
Step 2 — I produces more dust; II records the resulting pollution. II *follows from* I, so I is the cause and II is the effect. Eliminate common-cause and independent-effects options because the chain is clean.

#### Common Mistakes to Avoid

| Mistake | Why it fails |
| --- | --- |
| Picking a symptom as the cause | Symptoms are downstream effects, not upstream triggers |
| Confusing temporal order | The cause must precede the effect — reverse and the verdict flips |
| Defaulting to "neither" | When a contributory link exists, "neither" is rarely correct |
| Ignoring shared root | Misses the common-cause / common-effect pattern entirely |

#### Practice Prompts

1. *Scenario:* Power cuts doubled in District Y. **I.** A new industrial unit was commissioned. **II.** Transformer failures were reported across three feeders. Verdict?
2. *Scenario:* Passengers complained of delayed trains. **I.** A new timetable was issued. **II.** Track maintenance work was scheduled. Verdict?

#### Strategy for HAT-UG

Target **60–90 seconds** per question. Eliminate the obviously wrong verdicts first, then test the surviving candidates against the "immediate cause" rule. In recent HAT-UG papers, the Analytical Reasoning block carries roughly **4%** of the overall weight, and Cause-Effect contributes 1–3 questions to that block.
