# Ecology, Ecosystems and Biodiversity — Leaving Certificate (Established) Biology Notes

Unit 3.1 opens Strand 3 and it is the most *quantitative* unit in the specification, because it asks you to **model species diversity using Simpson's Diversity Index**. A formula with a worked calculation in it is unusual on this paper and it is reliably examinable, so learn the formula and be able to compute it without panic. The rest of the unit is the reasoning that goes around it: energy and matter flow through trophic levels, pyramids show what you can and cannot count, carrying capacity depends on limiting factors, and biodiversity loss has environmental, economic, social and cultural consequences that you should be able to argue rather than assert.

The Irish emphasis is deliberate — the specification repeatedly says *Irish ecosystems* when discussing carrying capacity and population curves, and it asks you to explore local ecosystems through current news and local media. That is a strong hint that exam context will be Irish: hedgerows, bogs, blanket bog, upland grassland, freshwater lake systems, the marine intertidal zone, and pressures such as drainage, overgrazing, invasive species and fish stock depletion.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Biosphere** — the global sum of all ecosystems and the interactions between them. **Ecosystem** — a community of organisms interacting with each other and with their non-living environment, in a defined area. **Habitat** — the place where an organism lives. **Community** — all the populations of different species living and interacting in the same area. **Population** — all the individuals of one species in one area.
- **Producers** make their own food by photosynthesis and form the first **trophic level**. **Primary consumers** are herbivores; **secondary consumers** eat primary consumers; and so on.
- **Food chain vs food web.** A chain is a single pathway. A **food web** links all the chains together, and is the realistic representation because most organisms eat more than one thing and are eaten by more than one thing. **Arrows point from the food to the consumer** — energy flows that way.
- **Energy transfer.** Only about **10%** of the energy at one trophic level is transferred to the next; the rest is lost as heat through respiration, movement and excretion. Energy therefore **flows** one way and is lost at every level, so there is a limit to the size of the food chain.
- **Matter, by contrast, cycles.** Nutrients are recycled through the ecosystem and through the biosphere by decomposers (see unit 3.2). Energy enters, matter circulates. Confusing the two is one of the most penalised errors in this unit.
- **Simpson's Diversity Index:**

  **Dₛ = 1 − [ Σ n(n − 1) ] / [ N(N − 1) ]**

  where *n* is the total number of organisms of a particular species and *N* is the total number of organisms of **all** species. Dₛ runs from 0 to 1. A value near **0** means very low species diversity; a value near **1** means high diversity. The index measures **richness and evenness** together — two communities with the same number of species can score differently depending on whether the individuals are evenly spread between them.
- **Pyramid of numbers.** Each trophic level represented by the number of individuals. **Upright** in most ecosystems. Can be **inverted** where a single tree supports many herbivorous insects — e.g. one oak tree (1) → insects (thousands) → birds (a few) → predators (fewer). Can be **spindle-shaped** in a parasitic food chain, e.g. one tree host → thousands of parasitic insects → few birds.
- **Limitation of pyramids of numbers:** they count individuals, not biomass, so a large number of tiny organisms counts the same as a small number of large ones.
- **Pyramid of biomass.** Each level represented by the **mass** of living material. Generally **upright on land**. Can be **inverted in aquatic ecosystems**, e.g. phytoplankton (very low standing biomass, because it turns over extremely fast) → zooplankton → fish, where the fish biomass exceeds the phytoplankton biomass at any one instant. This is a limitation of the *method*, not of the ecosystem — the phytoplankton simply reproduce and are eaten faster than they accumulate.
- **Limitation of pyramids of biomass:** a snapshot in time, so it cannot show a rapidly renewing producer base; it also ignores the rate of turnover.
- **Carrying capacity** — the maximum population an environment can sustain indefinitely.
- **Abiotic (non-living) limiting factors**: water, oxygen, space, temperature, soil. **Biotic (living) limiting factors**: food, disease, competition, predation.
- **Population curves.** **S-shaped (S-population) curves** occur in resource-limited environments where carrying capacity stabilises the population — the birth rate falls to match the death rate as the population approaches K. **J-shaped curves** occur where resources are effectively unlimited, the population grows exponentially and then, in reality, crashes as resources run out.
- **Niche** — the full range of conditions and resources a species uses, and the role it plays in its community. A broad niche generalist and a narrow niche specialist. **Competition between species with identical niches is intense**, and competitive exclusion means one outcompetes the other; identical niches cannot coexist stably.
- **Symbiosis and other biotic interactions**: **mutualism** (both benefit), **commensalism** (one benefits, the other unaffected), **parasitism** (parasite benefits, host harmed), **predation** (predator benefits, prey harmed).
- **Ecological fieldwork**: **traps** (for small mammals, invertebrates), **pooter** (a suction device for collecting small invertebrates), **nets** (for aquatic sampling). **Qualitative surveys** identify which organisms are present; **quantitative surveys** also count or measure them. **Abiotic factors measured** include air and ground temperature, water conditions, oxygen, soil, aspect (which way a slope faces), wind speed and slope.
- **Human effects on species diversity** — evaluate primary or secondary data on this. Overfishing, habitat drainage and destruction, pollution, invasive species, and agricultural intensification all reduce diversity, usually first by removing the specialists.

#### Examiner traps

- **Reversing the arrows in a food web.** Arrows show the direction of **energy transfer**, from food to consumer.
- **Saying 10% of the *organisms* transfer.** It is 10% of the **energy**.
- **Claiming matter is lost from an ecosystem.** Matter is recycled; only energy is lost.
- **Interpreting an inverted pyramid of biomass as a broken ecosystem.** In aquatic systems a fast-turning producer base genuinely supports a larger consumer standing biomass.
- **Using a pyramid of numbers to argue about biomass.** The limitation of numbers is that it ignores size.
- **Using Σ without stating what is being summed.** Write "sum of n(n−1) for each species".
- **Describing a niche as "where an organism lives".** That is the **habitat**; the niche is what it does and what it uses.

#### 30-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Can you write Simpson's Diversity Index from memory and define n and N?
- [ ] Can you calculate Dₛ for a given table of species counts?
- [ ] Can you draw a pyramid of numbers for a tree–insect–bird chain and explain its inversion?
- [ ] Can you list five abiotic and four biotic limiting factors?
- [ ] Can you give one example each of mutualism, commensalism, parasitism and predation?

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### Worked Simpson's Diversity Index calculations

*Example 1 — high diversity.* A quadrat survey of a grassland gives: daisies 24, clover 22, plantain 18, grasses 20. N = 84.

- Σ n(n−1) = 24×23 + 22×21 + 18×17 + 20×19 = 552 + 462 + 306 + 380 = **1700**
- N(N−1) = 84 × 83 = **6972**
- Dₛ = 1 − (1700 / 6972) = 1 − 0.2438 = **0.756**

*Example 2 — same richness, different evenness.* Another quadrat gives: species A 60, species B 10, species C 4, species D 2. N = 76.

- Σ n(n−1) = 60×59 + 10×9 + 4×3 + 2×1 = 3540 + 90 + 12 + 2 = **3644**
- N(N−1) = 76 × 75 = **5700**
- Dₛ = 1 − (3644 / 5700) = 1 − 0.6393 = **0.361**

Both sites have four species, but the second is heavily dominated by one species. The second site is therefore much less diverse, and the index has captured the difference — which is exactly why the index includes the *n(n−1)* term rather than simply counting species. A mark scheme awards method marks for: defining n, defining N, summing correctly, dividing by N(N−1), subtracting from 1, and stating what the value means.

#### Reading population curves

*Worked question.* A population of an animal in an Irish upland grassland follows an S-shaped curve and plateaus at 4,000 individuals. The spring following a very wet winter, which raises the water table and reduces available grass, shows a fall to 2,800 before recovery. Explain.
*A.* The plateau at 4,000 represents the **carrying capacity** — the population is limited by a resource, here grass availability and possibly the oxygen available to the grass roots in waterlogged soil. The fall after the wet winter shows a **change in carrying capacity**, not a change in the population's nature: more waterlogging means less grass, fewer herbivores can be supported, and some animals emigrate or die, so the death rate temporarily exceeded the birth rate. As the soil drains and grass recovers, carrying capacity rises again and the population returns towards it. A strong answer notes that the carrying capacity is **not a fixed property of the habitat** — it varies with conditions, which is exactly what the specification asks you to consider for Irish ecosystems.

#### Pyramids — what each can and cannot show

| | Pyramid of numbers | Pyramid of biomass |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Measures | Number of individuals | Mass of living material |
| Strength | Quick to collect and count | Closer to the amount of energy available |
| Limitation 1 | Ignores organism size | A snapshot; ignores turnover rate |
| Limitation 2 | Misleading in inverted chains | Inverted in aquatic systems |
| Limitation 3 | Ignores energy flow | Cannot predict the number of organisms |

A comparison question asks you to state the strength *and* the limitation of each — that is what the specification's word "compare" is asking for.

#### Designing an ecosystem investigation

The specification asks you to use primary data gathered from a chosen ecosystem to model the habitat, investigate the effect of abiotic factors on distribution and abundance, and explain feeding and symbiotic relationships. A workable local study:

1. **Choose a defined area** and record its size, boundaries, aspect, slope and land use.
2. **Sample systematically** — quadrats along a transect at regular intervals, or a pitfall trap grid. Note that a **quadrat** is used for organisms on or in the ground, a **pooter** for small invertebrates, and a **net** for aquatic sampling.
3. **Measure abiotic factors** at each sampling point: light intensity, air and ground temperature, soil moisture, pH, wind speed, and aspect.
4. **Identify and count** the organisms present. Use a reliable identification key; record species rather than broad groups wherever possible, because an index computed from "grass" gives a meaningless result.
5. **Calculate** abundance per quadrat, species richness and **Dₛ**.
6. **Plot** the data — for example a scatter plot of abundance against soil moisture — and use it to test the hypothesis.
7. **Justify the conclusion** with the data, and state the sources of error and how you would reduce them.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Worked Higher-level questions with answers

**Q1.** A pyramid of biomass for a lake inverts at the first level, while a pyramid of numbers for the same lake is upright. Explain both observations.
*A.* The **pyramid of biomass** inverts because phytoplankton have a very low standing biomass at any one moment — they reproduce rapidly and are eaten rapidly — yet they support a larger standing biomass of zooplankton and fish. Energy is still lost at each transfer; the inversion appears only because the measure is a snapshot of standing crop, not a measure of turnover. The **pyramid of numbers** stays upright because there are always far more individual phytoplankton than individual fish, whatever their mass. Together the two examples show that neither pyramid is a complete picture: numbers ignore size, biomass ignores turnover rate. Neither shape tells you directly about energy flow, which is the actual quantity that is strictly reduced at every level.

**Q2.** Species A and species B have identical niches in the same grassland. Predict what happens and explain why.
*A.* This is **competitive exclusion**: two species competing for exactly the same limiting resources cannot coexist stably in one habitat. Whichever is the more efficient competitor — higher birth rate, faster growth, better use of the resource — will eventually out-compete and exclude the other, unless the two species are spatially separated, or a predator preferentially eats the superior competitor, or the resource supply increases enough to make competition irrelevant. This is why **conservationists deliberately manage habitats to be structurally varied**: a mosaic of habitats creates many different niches, so more species can coexist rather than excluding one another.

**Q3.** Evaluate the claim that introducing a large number of a keystone species is a useful conservation measure.
*A.* The evidence for it is that a keystone species has an effect on the ecosystem far larger than its abundance suggests, so reintroducing one can restore ecosystem processes — the reintroduction of sea otters to control sea urchins and allow kelp forests to recover is the standard case. The limitations are that a keystone is only keystone in one ecosystem, and often one place: the same species elsewhere may be ordinary or invasive, so a general claim about "the keystone species" is unsafe. Reintroduction also carries risk, including disease transmission, outbreeding, and genetic mixing with a resident population. A considered answer judges the measure on evidence for that specific ecosystem, not on the label.

**Q4.** Explain why biodiversity loss has economic, social and cultural consequences as well as environmental ones.
*A.* Environmental loss is the direct one: fewer species means a less resilient ecosystem, poorer nutrient cycling and lower productivity. The **economic** consequences follow because ecosystem services are used commercially — pollination for crops, fisheries, water purification, tourism and recreation, and the genetic resources of wild species for crop breeding. **Social** consequences include the loss of livelihoods that depend directly on those resources, and reduced access to natural environments for health and wellbeing. **Cultural** consequences concern the relationship between a community and its landscape: for rural Irish communities, a blanket bog or an upland common that is no longer functioning as a cultural landscape is a loss of identity and of the practices and knowledge attached to it, not merely of a species count. The specification lists all four impacts, so a full answer must reach past the ecological ones.

#### How this unit pays elsewhere

- **Unit 3.2** supplies decomposers, nitrogen fixation and the carbon cycle — the matter-recycling side of what this unit sets up.
- **Unit 1.5** explains how biodiversity arises and is maintained by selection.
- **Unit 2.6** supplies the physiology of the photosynthesis, respiration and transpiration that drive energy and matter flow.
- **Unit 2.4** supplies the disease, predation and competition pressures on Irish populations.
- **Biology in Practice Investigation** (40% of the grade) — a local ecosystem study with quadrats, a transect and a computed Dₛ is one of the strongest possible projects for the Biology in Practice Investigation, because it produces quantitative primary data, a testable hypothesis about an abiotic factor, and a conclusion that can be justified against the data.

#### Mistakes students actually make

1. **Arrows on the food web pointing the wrong way.** They point from the food to the eater, because that is the direction energy moves.
2. **Saying nutrients are lost between trophic levels.** Energy is lost as heat; nutrients are returned by decomposers.
3. **Using a pyramid of numbers as evidence of energy transfer.** It is not a measure of energy.
4. **Forgetting Σ in the diversity formula**, and computing Σn instead of Σn(n−1).
5. **Misreporting the range.** Dₛ approaches 0 for very low diversity and 1 for very high diversity; a value of 0.76 is high, not low.
6. **Describing niche as habitat.** Niche is role and resource use; habitat is location.
7. **Sampling non-systematically.** Randomly placed quadrats on a slope give biased data; a systematic transect is what the specification's investigation implies.
8. **Naming abiotic factors without measuring them.** "Light intensity" is a name; "light intensity in lux measured at ground level in each quadrat" is data.
