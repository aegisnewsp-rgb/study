# Semiconductors

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your NEET Physics exam.

A **semiconductor** is a Class IV solid (Si, Ge) or III–V compound (GaAs) whose electrical conductivity (≈10⁻⁵ to 10² S/m) lies between conductors and insulators because its **forbidden energy gap E_g** is small (Si ≈ 1.1 eV, Ge ≈ 0.67 eV). Thermal agitation lifts electrons from the **valence band** across E_g into the **conduction band**, leaving behind mobile **holes**. Doping with pentavalent impurities (P, As, Sb) creates **n-type** material (electrons majority), while trivalent doping (B, Al, Ga, In) creates **p-type** material (holes majority). A **p-n junction diode** conducts only under forward polarity, giving rectifier action.

- **Intrinsic carriers:** n = p = nᵢ, where nᵢ² = N_C N_V exp(−E_g/k_B T), E_g in J or eV, k_B = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K, T in K.
- **Mass-action law:** n·p = nᵢ² (units m⁻⁶).
- **Conductivity:** σ = N e μ, with N = carrier density (m⁻³), e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C, μ = mobility (m²/V·s); σ in S/m.
- **Shockley diode equation:** I = I₀[exp(V/ηV_T) − 1], where V_T = k_B T/e ≈ 25.85 mV at 300 K, η ≈ 1–2.
- **Transistor gains:** α = I_C/I_E (<1), β = I_C/I_B (>1), β = α/(1−α).

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"NPN = Negative-Positive-Negative, electrons flow N→P→N, so electrons are majority in the two N-regions"** — and recall **"P + N = 14"** (group numbers for trivalent and pentavalent dopants in column V and III). For α vs β: **"α is Alpha, smaller than 1; β is Beta, bigger than 1."**

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students preparing over a few days to months.

#### Energy Bands and Intrinsic Semiconductors

In the band picture, the **valence band** is fully occupied at 0 K and separated from the empty **conduction band** by E_g. Si has E_g ≈ 1.1 eV and Ge ≈ 0.67 eV at 300 K, small enough that thermal phonons promote electrons VB → CB. Each promoted electron leaves a hole that drifts as a positive charge. Intrinsic carrier density follows:

nᵢ² = N_C N_V exp(−E_g / k_B T)

where N_C, N_V are effective densities of states (m⁻³) in conduction and valence bands. Raising T sharply increases nᵢ, so **intrinsic conductivity grows exponentially with temperature** — opposite to metals.

#### Doping: n-type and p-type

Adding pentavalent P (group V) to Si produces an extra electron bound weakly (donor level ≈ 0.01–0.05 eV below CB). At 300 K it is ionised, freeing an electron — **majority carriers are electrons, minority are holes**. Similarly trivalent B (group III) creates an acceptor level just above VB, freeing a hole — **holes majority**. Mass action n·p = nᵢ² remains valid.

| Property | n-type (Si + P) | p-type (Si + B) |
|---|---|---|
| Dopant group | V (pentavalent) | III (trivalent) |
| Majority carriers | Electrons (≈ N_D) | Holes (≈ N_A) |
| Minority carriers | Holes (= nᵢ²/N_D) | Electrons (= nᵢ²/N_A) |
| Ionised impurity | Positive donor ion | Negative acceptor ion |
| Fermi level | Just below CB | Just above VB |

#### The p-n Junction

When p- and n-regions meet, electrons diffuse into p-side and holes into n-side, leaving behind a **depletion region** of ionised dopants. A **barrier electric field** opposes further diffusion, generating a **built-in potential** V_bi ≈ 0.7 V for Si and ≈ 0.3 V for Ge at 300 K.

**Forward bias** (p-side +, n-side −) lowers the barrier; current rises exponentially per Shockley equation. **Reverse bias** widens the depletion layer; only a tiny reverse saturation current I₀ flows (≈ µA in Ge, nA in Si), which doubles roughly every 10 °C. At high reverse voltage, **Zener (tunneling) or avalanche breakdown** occurs — exploited in Zener regulators.

#### Diode Applications

| Application | Mode of operation | Key fact |
|---|---|---|
| Half-wave rectifier | Forward half-cycle conducts | Rectification efficiency ≈ 40.6 % |
| Full-wave centre-tap | Both halves conduct | Efficiency ≈ 81.2 % |
| Bridge rectifier | Four diodes, no transformer tap | Efficiency ≈ 81.2 % |
| Zener diode | Reverse breakdown | Used as voltage regulator |
| Photodiode | Reverse-biased | Photocurrent ∝ light intensity |
| LED | Forward-biased | hν ≈ E_g of semiconductor |
| Solar cell | Zero bias, photovoltaic | Converts light → electricity |

The ripple factor r = I_ac/I_dc of a half-wave rectifier is 1.21; for full-wave it is 0.48 — these constants appear in NEET numericals.

#### Transistor (BJT)

An n-p-n transistor has a **heavily doped emitter**, a **thin, lightly doped base**, and a **moderately doped collector**. The **base-emitter junction is forward-biased**; the **base-collector junction is reverse-biased**. Emitter current splits: I_E = I_B + I_C. The two dimensionless gains obey β = α/(1−α) and α = β/(1+β). The **common-emitter** configuration is most often tested, with input applied between base and emitter and output taken between collector and emitter.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** A silicon p-n junction diode has reverse saturation current I₀ = 1 µA at 300 K. Find the forward current when the applied voltage is 0.6 V. Take V_T = 25.85 mV, η = 1.

#### Solution:
Apply I = I₀[exp(V/ηV_T) − 1].

V/ηV_T = 0.6 / (1 × 0.02585) ≈ 23.21.

exp(23.21) ≈ 1.19 × 10¹⁰.

I = 1 × 10⁻⁶ × (1.19 × 10¹⁰ − 1) ≈ 1.19 × 10⁴ A ≈ 11.9 kA.

This unphysically large answer shows the diode enters strong conduction well below the built-in potential. A more realistic model (series resistance) limits the current.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students often plug V = 0.7 V directly and get "no conduction below 0.7 V" — wrong. The diode conducts progressively; at 0.6 V on Si the current is already large. Always use Shockley for forward currents, not the "cut-in voltage" rule of thumb.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students building long-term command.

#### Temperature Dependence and Thermistors

Intrinsic conductivity σᵢ = nᵢ e (μ_e + μ_h) varies as exp(−E_g/2k_B T). Doubling T near room temperature roughly doubles nᵢ because the exponential dominates mobility loss. This negative temperature coefficient of resistance is the operating principle of **NTC thermistors** used in NEET-level instrumentation questions. In extrinsic material at low T, ionisation of dopants is incomplete; above a certain T (the **exhaustion temperature**), all donors/acceptors are ionised and the slope of log σ vs 1/T changes.

#### Fermi Level in Doped Material

The Fermi energy E_F shifts toward the conduction band in n-type material and toward the valence band in p-type. At equilibrium in a p-n junction, E_F is constant across the device, but band edges bend across the depletion region by an amount equal to qV_bi — visible as the **band diagram** routinely tested by NTA. Under forward bias, bands on the n-side drop by qV; under reverse bias they rise.

#### Transistor Limits and Early Effect

In active mode, I_C = β I_B holds only when the collector-base reverse voltage is moderate. The **Early effect** (base-width modulation) causes β to rise slightly with V_CE, modelled by I_C = I_C₀(1 + V_CE/V_A), where V_A is the Early voltage. For NEET numericals V_A is large enough to ignore, but conceptually the collector current is not perfectly independent of V_CE.

#### Photodiode vs Solar Cell

A **photodiode** is reverse-biased: incident photons generate e-h pairs swept by the field, producing a photocurrent that is linear in light intensity. A **solar cell** is operated at zero external bias; the photo-generated current forward-biases the junction, delivering power to a load. Both rely on photon energy hν ≥ E_g to liberate carriers — sub-band-gap photons pass through unabsorbed.

#### Common Advanced Traps

1. Confusing **diffusion length** L = √(D τ) with diffusion coefficient D (m²/s); L has units of metres.
2. Assuming the depletion width depends on doping symmetrically: it actually scales as √(ε V_bi / q · N_A N_D / (N_A + N_D)) — heavily doped side is narrower.
3. Treating β as a constant of the transistor regardless of operating point — β varies with I_C and T.
4. Writing the LED colour formula hν = eV (applied voltage) instead of hν ≈ E_g (band gap).
5. Believing a forward-biased p-n junction has zero depletion width — it shrinks but never fully vanishes.

#### Two Advanced Practice Prompts

1. A Ge diode (E_g = 0.67 eV) and a Si diode (E_g = 1.1 eV) are connected in series with 5 mA forward current. Using Shockley with matched I₀ and η = 1 at 300 K, estimate which diode drops more voltage and by how much.
2. A common-emitter transistor has α = 0.98. If base current changes by 20 µA, compute ΔI_C, ΔI_E, and the new β. Verify β = α/(1−α) numerically.
