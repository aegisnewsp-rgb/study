# Topic 2: Geography of Regional Concept

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **region** is an area of the earth differentiated by one or more unifying physical or cultural traits, used by geographers as a device for generalisation. Regions are classified into three principal types: **formal (uniform)**, **functional (nodal)**, and **perceptual (vernacular)**. The formal-functional distinction is the single most tested idea in TNPSC Group 1 prelims on this topic.

- **Formal region**: defined by *spatial homogeneity* — uniform climate, vegetation, language, or religion across the area.
- **Functional region**: defined by *spatial interaction* around a focal node (city, port) and its hinterland.
- **Whittlesey's agricultural regions**: 13 world regions classified by crop combination, livestock, intensity, and commercialisation.
- Hierarchy runs **macro → meso → micro** (e.g., Tropical Climate → Monsoon Asia → Cauvery Delta).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Defining the Region

Geography treats the earth as a mosaic of differentiated areas sharing common attributes. The region is the geographer's tool of **generalisation and synthesis** — it groups places that are alike in a chosen criterion so that spatial patterns become legible. Without regionalisation, the world map is an undifferentiated surface; with it, the map becomes a working model for planning and analysis.

#### Three Types of Regions

The Whittlesey-Herbertson tradition distinguishes regions by *what unites them*, not by where their boundaries fall.

| Region type | Defining criterion | Classic example | Boundary nature |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Formal (uniform) | Spatial homogeneity of one or more phenomena | Monsoon Asia; Tamil-speaking belt | Sharp where the trait ends |
| Functional (nodal) | Flows and linkages to a focal point | Chennai metropolitan region | Gradual, strength-based |
| Perceptual (vernacular) | Shared mental image of residents | "The South"; "Roti belt" | Subjective, fuzzy |

A **single-factor region** uses one variable (rainfall isohyet, language boundary); a **multi-factor region** combines several (Whittlesey's agricultural regions merge crop, livestock, intensity, and commercialisation).

#### Whittlesey's Agricultural Classification

Derwent Whittlesey (1936) divided the world into 13 agricultural regions using five criteria: crop and livestock combination, intensity of land use, degree of commercialisation, crop rotation pattern, and the role of livestock. The list still frames textbook questions even though commercial farming patterns have shifted.

#### Common Examiner Traps

- Treating "region" as a synonym for administrative unit (district, state).
- Saying every region has fixed boundaries — only *formal* regions do.
- Confusing *perceptual* regions (lived identity) with *functional* regions (economic node).
- Ignoring that a functional region's hinterland *shrinks or expands* with the nodal centre's pull.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Single-Factor vs Multi-Factor Regions

Single-factor classifications (rainfall regions, language regions, soil regions) are easy to map but rarely capture reality. Multi-factor classifications — climatic (Köppen), agricultural (Whittlesey), economic (planning regions) — better represent the *organised whole* that Hartshorne and Herbertson argued geography must study. The drawback is overlapping boundaries: a Cauvery delta village can sit simultaneously in a rice-farming region, a Tamil-speaking region, and a Chennai-centric functional region.

#### Functional Regions Are Dynamic

A functional region is held together by **interaction**, not uniformity. Distance decay, transport cost, and the node's economic weight decide its hinterland. When a new highway opens or a port is upgraded, the functional region's edges redraw themselves. Tamil Nadu's Madurai hinterland, for example, contracts when Chennai's connectivity improves for southern districts.

#### Practice Prompts

1. Distinguish formal, functional, and perceptual regions, giving one Indian example for each and stating one limitation of each type.
2. List Whittlesey's five criteria for agricultural regionalisation and explain why India is placed in the "intensive subsistence, rice-dominated" region rather than the "plantation" region.
