# Simple Logic and Deduction

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Simple Logic and Deduction** is the NCEE Verbal Reasoning sub-skill that asks you to extract a *necessary* conclusion from stated premises. The paper carries 4% of total weight, in most keys 10–15 MCQs across syllogisms, logical series, coding–decoding, analogies, and statement–conclusion items. Every answer must be **certain**, never merely **possible**.

| Sub-skill | Core Question Type | Key Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Syllogism | Two premises → one conclusion | Only "All" / "No" can distribute a term; "Some" cannot |
| Logical Series | Find the next figure | Track both **shape change** AND **position shift** |
| Coding–Decoding | Map letters via a rule | Opposite-letter code: coded = A + (Z − plain) |
| Statement–Conclusion | Pick the must-follow idea | Reject "possible"; only "necessarily true" counts |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **P-D-C-S** — **P**remise first, **D**istribute only universals, **C**onclusion must be **C**ertain, use **S**yllogism + Series + Coding daily. Or remember: **"Some never distributes, All always does."**

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Syllogisms and Categorical Logic

A syllogism joins two premises (major + minor) sharing one **middle term** to produce a conclusion. The four standard forms use quantifiers **A** (All), **E** (No), **I** (Some are), **O** (Some are not). Only **A** and **E** *distribute* their subject, so an invalid universal in many papers slips in when a student tries to push a "Some" conclusion into an "All" one.

| Form | Statement | Venn Diagram | Distributes |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | All A are B | Circle A ⊂ Circle B | A (subject) |
| E | No A is B | Circles disjoint | A and B |
| I | Some A are B | Partial overlap | Nothing |
| O | Some A are not B | Partial inside, partial outside | B (predicate) |

The three classically valid moods in figure 1 are **AAA-1**, **EAE-1**, and **AII-1**. Anything else in figure 1 (e.g., AEE, OAO) is invalid.

#### Logical Series Completion

A logical series shows 4–5 figures whose changes follow a hidden rule. Always test three things in order:

1. **Shape rotation** — clockwise, anticlockwise, or 180° flip.
2. **Position shift** — how many squares the shape moves each step.
3. **Element count** — number of lines, dots, or shaded regions added/removed.

If two candidates explain the data equally well, the rule that **requires fewer assumptions** wins.

#### Coding and Decoding

Letter coding uses three common rules: **reverse alphabet** (A=26, B=25, … Z=1), **opposite letter** (A↔Z, B↔Y, M↔N), and **position arithmetic** (each letter shifted by +k or −k). For opposite-letter coding the rule is:

$$\text{Coded position} = 27 - \text{Plain position}$$

so the plain letter **M** (13) becomes coded letter **N** (14), and **N** (14) becomes **M** (13).

#### Statement–Conclusion and Statement–Assumption

A valid **conclusion** is a statement that *must* follow — if a single counter-example exists, the conclusion is rejected even if it sounds reasonable. A valid **assumption** is a statement that *must be true* for the main statement to hold; helpful-but-not-required ideas are wrong.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** *Premise 1: All cats are mammals. Premise 2: All mammals are animals. Which conclusion necessarily follows?*
(A) All cats are animals
(B) Some animals are cats
(C) All animals are cats
(D) No cats are animals

**Solution:** Apply the syllogism **AAA-1**. Middle term = "mammals" (distributed in both A-premises). Distribute "cats" from premise 1 and "animals" from premise 2, then link them: cats ⊂ mammals ⊂ animals → cats ⊂ animals.
- (A) "All cats are animals" → **follows necessarily**. ✅
- (B) "Some animals are cats" → also follows but is *weaker* than A; examiners accept (A) as the strongest valid conclusion.
- (C) "All animals are cats" → converse error, invalid.
- (D) "No cats are animals" → contradicts both premises.

#### Answer: (A).

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Picking (C) because it "sounds like the same idea." The converse of "All A are B" is **not** logically equivalent — cats being a subset of animals does not mean animals are a subset of cats.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Advanced Traps

| Trap | What Students Do | Why It Fails |
|---|---|---|
| Converse vs Contrapositive | Treat "All A are B" ⇒ "All B are A" as valid | Distribution of B in the premise is not guaranteed |
| "Possible" vs "Necessary" | Choose a conclusion that *can* be true | The stem demands must-follow; "possible" is not enough |
| Some-not (O) conversion | Infer "Some not-A are not-B" | O-propositions do not convert cleanly |
| Series position drift | Track shape only, ignore translation | Many NCEE items hide the rule in the *path*, not the form |
| Coding sign error | Apply +k when rule says −k | Always test the rule against **two** known pairs before decoding the rest |
| Assumption over-reach | Pick a "helpful" assumption | An assumption without which the statement collapses is required; one that merely strengthens it is not |

#### Adjacent Topics to Connect

- **Analogy reasoning** overlaps with deduction when the shared relationship is **function** or **cause–effect** — practice converting an analogy into a one-line syllogism to confirm validity.
- **Number series** in Quantitative Aptitude uses the same shape-vs-position discipline as figure series; the underlying habit of testing multiple hypotheses transfers directly.
- **English comprehension** passages in many papers end with an inference question — apply the same "must be true, not merely likely" filter you use for Statement–Conclusion.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. Given *Premise 1: No reptiles have fur. Premise 2: All snakes are reptiles*, test whether "Some furred animals are snakes" and "No snakes have fur" are both valid conclusions, and explain using distribution.
2. A figure series shows a square moving one cell right, then one cell down, then one cell left, then one cell up. Predict the fifth figure and state the *minimum* rule that explains the cycle.
