# Blood Relations

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Blood Relations tests your ability to decode family clues into a tree and name the kinship between two people. The single most-tested skill is **gender identification first, diagram second** — pronoun or keyword ("wife", "son") tells you male/female before any relation is drawn.

- **Vertical family tree**: grandparents at top, parents in middle, children at bottom; spouses join with a horizontal `=`.
- **In-laws expand sideways**, not vertically: spouse's siblings attach at the same generation as your own siblings.
- **Self-reference trap**: "my father's only son's son" resolves to the speaker's own son, not a nephew.
- **Coded notation shortcut**: `A + B` = siblings, `A × B` = spouse, `A − B` = parent–child.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Decoding the Clue

Every Blood Relations sentence contains two payloads: a **gender signal** and a **relation signal**. Read both before sketching. A phrase like "A is the daughter-in-law of B" packs gender (female), marriage (to B's son), and generation (one below B) into nine words. Misreading any payload breaks the entire chain.

#### The Substitution Method

Pick a fixed anchor — usually the speaker ("I" / "my") or the person named first. Replace every subsequent individual with their relation to that anchor. For example, "My mother's husband is my father. My father's sister is my aunt. My aunt's son is my cousin." The chain becomes a single expression: **speaker → mother → husband → father → sister → son → cousin**. This compresses three generations into one mental step.

#### Standard Relation Map

| Code word in question | Real relation | Gender | Generation shift |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| son / brother / husband / father / uncle / nephew | Male relative | M | down or sideways |
| daughter / sister / wife / mother / aunt / niece | Female relative | F | down or sideways |
| father-in-law / mother-in-law / son-in-law / daughter-in-law / brother-in-law / sister-in-law | Married-side relative | depends on keyword | sideways through spouse |
| grandfather / grandmother / grandson / granddaughter | Two-generation gap | depends on keyword | up or down two levels |
| cousin | Same generation, shared grandparent | either | same level |

- Always draw the diagram **before** reading the options; the diagram is your scratchpad.
- When the stem says "only son" or "only daughter", treat that sibling as the unique link — it shortens the chain.
- Cross-cousins (father's sister's child, mother's brother's child) are still called cousins, but examiners sometimes use them to test whether you tracked the maternal/paternal side correctly.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases That Appear in SSC CGL

The Tier-I Reasoning section allocates roughly 1 question to Blood Relations within its 4% weight, and CGL has historically inserted 1–2 such sets in higher tiers. Three edge cases recur:

1. **Pointing/Introduction-based clues**: "Pointing to a man, Sita said, 'He is the son of my mother's only brother.'" Identify the speaker's anchor (Sita → mother → only brother), then attach the target one generation below. The trick is that the "man" stands outside the family tree — he is the answer's referent, not a node you must position.
2. **Symbolic equations**: `A + B` = siblings, `A × B` = spouses, `A − B` = one is the parent of the other, `A ÷ B` = one is the child of the other. These compress a four-line paragraph into one line, but they hide gender — you must infer it from `he/she` or `husband/wife` keywords.
3. **Reverse-reasoning traps**: "If A is the grandson of B, how is B related to A?" The forward phrasing leads students to answer "grandfather" without qualifying paternal/maternal. Standard answer key format accepts just "grandfather", but examiners occasionally demand "maternal grandfather" if the clue chain specifies the mother's side.

#### Worked Mini-Example

**Statement**: "Ravi is the son of Mohan. Mohan's wife is Sita. Sita's brother is Amit. Amit's son is Karan. How is Karan related to Ravi?"

- Ravi = male, child of Mohan and Sita → Generation 2.
- Amit = Sita's brother → same generation as Mohan (Generation 1).
- Karan = Amit's son → Generation 2, sibling-line of Ravi through their mothers.
- **Answer**: Karan is Ravi's **cousin** (maternal cousin, since the link runs through Sita).

#### Common Traps and How to Dodge Them

| Trap | Why students fall for it | Correct handling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confusing brother-in-law meanings | Three valid referents (husband's brother, wife's brother, sister's husband) | Trace through the spouse node in the diagram |
| Treating "uncle" as paternal only | Habit from childhood usage | Uncle = parent's brother OR parent's sister's husband |
| Drawing in-laws vertically | Loss of generation tracking | Always draw spouse's sibling at the same horizontal level |
| Stopping at intermediate relation | "How is X related to Y?" answered as "grandson" instead of "maternal grandson" | State the side (paternal/maternal) when the chain crosses parents |

#### Practice Prompts

1. "Pointing to a photograph, A says, 'She is the daughter of my grandfather's only son.' How is the person in the photograph related to A's father?" → Answer: **sister**.
2. If `P × Q` (spouse), `P + R` (siblings), and `R − S` (R is parent of S), what is S to Q? → Answer: **son or daughter** (S is Q's child through P's sibling R).
