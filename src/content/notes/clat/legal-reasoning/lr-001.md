# Legal Maxims

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your CLAT Legal Reasoning section.

Legal Maxims are short Latin phrases encapsulating a rule of law. CLAT tests three skills: recall the meaning, match the maxim to a fact pattern, and infer the legal consequence. Around **4–6 MCQs** appear per paper, usually passage-based.

| Maxim | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Mens Rea | Guilty mind |
| Actus Reus | Guilty act |
| Audi Alteram Partem | Hear the other side |
| Nemo Judex In Causa Sua | No one should be a judge in their own cause |
| Res Ipsa Loquitur | The thing speaks for itself |
| Caveat Emptor | Buyer beware |
| Volenti Non Fit Injuria | To a willing person, no injury is done |
| Ignorantia Juris Non Excusat | Ignorance of law is no excuse |

- Prioritise the 25–30 maxims that recur in past CLAT papers.
- Always read the fact pattern for **consent**, **knowledge**, or **bias** clues before picking.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### What Makes a Maxim Testable
A legal maxim carries **binding force only in its correct legal context**. CLAT rarely asks a direct translation; instead, the question embeds a 3–5 line scenario where one element (consent, fault, procedure) is highlighted. You must identify which Latin principle governs that element.

#### Three Categories of Maxims

| Category | Function | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Substantive law | Creates or defines a right/liability | Mens Rea, Caveat Emptor, Volenti Non Fit Injuria, Pacta Sunt Servanda |
| Evidence | Helps infer liability from facts | Res Ipsa Loquitur, Prima Facie |
| Procedure | Governs court conduct | Audi Alteram Partem, Nemo Judex In Causa Sua, Habeas Corpus |

#### Anchoring Maxims to Indian Statutes
- **Mens Rea** → Section 6, IPC (1860); excusing acts done without intent or knowledge.
- **Free consent in contracts** → Section 10, Indian Contract Act, 1872.
- **Caveat Emptor** → Sale of Goods Act, 1930, with exceptions in Section 14 implied conditions.
- **Audi Alteram Partem** → Article 14 read with natural justice under **Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)**.

#### Application Steps for Scenario Questions
1. Identify the legal issue: contract, crime, tort, or procedure.
2. Spot the operative element (consent, fault, opportunity to be heard).
3. Match that element to a maxim, not to its translation alone.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Distinction Traps
Examiners build wrong options by pairing semantically close maxims. Memorising only the Latin phrase guarantees the trap. **Audi Alteram Partem** concerns the *procedure* of being heard; **Nemo Judex In Causa Sua** concerns the *person* hearing — they are complementary, not interchangeable.

**Caveat Emptor** has been substantially diluted by the **Consumer Protection Act, 2019**, Section 14 implied conditions, and the rise of strict liability under **McDonald v. Union of India (M.C. Mehta, 1986)** for hazardous industries. Apply the maxim only when the buyer had a reasonable opportunity to inspect.

**Volenti Non Fit Injuria** requires *free and informed* consent to risk, not mere awareness. A spectator injured by a reckless cricket ball outside the stadium does not attract the defence because the risk was outside what was consented to.

#### Worked Micro-Example
A warehouse collapses, injuring a passerby. No witness saw the collapse. The defendant store owner argues no negligence is proved. The correct maxim is **Res Ipsa Loquitur** — the occurrence of such an event itself permits an inference of negligence, shifting the evidentiary burden.

#### Practice Prompts
1. A driver knowingly boards a friend's car knowing the brakes are faulty. The brakes fail and he is injured. Identify the defence and its limitation.
2. A municipal officer suspends a rival's trade licence without hearing him. Which procedural maxim is violated, and which constitutional article supports it?
