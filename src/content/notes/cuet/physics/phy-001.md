# Units & Measurement

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Units & Measurement** is the foundation layer of CUET UG Physics: every numerical you solve in Mechanics, Electrostatics, or Optics depends on the SI system, dimensional analysis, and error rules covered here. **Seven base quantities** define the SI: **metre (m, length)**, **kilogram (kg, mass)**, **second (s, time)**, **ampere (A, current)**, **kelvin (K, temperature)**, **mole (mol, amount of substance)**, and **candela (cd, luminous intensity)**. All other units are **derived** from these (e.g., newton = kg·m·s⁻², joule = kg·m²·s⁻²). The two workhorses to memorise are the **unit-conversion rule n₁u₁ = n₂u₂** and the **relative-error addition rule Δa/a = Δx/x + Δy/y + Δz/z** (for products/quotients). Always count **significant figures** by including the rightmost doubtful digit. High-yield traps: writing "kgs", adding absolute errors across multiplied quantities, and trusting dimensional consistency alone to prove a formula.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Physical, Fundamental, and Derived Quantities
A **physical quantity** is anything measurable, expressed as a number × unit (e.g., 5.2 m). CUET distinguishes **fundamental quantities** (independent, seven in SI) from **derived quantities** formed by multiplying/dividing them. Dimensional analysis writes any derived quantity as **[MᵃLᵇTᶜ]** (extending to A, K, mol, cd when relevant). Example: force = mass × acceleration gives [M L T⁻²]; energy = force × distance gives [M L² T⁻²].

#### SI Units and Significant Figures
The **SI system** is the global standard adopted by the General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM). Symbols are case-sensitive and never pluralised: 5 kg, not 5 kgs. **Significant figures** include all certain digits plus the first uncertain digit; for a metre-rule reading of 3.42 cm, three figures are significant and the "2" is doubtful. **Least count** = (value of one smallest division) / (number of divisions on vernier scale).

#### Unit Conversion
For two systems, **n₁u₁ = n₂u₂**, where n is numerical magnitude and u is unit size. Example: 1 mile = 1.609 km ⇒ converting 60 mph: 60 × 1.609 km/h = 96.54 km/h. Unit conversion is a guaranteed 1-mark question almost every CUET paper.

#### Errors in Measurement
- **Systematic error**: reproducible bias (zero error, calibration) — reduced by better instruments.
- **Random error**: scatter around mean, reduced by repeating and averaging.
- **Gross error**: blunder, removed by care.
- **Absolute error** Δa = |aₘₑₐₛ − aₜᵣᵤₑ|; **mean absolute error** is the average of absolute errors; **relative error** Δa/a = (Δx/x) + (Δy/y) + (Δz/z) for a product/quotient; for a power, Δ(aⁿ)/aⁿ = n(Δa/a). For sums/differences, **absolute errors add**.

#### Parallax and Precision
Read a scale with your eye **perpendicular** to avoid **parallax error**. **Precision** = reproducibility (low scatter); **accuracy** = closeness to true value. A precise instrument can still be inaccurate if it has a zero error.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases in Dimensional Analysis
The **principle of homogeneity** ([LHS] = [RHS]) verifies equation consistency but **cannot detect missing dimensionless constants**. A student who "derives" T = 2π√(L/g) from dimensions alone is wrong — the 2π must come from experiment or trigonometry. Similarly, equations like v = u + at vs. s = ut + ½at² are dimensionally consistent but require specific conditions. Use dimensions to (i) check formulae, (ii) find dependence on variables (e.g., time period of a pendulum on L and g ⇒ T ∝ √(L/g)), and (iii) convert units between CGS/SI/Imperial.

#### Worked Example — Error Propagation
Let density ρ = m/V. If m = (4.0 ± 0.1) g and V = (2.0 ± 0.2) cm³:
- Relative error in m: 0.1/4.0 = 0.025 (2.5%)
- Relative error in V: 0.2/2.0 = 0.10 (10%)
- Relative error in ρ: 0.025 + 0.10 = 0.125 (12.5%)
- ρ = 2.0 g/cm³; absolute error Δρ = 0.125 × 2.0 = 0.25 g/cm³, so ρ = (2.0 ± 0.25) g/cm³.
Note V dominates the uncertainty — to improve ρ, measure V more carefully.

#### Common Mistakes and CUET-Specific Strategy
1. Writing "kgs", "ms⁻¹s", or "Newton" (capitalised) loses marks in the symbol-matching section.
2. Confusing **precision** with **accuracy**: a voltmeter reading 10.1, 10.0, 10.1 V is precise but could be inaccurate if it has a 0.5 V zero error.
3. In **scientific notation**, "3.20 × 10⁴" has three significant figures; "32000" (without context) has two — CUET tests this with trailing-zero traps.
4. Mixing CGS and SI in one calculation (e.g., cm with kg·m·s⁻²) — convert first.
