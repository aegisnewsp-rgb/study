# Critical Reasoning

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Critical Reasoning (CR) in CAT VARC tests how you dissect a 100–200 word argument into its **premises** (supporting reasons) and **conclusion** (the claim the author wants you to accept), then answer passage-dependent MCQs. The section contributes roughly 6–8 questions per slot and carries 3 marks each with –1 negative marking, so accuracy — not guesswork — drives the percentile.

The eight recurring question types are Main Idea, Assumption, Strengthen, Weaken, Inference (Must Be True), Flaw, Paradox, and Method/Parallel Reasoning. Each type demands a different operation on the same argument skeleton.

- **Assumption**: apply the **Negation Test** — if negating the option breaks the argument, that option is the assumption.
- **Weaken vs Strengthen**: pick what makes the conclusion less (or more) likely; outside knowledge is irrelevant.
- **Must Be True**: only statements *guaranteed* by the passage; "can be true" choices are wrong.
- **Parallel Reasoning**: match the **logical pattern**, not the topic.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Anatomy of an Argument

Every CR passage hides a three-part skeleton: **conclusion** (the claim the author is defending), **premises** (evidence offered in support), and **assumption** (the unstated link that, if removed, would collapse the bridge between premises and conclusion). Signal words help — "therefore", "thus", "hence" flag conclusions; "because", "since", "for example" flag premises. Distinguishing the two is the most common first step; misidentifying a premise as the main point is a frequent trap on passages loaded with statistics or counterexamples.

#### The Eight Question Families

| Question Type | What It Asks | Core Test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main Idea / Primary Purpose | What the passage is about / what the author is doing | State the conclusion in one line |
| Assumption | What must be true for premises → conclusion | Negation Test |
| Strengthen | What makes the conclusion more likely | Does it add support? |
| Weaken | What makes the conclusion less likely | Does it undermine support? |
| Must Be True (Inference) | What is guaranteed by the passage | No new info, no contradiction |
| Flaw | Where the reasoning fails | Identify pattern (see below) |
| Paradox | Why an unexpected outcome occurred | Resolve the surprise |
| Method / Parallel Reasoning | How the argument is built / replicate its structure | Match the pattern, not the topic |

#### Applying the Tests

- **Negation Test** is exclusive to Assumption questions. On Weaken or Strengthen, just ask: does this option reduce (or raise) the probability of the conclusion?
- **Must Be True** demands a statement logically entailed by the passage. "Can be true" options introduce fresh information; "Cannot Be True" options directly contradict — both are wrong answers.
- **Flaw questions** reward pattern recognition: hasty generalisation, false cause (post hoc), slippery slope, ad hominem, straw man, false dilemma, confusing correlation with causation, and appeal to emotion.
- **Parallel Reasoning** ignores subject matter. If the original argues "X caused Y; therefore Z", the correct parallel also argues "A caused B; therefore C" — even on a different topic.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Scope Shifts and Quantifier Traps

The most common trap across CR question types is the **scope shift**. The passage says "some students", but an option concludes "all students" — this leap is almost always the answer to a Flaw question, or a wrong answer to Must Be True. Train yourself to circle every quantifier (some, most, all, none, few) before you read the options. A weaker quantifier in the option than in the passage is a classic Weaken; a stronger one in the option is in many papers the Assumed gap.

#### Must Be True vs Can Be True

The single highest-yield distinction in CAT CR: **Can Be True** options feel reasonable and are in many papers tempting, but they smuggle in unstated assumptions. Test each Must Be True candidate against three gates: (1) is it directly stated or logically forced? (2) does it introduce any new entity, comparison, or cause? (3) does any part contradict the passage? If the answer to (2) is yes, eliminate — no matter how plausible it sounds in the real world.

#### Flaw Pattern Catalogue

| Flaw | Pattern in Passage | Quick Cue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hasty Generalisation | One case → broad rule | "therefore, all X are Y" |
| False Cause (Post Hoc) | A then B → A caused B | "since X happened first" |
| Slippery Slope | One step → chain of disasters | "will inevitably lead to" |
| False Dilemma | Two options presented as only choices | "either X or Y" |
| Straw Man | Distorts opponent's view, then attacks it | "critics claim P (weak form)" |

#### Practice Prompts

1. A passage argues that a city's crime rate fell after a new policing policy. Which CR question type is most likely to follow, and which flaw pattern should you screen the options against first?
2. A paradox passage reports that a country with high vaccination rates also has high disease incidence. What two elements must a correct Paradox-resolution option contain?

#### Exam Strategy for CAT

Across 2021–2024 CAT slots, CR contributes 6–8 of the ~24 VARC questions and is the single most score-differentiating type at the 99+ percentile. Budget roughly 90 seconds per CR question, more for Paradox and Parallel Reasoning. Skip and return beats wild guessing — with –1 negative marking, an unjustified pick costs you a net 4 marks versus leaving it blank.
