# Odd One Out (Words)

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Odd One Out (Words) gives a cluster of four (sometimes five) terms and asks which one breaks the hidden rule binding the rest. The rule sits on a single dimension — **semantic category**, **function**, **synonymy**, **antonymy**, **part–whole**, or **word-formation pattern** (prefix, suffix, root). Three options share that property; one does not. Speed strategy: scan for the most obvious category first, then **verify** the candidate odd word against the other three to confirm exactly one rule isolates it. **Trap alert:** in NAT-I items, two words may *look* unrelated; only the rule that picks three and excludes one is correct. Expect 4–6 MCQs, ~30–45 seconds each.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Definition and Core Task
The item type belongs to the **verbal classification** family. The stem supplies a set of four or five words, and the candidate must name the single term that does not share the implicit common property. The correct odd word is not random — it differs on the *same* axis on which the other three agree.

#### Linking Rules to Test
- **Class membership** (e.g., Mango, Apple, Guava, Carrot — Carrot is the vegetable among fruits).
- **Function / role** (e.g., Knife, Saw, Needle, Spoon — Spoon is the only serving implement, not a cutting/piercing tool).
- **Part–whole** (e.g., Petal, Root, Stem, Wing — Wing belongs to a bird, the others to a plant).
- **Synonymy / antonymy** (e.g., Happy, Joyful, Cheerful, Sad — Sad is the antonym).
- **Word-formation pattern** (e.g., Return, Recall, Recover, Retake — Retake is the only one formed with a noun base; the rest use verbs).
- **Letter/number pattern** (used as a backup when semantic rules fail).

#### Solving Procedure
1. Read all words; list plausible shared features.
2. Pick the strongest, most economical rule.
3. Apply it: how many words does it include? It must include **exactly three** and exclude **exactly one**.
4. If two rules work, prefer the semantic one — examiners usually encode category over orthography.

#### NAT-I Format
Items are MCQs with four options; the stem itself contains the group. Weightage is light (3% of Verbal Reasoning), but accuracy here frees time for heavier passages.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Trap Designs
- **Dual-category decoys:** Three words may belong to category A, one to category B that *overlaps* with A on a different axis (e.g., Rose, Tulip, Marigold, Onion — Onion is a flower-shaped bulb but a vegetable; the semantic rule is *ornamental flowering plant*, isolating Onion). Always confirm the rule excludes *only* the intended word.
- **Word-formation decoys:** When all four words look semantically scattered, examine prefixes/suffixes. *Inhale, Exhale, Perspire, Digest* — three contain the bound morpheme relating to **breathing/respiratory exchange**; Digest does not.
- **Antonym traps:** A word may be the odd one because it is the *opposite* of the group’s shared property, not a true outsider.
- **Polysemy pivots:** One word carries a second meaning that lets it appear to fit; recognise which meaning the other three share.

#### Worked Example
Set: *Circle, Square, Triangle, Pentagon, Orange*. Obvious read: four are polygons, one is a fruit → **Orange**. Verify: does any other rule pick three and exclude one? "Four are geometric shapes" holds for exactly four, excluding Orange; "four are colours that contain the letter ‘a’" is weak and breaks on Pentagon. The polygon rule wins.

#### Connection to Adjacent Topics
Strong overlap with **Analogies** and **Word Analogies** (NTS) — both demand rule detection. Practice here transfers to **Sentence Completion** vocabulary depth.

#### Practice Prompts
1. *Pen, Pencil, Eraser, Sharpener, Notebook* — identify the odd one and state the rule in one sentence.
2. *Whisper, Murmur, Shout, Hum, Mumble* — three are low-volume vocalisations; which word breaks the rule and why?
