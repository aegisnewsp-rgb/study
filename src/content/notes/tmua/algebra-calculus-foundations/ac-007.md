# MM3 Coordinate Geometry

MM3 is two halves that share a habit of thinking. The first is lines: MM3.1 asks for $y - y_1 = m(x-x_1)$ and $ax + by + c = 0$, the conditions for two lines to be parallel or perpendicular, and equations of lines from information given in various forms. The second is circles: MM3.2 fixes the two standard equations, and MM3.3 lists seven circle properties as a numbered set.

The habit is this: in both halves you are expected to move between a geometric statement and an algebraic one. "The tangent is perpendicular to the radius at that point" is a fact about circles, and it becomes a gradient question the moment you have two points.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> The seven circle properties in MM3.3 are worth learning as a list, because a question may use any one of them.

| Property | Statement |
|---|---|
| a | The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord |
| b | The tangent at any point on a circle is perpendicular to the radius at that point |
| c | The angle subtended by an arc at the centre is **twice** the angle subtended at the circumference |
| d | The angle in a semicircle is a right angle |
| e | Angles in the same segment are equal |
| f | Opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral add to $180^\circ$ |
| g | The angle between the tangent and chord at the point of contact equals the angle in the alternate segment |

**Lines, in two forms.** $y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)$ for anything given a point and a gradient. $ax + by + c = 0$ for anything where "find the equation" is the instruction.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Finding the equation of a line

Through $(2,5)$ and $(-1,-1)$: the gradient is $m = \dfrac{5-(-1)}{2-(-1)} = \dfrac{6}{3} = 2$. Substitute one point into point-gradient form: $y - 5 = 2(x-2)$, i.e. $y = 2x + 1$. Rearranged into general form that is $2x - y + 1 = 0$. Check both points: $4-5+1 = 0$ ✓ and $-2+1+1 = 0$ ✓.

**Parallel and perpendicular.** Two lines are parallel when their gradients are equal, $m_1 = m_2$. Two non-vertical lines are perpendicular when $m_1 m_2 = -1$. So a line of gradient 2 has a perpendicular of gradient $-\tfrac12$. Two points about the sign: it is $-1$, not $+1$, and it does not exist for vertical and horizontal lines, which are perpendicular without being able to satisfy the product rule.

**From other information.** A line parallel to $y = 2x + 1$ through $(0, 4)$ has $m = 2$, so $y = 2x + 4$. A line perpendicular to it through $(3, 1)$ has $m = -\tfrac12$, so $y - 1 = -\tfrac12(x-3)$, giving $x + 2y = 5$. Anything given as a distance, an area, or a condition on intercepts needs you to convert into a point or a gradient before any of this applies.

#### Circles in both forms

The centre-radius form is $(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 = r^2$; the general form is $x^2+y^2+cx+dy+e = 0$. To convert a general-form circle to centre-radius, complete the square. Take $x^2 + y^2 + 6x - 4y - 3 = 0$:

$$(x+3)^2 - 9 + (y-2)^2 - 4 - 3 = 0 \implies (x+3)^2 + (y-2)^2 = 16$$

so the centre is $(-3, 2)$ and $r = 4$. Read it back off: the centre is $(-c/2, -d/2)$, and $r = \sqrt{\left(\tfrac{c}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{d}{2}\right)^2 - e}$. Here $c = 6$, $d = -4$, $e = -3$, giving centre $(-3, 2)$ and $r = \sqrt{9+4+3} = \sqrt{16} = 4$ ✓.

**Intercepts.** Setting $y = 0$ in $(x-3)^2 + (y+1)^2 = 16$ gives $(x-3)^2 = 15$, so $x = 3 \pm \sqrt{15}$. Setting $x = 0$ gives $9 + (y+1)^2 = 16$, so $(y+1)^2 = 7$ and $y = -1 \pm \sqrt7$. Two intercepts on each axis, which is what the geometry of a circle of radius 4 centred at $(3,-1)$ demands.

#### Turning a circle property into algebra

Property (b) — the tangent is perpendicular to the radius — is the most-used. For $x^2 + y^2 = 25$, the point $(3,4)$ is on the circle since $9+16=25$. The radius from $(0,0)$ to $(3,4)$ has gradient $\tfrac43$, so the tangent has gradient $-\tfrac34$ and passes through $(3,4)$:

$$y - 4 = -\tfrac34(x-3) \implies 3x + 4y = 25$$

Substituting $(3,4)$ gives $25$ ✓. Notice the result: for a circle centred at the origin, the tangent at $(a,b)$ is $ax + by = r^2$.

Property (a) — the perpendicular from the centre bisects a chord — works the same way. If a chord lies along $x + y = 4$, the foot of the perpendicular from the origin is $(2,2)$, and that foot is the midpoint of the chord. If the chord is cut from the circle $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$ with $r > 2\sqrt2$, its endpoints are $(2,2)$ displaced along the line, and their sum is $(4,4)$.

Property (d), the angle in a semicircle, is Thales' theorem, and it is the one most often needed without being named. The circle $x^2+y^2=1$ has diameter from $(-1,0)$ to $(1,0)$. Any point on it, say $(\tfrac35, \tfrac45)$, forms a right angle at that point: the vectors to the two ends are $(-\tfrac85, -\tfrac45)$ and $(\tfrac25, -\tfrac45)$, whose dot product is $-\tfrac{16}{25} + \tfrac{16}{25} = 0$ ✓.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (3mo+)

#### The angle facts are all one fact

Properties (c), (e), (f) and (g) are four faces of the same structure. The centre angle is twice the circumference angle; angles standing on the same chord from the same side are equal, which is why they differ by the factor of two when they stand on opposite sides; a cyclic quadrilateral's opposite angles supplement each other because the two angles together make the full centre angle $360^\circ$; and the alternate segment theorem says the tangent–chord angle equals the inscribed angle on the far arc. If you hold one of them, you can usually reconstruct the rest.

#### The circle properties are coordinate-free

Nothing in MM3.3 mentions coordinates. That is deliberate — the specification is testing whether you can carry a theorem into the plane and use it. The strategy that works: identify which property applies, convert each object it mentions (centre, tangent, chord, arc) into its algebra, and then you are doing MM3.1 arithmetic with an extra fact about the geometry. A question that says "the tangent to the circle at $P$ meets the $x$-axis at $Q$; find the length $QP$" is a gradient question wearing a circle costume.

#### Where the two halves stop helping

Two limits are worth naming. Gradients fail for vertical lines — a vertical line has no gradient and cannot be written $y = mx + c$, which is exactly why $ax+by+c = 0$ exists and why $b \ne 0$ is required. And the general form of a circle is genuinely more useful than the centre form: it is the only one of the two that survives substitution into an equation of a line, since substituting $y = -x$ or a parametric form into $(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2=r^2$ produces squares. When a question mixes a line and a circle, take the general form on both sides.

#### Common mistakes

Using $m_1 m_2 = +1$ for perpendicular lines instead of $-1$.

Losing a sign when moving $y_1$ and $x_1$ out of $y - y_1 = m(x-x_1)$.

Forgetting that the general form is $r^2$, not $r$, after completing the square — and forgetting the constants you moved to the right.

Swapping the $x$- and $y$-intercepts by setting the wrong coordinate to zero.

Claiming the angle at the centre equals the angle at the circumference. Property (c) says twice.

Taking the perpendicular from the centre to hit the chord's *midpoint* by assumption rather than by construction — the theorem goes centre $\to$ perpendicular $\to$ midpoint, and the perpendicular must be genuinely perpendicular.

Forgetting to exclude vertical lines from the $m_1m_2 = -1$ rule.
