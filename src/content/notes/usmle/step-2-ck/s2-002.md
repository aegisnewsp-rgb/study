# Medicine by Presentation

Step 2 CK evaluates clinical knowledge and the ability to apply medical concepts in patient care, and its stated organisation is around presentations rather than around diseases. That single sentence decides how you study this step. A patient walks in with a complaint; the question asks for the next step; the options are actions. Never a label. If you revise by memorising disease lists you will recognise nothing at the examination, because the stem never names a disease for you to recognise — it gives you a symptom and asks what to do about it.

The format makes the pressure explicit. Step 2 CK is a one-day examination of approximately 316 multiple-choice questions divided into sixteen 30-minute blocks, administered in one session of approximately 9 hours, with at most 20 questions per block, 55 minutes of break time and a 5-minute optional tutorial. The block structure is recent: before 7 May 2026 the step ran as eight 60-minute blocks of up to 40 questions, and the older figures still circulating in study material describe that. Every Step is scored as a recommended pass or fail rather than with a number, and examinees typically need to answer approximately 60 per cent of questions correctly. Twenty items inside a thirty-minute block is fast, and the reading speed you need for a paragraph-style vignette has to be trained, not assumed.

This topic covers the presentations that carry the most weight in practice and in items: chest pain, dyspnoea, jaundice, oedema, fever and weight loss. For each one, the exam expects you to hold a differential, know which member of it you must rule out first, and choose the investigation or treatment that separates the survivors. The topic list and weights are StudyRoadmap's own organisation rather than an NBME blueprint, so treat the relative size of these presentations as a study priority, not as a statement about how many items NBME spends on them.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

**Memory hook — T-A-C-K.** Every presentation question is the same four moves, in the same order: **T**empo (how fast it came on), **A**ssociated features (what travels with it), **C**ontext (the patient in front of you), **K**illers (the diagnosis you cannot afford to miss). Read the stem in that order and the differential writes itself, because tempo divides the possibilities before any other feature does.

| The stem hands you | Your first split | The discriminator that decides it |
|---|---|---|
| Chest pain | Cardiac vs non-cardiac vs other | Character, radiation, associated features; ECG and troponin early |
| Dyspnoea | Acute vs chronic | Time course, before anything else |
| Jaundice | Pre-hepatic, hepatic, post-hepatic | Urinalysis — conjugated bilirubin darkens the urine |
| Oedema | Local vs systemic | Distribution: one leg, or both legs and sacral |
| Fever | Infectious vs non-infectious | Host risk factors, and whether the patient is septic |
| Weight loss | Appetite preserved vs lost | Appetite preserved points to catabolic and malabsorptive causes |
| Abdominal pain | Perforative, obstructive, inflammatory | Whether the pain is peritonitic, colicky or diffuse |

**Ten facts worth a mark each.**
- Step 2 CK is a single day of approximately 316 questions in sixteen 30-minute blocks, at most 20 questions per block.
- Every Step is scored as a recommended pass or fail rather than with a number; examinees typically need approximately 60 per cent of questions correct.
- The question type decides the answer: initial management, further investigation, diagnosis, or definitive treatment.
- Time course splits most differentials before any other feature — acute and chronic dyspnoea share almost nothing.
- Pre-test probability decides how you read a result: a negative D-dimer rules out in a low-probability patient and says almost nothing in a high-probability one.
- Urinalysis separates the jaundice groups faster than any blood test, because only conjugated bilirubin appears in urine.
- Unilateral leg swelling is a local problem until shown otherwise; both legs and sacral oedema is systemic until shown otherwise.
- Unintentional weight loss with appetite preserved points towards malignancy, diabetes, hyperthyroidism or malabsorption; with appetite lost, towards inflammation, infection, depression or heart failure.
- Take blood cultures before antibiotics in a stable patient with suspected endocarditis — but never delay antibiotics in sepsis.
- In a time-critical presentation, treatment comes before the confirmatory test.

**Three ways to lose the item.** Anchoring on the first diagnosis that fits and discarding the alternative that explains the whole picture; ordering an investigation with no plan attached to the result; and treating a number rather than the patient in front of you.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### The shape of a presentation item

Every item in this family is the same skeleton with a different task attached. The vignette describes a patient, the question asks for one thing, and the options are candidate actions. The task is one of four: the most likely diagnosis, the next best investigation, the most appropriate initial management, or the most appropriate definitive treatment. Because the four tasks have different correct answers, read the question stem before you read the vignette — otherwise you will start reasoning towards a diagnosis and miss that you were actually asked what to do.

The other thing every vignette carries is **context**: age, comorbidity, pregnancy, medications, recent surgery, immobilisation, travel, occupation, and the setting of care. Context is not decoration, it is the prior probability. A chest pain in a young person with reproducible tenderness and no risk factors is a different problem from the same sentence in an older patient with diabetes and hypertension, and the investigation you pick changes with it. Read for context first, then for tempo, then for associated features.

What kills candidates is treating the vignette as a quiz. The exam is not asking which disease this is; it is asking what you do next, and the "next" is defined by what would change your management. If you cannot name the question an investigation answers, it is not the right answer.

#### Chest pain

Chest pain has two readings: the commonest causes in ambulatory patients are musculoskeletal and oesophageal, but the commonest causes in a written item are the four you cannot miss — acute coronary syndrome, aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism and tension pneumothorax — with oesophageal rupture and cardiac tamponade close behind. Your job in the first seconds is to exclude those, not to name the most likely cause.

The features that move you are character, radiation, timing and associated features, and they are worth learning as a set because they travel together. Pressure or squeezing pain, substernal, radiating to the arm or jaw, with sweating and nausea, relieved by rest and brought on by exertion, is ischaemic until proven otherwise. Sharp pleuritic pain that worsens on inspiration, with breathlessness, tachycardia and a swollen calf, is venous thromboembolism until proven otherwise. Sharp pain that reproduces on palpation and shifts with posture in a young patient is chest wall. Burning pain that rises on lying flat and comes with regurgitation is oesophageal. A tearing pain radiating to the back, with a blood pressure difference between arms, is dissection. Pleuritic pain with a non-blanching rash, or fever and productive cough, changes the frame again.

The management answer follows the tempo. A patient with ischaemic-sounding pain needs an ECG and a troponin, and if the ECG shows ST elevation the answer is immediate reperfusion, not a wait for tests. A patient with a pleuritic picture and a swollen calf needs pre-test probability assessment for pulmonary embolism, and if the probability is low a negative D-dimer ends the episode, while if the probability is high the correct next step is imaging rather than a D-dimer. Fever and cough with focal chest signs sends you to a chest radiograph and antibiotics. The trap in every chest pain item is anchoring: treating the patient whose chest pain is cardiac because the history "sounds benign", or treating the one whose chest pain is musculoskeletal because the patient is young.

#### Dyspnoea

Split acute from chronic before anything else, because the two lists share almost nothing. Acute dyspnoea is pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, acute pulmonary oedema, an asthma or COPD exacerbation, pneumonia, anaphylaxis, upper airway obstruction, aspiration, or the hyperventilation of panic. Chronic dyspnoea is heart failure, COPD, interstitial lung disease, bronchiectasis, a chronic pleural effusion, pulmonary hypertension, or the non-pulmonary causes — anaemia, thyrotoxicosis, acidosis, obesity, ascites, and neuromuscular weakness.

The discriminators are pattern and associated features rather than the measurement. Orthopnoea and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea point to the heart. Wheeze with reversibility points to the airway; a dry, fine crackle at the bases points to the interstitium. Unilateral reduced breath sounds point to effusion, pneumothorax or collapse, and bilateral findings point to a systemic cause. A young person with a normal chest examination and no fever whose breathlessness is episodic and comes with palpitations and tingling is hyperventilation, not asthma, and the difference matters because the investigations and the treatment are completely different. One general rule: the item is rarely about the oxygen saturation. The number is context, not the answer.

#### Jaundice

Three groups: pre-hepatic, hepatic, post-hepatic. The fastest split is the urinalysis, because only conjugated bilirubin is water-soluble and appears in urine. Bilirubin in the urine therefore means the problem is on the hepatobiliary side; unconjugated bilirubin, from haemolysis, does not. Urobilinogen completes the picture — it rises when bilirubin is being destroyed and reaches the gut, and it falls when bilirubin is blocked before the gut. Dark urine with pale stools, with bilirubin present and no urobilinogen, is obstruction until proven otherwise.

Then the modifiers decide urgency. Fever with right upper quadrant pain and jaundice is ascending cholangitis and needs urgent biliary drainage. Pain without fever suggests a stone; no pain and no fever, with weight loss in an older patient, suggests malignant obstruction — the painless obstructive jaundice that you must not send home with an antipruritic. In the hepatic group, look for the stigmata of chronic liver disease — spider naevi, palmar erythema, ascites, a caput medusae — and for the systemic causes of hepatitis: drugs, alcohol, viral exposure, travel, pregnancy with vomiting or with raised blood pressure and raised liver enzymes, and the patient who is unwell and febrile with jaundice.

Two traps catch people. First, dark urine is read as haematuria or as dehydration when it is conjugated bilirubin, and a dipstick settles it. Second, jaundice is presented as the diagnosis when it is only the finding — and the item asking for the next step usually wants the imaging or the urinalysis, not the label.

#### Oedema

Distribution is the first split, because it separates a local problem from a systemic one. One leg, or one arm, is local: deep vein thrombosis, cellulitis, lymphatic obstruction, a ruptured Baker's cyst, or venous obstruction from a mass. Both legs and the sacral area is systemic: heart failure, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis, severe hypoalbuminaemia, or a drug effect. Periorbital oedema that is worse in the morning and generalised oedema with frothy urine is renal. Sacral oedema with ascites and a caput medusae is hepatic. Ankles worse in the evening and improving overnight with a history of varicose veins is venous insufficiency. Pitting is the norm; non-pitting oedema suggests lymphoedema, lipoedema or thyroid disease.

Then the systemic workup. Bilateral oedema with raised jugular venous pressure, basal crackles and orthopnoea is heart failure. Bilateral oedema with heavy proteinuria is nephrotic. Ascites dominating with relatively modest leg oedema and stigmata of chronic liver disease is cirrhosis. And always review the drugs: calcium channel blockers cause peripheral oedema, corticosteroids and NSAIDs cause sodium retention, and diuretics can cause oedema through poor compliance or through over-diuresis. The trap is treating bilateral leg oedema as heart failure by reflex — most ambulatory patients with it do not have heart failure, and the item will punish you for the reflex while rewarding the examination findings and the urine.

#### Fever

Fever is a symptom with a wide differential, and Step 2 CK expects you to use it as a pointer rather than a diagnosis. First, describe the pattern — continuous, remittent, relapsing, undulant, or the pattern of the neutropenic patient who has a serious infection without a temperature rise. Then, ask about the host: age, immunocompromise, pregnancy, indwelling lines and catheters, prosthetic valves and joints, recent surgery or hospital stay, injection drug use, animal and vector exposure, travel, occupation, and the timing relative to any dental or surgical procedure.

Then find the source: skin and soft tissue, lung, urine, gastrointestinal tract, bone and joint, heart, central nervous system, and the deep abdominal and pelvic organs. Two presentations carry urgency — fever with a new murmur or an embolic skin lesion suggests infective endocarditis, and fever with neck stiffness and photophobia suggests meningitis, and in both you need to act before you have the result. Fever with a non-blanching rash is meningococcal disease until excluded. And never stop at infection: drug fever, thyroiditis, autoimmune disease, deep vein thrombosis, malignancy, and factitious fever all present this way, and the item that gives you a normal white count, a negative culture set and a rash is pointing at one of them.

#### Weight loss

The first question is whether it is intentional, because weight loss is often deliberate and a patient who has been dieting will not have the differential you are about to build. Then ask about the rate of onset and about appetite. Weight loss with appetite preserved points to malignancy, diabetes, hyperthyroidism, malabsorption and chronic infection such as tuberculosis or HIV. Weight loss with appetite lost points to depression, inflammatory disease such as inflammatory bowel disease or rheumatoid arthritis, heart failure, advanced malignancy, and the effect of drugs. The third question is what kind of tissue has gone: muscle, fat or fluid. Fluid loss masquerading as weight loss is the commonest trap, because diuretics, heart failure and nephrotic syndrome produce a falling scale with a patient who is not catabolic at all.

Look for the clues that direct the investigation: pallor for anaemia, thyrotoxic features, altered bowel habit or diarrhoea for a gastrointestinal cause or coeliac disease, lymphadenopathy, a smoker with weight loss pointing at the lung, alcohol use pointing at the pancreas, and in an older adult unintentional weight loss as a presentation of malignancy in its own right. Depression and social causes are real and must be acknowledged, but not before organic disease has been excluded. The item usually wants the next investigation rather than the diagnosis, and the investigation list follows the pattern: blood count, electrolytes and liver enzymes, inflammatory markers, thyroid function, glucose, urine testing, coeliac serology where diarrhoea is present, a chest radiograph, and the age-appropriate cancer screening and referral pathways where red flags exist.

#### The next-step discipline

Three habits turn a differential into an answer. First, before ordering any investigation, state the question it answers, the pre-test probability you assign, and what you will do differently if it is positive and if it is negative. A test you would not act on is not the answer. Second, in an unstable patient the order is stabilisation first — airway, breathing, circulation, oxygen, access, fluids, ECG, glucose — and the diagnosis comes after. Third, know when not to investigate: with a low pre-test probability, a well-appearing patient and a benign explanation, the correct action is often to reassure, treat the cause and review, and any item offering "no further investigation" is there because a well-written item wants you to recognise that.

And do not let a result close a differential that the story has not closed. A normal early investigation does not exclude a time-critical diagnosis, and one positive result does not exclude the alternatives. The patients who die on the examination are the ones where an answer was accepted too early.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Worked item 1 — pleuritic chest pain with a swollen calf

**Question.** A patient in middle age presents with sudden-onset sharp pain on the right side of the chest, worse on inspiration, and breathlessness that started at the same moment. The left calf is swollen and tender. The pulse is fast, the oxygen saturation is normal, and the ECG shows sinus tachycardia with no ischaemic change. What is the next step in management?

**Step 1 — apply T-A-C-K before naming anything.** Tempo: sudden onset, simultaneous breathlessness. Associated features: pleuritic pain, a swollen calf. Context: risk factors for venous thromboembolism may or may not be stated — look for immobility, recent surgery, malignancy, pregnancy, thrombophilia, combined oral contraception. Killers: pulmonary embolism is the one that must not be missed here, and myocardial infarction and dissection stay on the list until excluded.

**Step 2 — what the normal results do and do not tell you.** A normal oxygen saturation and a non-diagnostic ECG do not exclude pulmonary embolism; the ECG in pulmonary embolism is most often sinus tachycardia and nothing more. That is exactly why the item included them — as false reassurance.

**Step 3 — pre-test probability first.** Use a structured clinical probability tool rather than gestalt, then act on the branch you land in. If the probability is low or unlikely, a negative D-dimer ends the evaluation and the answer is that you should not image. If the probability is high, the next step is imaging — CT pulmonary angiography, with ventilation-perfusion scanning where contrast is a problem — and a D-dimer is the wrong test because a positive result at that probability tells you nothing you did not already know.

**Step 4 — what happens while you wait.** If suspicion is high and imaging is not immediate, interim anticoagulation is the right answer unless there is a contraindication; if the probability is low and imaging is not indicated, neither anticoagulation nor antibiotics. Also mobilise the patient and consider mechanical prophylaxis for the leg.

> ⚡ **Examiner Trap.** The trap is the reassuring normal ECG and normal oxygen saturation, and the second trap is ordering a D-dimer in a patient whose pre-test probability is high and then waiting for it. A third trap is treating this as pneumonia because the patient is febrile, when the febrile patient with pleuritic pain and a swollen calf has a clot until proved otherwise.

#### Worked item 2 — jaundice, dark urine and pale stools

**Question.** A patient presents with jaundice, itching, dark urine and pale stools. There is no fever and no abdominal pain. Urinalysis shows bilirubin in the urine and no urobilinogen. What is the next step in management?

**Step 1 — read the dipstick before anything else.** Bilirubin in the urine means conjugated bilirubin, which means the problem is on the hepatobiliary side. No urobilinogen means no bilirubin is reaching the gut. Together with pale stools and itching, this is a cholestatic, obstructive picture.

**Step 2 — the absence of pain and fever is the clinical modifier.** A painless obstructive jaundice raises malignancy — pancreatic head, periampullary or biliary tree — to the top of the list, and in an older patient with weight loss that is the working diagnosis. The same picture with fever and right upper quadrant pain would be ascending cholangitis, which is a different and far more urgent branch requiring drainage. The same picture after a gallstone with pain that has settled raises post-choledocholithiasis.

**Step 3 — the next step is imaging.** Right upper quadrant ultrasound first, to look for dilated ducts and for gallstones, and the obstruction is then defined with MRCP or endoscopic retrograde cholangiography, with endoscopic treatment if it is a stone. Alongside, the liver enzymes, the coagulation studies and the assessment of the patient's fitness and nutritional status matter, and if malignancy is suspected the tissue diagnosis and staging follow.

**Step 4 — what you must not do.** Do not treat the itch as the problem. Do not give empiric antibiotics in a patient with no fever — there is nothing to treat, and it muddies the picture. Do not stop at "drug-induced cholestasis" without reviewing drugs, alcohol and recent illness. Do not forget that jaundice can also be the presenting feature of gallstone pancreatitis, where the pain is the leading feature and the bilirubin follows.

> ⚡ **Examiner Trap.** Reading dark urine as haematuria or as dehydration, and giving vitamin K or an antihistamine before the urinalysis has been interpreted. The second trap is assuming every jaundice with bilirubin in the urine is infective — hepatitis puts both bilirubin and urobilinogen in the urine, and the absent urobilinogen is the finding that separates obstruction from hepatitis.

#### Worked item 3 — fever, a new murmur and a painful skin lesion

**Question.** A patient who injects drugs presents with fever, a new regurgitant heart murmur and a painful red lesion on one toe. What is the next step in management?

**Step 1 — name the pattern.** Fever plus a new murmur plus a peripheral embolic phenomenon is infective endocarditis until proved otherwise, and in a person who injects drugs the tricuspid valve is the usual site, with septic pulmonary emboli. The skin lesion is either an embolic phenomenon or the entry point.

**Step 2 — cultures before antibiotics.** In a stable patient, take multiple blood culture sets from separate venepuncture sites before the first dose of antibiotic, because antibiotics sterilise the blood and you will never make the diagnosis with certainty afterwards. Then echocardiography — transthoracic first, with transoesophageal imaging when the transthoracic study is non-diagnostic, when the prosthetic valve needs defining, or when the complication needs to be seen.

**Step 3 — find the source and the complications.** Skin and soft tissue, dental work, injection drug use, prosthetic material and indwelling lines are the usual sources. Complications to look for include stroke, renal and splenic infarcts, and heart failure from valve destruction.

**Step 4 — keep the mimics in view.** Culture-negative endocarditis after prior antibiotics or caused by organisms that need special culture conditions, non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis in malignancy, and the murmur of pre-existing valve disease are all realistic distractors.

> ⚡ **Examiner Trap.** Giving antibiotics before taking cultures, because "the patient looks septic". If the patient is in septic shock, antibiotics come first and blood cultures are taken immediately before if that costs nothing — but the item is testing the sequence in the stable patient. The second trap is accepting the murmur as the patient's known valve disease.

#### Worked item 4 — one leg that is hot, red and swollen

**Question.** A patient presents with unilateral calf swelling, warmth and tenderness, and a patch of red skin over the calf. What is the next step in management?

**Step 1 — the split.** One leg is local until proven otherwise, so the differential is deep vein thrombosis, cellulitis, ruptured Baker's cyst, and lymphoedema, with an assessment for a mass or venous obstruction.

**Step 2 — what separates them.** Cellulitis produces warmth, redness and tenderness with systemic upset and, typically, a rash that is unilateral but not a discrete swollen calf; the swelling of cellulitis is usually less dramatic than the erythema suggests. DVT gives calf pain and swelling with a circumference difference and, typically, a lack of erythema. A ruptured Baker's cyst mimics DVT closely and is diagnosed by ultrasound, with fluid and synovial debris on aspiration. One feature does not separate them: in practice DVT and cellulitis coexist, and one complicates the other.

**Step 3 — the next step.** Compression ultrasound of the affected leg, with the duplex study including the iliac veins when the calf study is negative but suspicion persists. If there is no contraindication, treat the presumed clot with anticoagulation while imaging is arranged, and treat cellulitis with antibiotics covering streptococci and staphylococci, including the methicillin-resistant organism where the local pattern demands it.

**Step 4 — look upwards and behind.** Proximal symptoms, a swollen thigh, a chest symptom or a sudden collapse shifts you to imaging the chest and to a different branch of the same differential.

> ⚡ **Examiner Trap.** Diagnosing cellulitis from redness alone and sending the patient home on antibiotics, or diagnosing DVT from calf tenderness alone and leaving an arterial or necrotic problem untreated. A limb that is acutely swollen, dusky and painful out of proportion needs to be assessed for a vascular emergency rather than filed under either diagnosis.

#### The presentation-to-next-step map

| Presentation | First split | The investigation that answers it | What follows the result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chest pain | Cardiac vs non-cardiac | ECG and troponin; imaging for dissection, embolism and pneumothorax | Reperfusion, anticoagulation, drainage or analgesia |
| Dyspnoea | Acute vs chronic | Examination, oxygenation, chest radiograph, ECG, echocardiography | Diuresis, bronchodilators, antibiotics, anticoagulation, decompression |
| Jaundice | Pre-hepatic, hepatic, post-hepatic | Urinalysis and liver enzymes; ultrasound for obstruction | Drainage, endoscopy, or cause-specific treatment |
| Oedema | Local vs systemic | Urinalysis and protein, liver and cardiac assessment, ultrasound for thrombosis | Anticoagulation, antibiotics, diuresis or treating the systemic cause |
| Fever | Infectious vs non-infectious | Cultures, full blood count, inflammatory markers, urinalysis, chest radiograph | Early antibiotics if septic; imaging or source control when indicated |
| Weight loss | Appetite preserved vs lost | Blood count, thyroid function, glucose, coeliac serology, imaging and screening | Cause-specific treatment, or referral on a cancer pathway |

#### Where the presentations collide

The best items put a presentation in front of you and then make the usual script wrong. Myocardial infarction presents without chest pain in women, older adults and patients with diabetes, where dyspnoea, fatigue or indigestion is the presenting feature instead. Jaundice is the presenting feature of gallstone pancreatitis, where the pain comes first and the bilirubin follows. Fever without infection is drug fever, thyroiditis, thromboembolism, autoimmune disease, deep abscess, malignancy or factitious illness, and a normal white count with a negative culture set is a clue rather than a reassurance. Weight loss can be pure fluid loss from diuresis or heart failure rather than catabolism. Oedema without heart failure comes from calcium channel blockers, corticosteroids and NSAIDs, from venous insufficiency, from lymphoedema and from sleep apnoea. Anaemia presents as dyspnoea. Pulmonary embolism presents as syncope, as collapse in a postoperative patient, or as "just tired" with no chest pain at all.

#### Memory anchors for the six presentations

- **Chest pain — C-R-A-S.** Character, Radiation, Associated features, Screen (ECG and troponin).
- **Dyspnoea — T-A-O-E.** Tempo, Airway versus heart versus other causes, Orthopnoea and exertional pattern, Examination including chest, legs and jugular venous pressure.
- **Jaundice — U-R-A-M.** Urine dipstick, Reticulocytes and unconjugated bilirubin, liver enzymes, Malignancy and imaging.
- **Oedema — D-I-S-T.** Distribution, Ischaemic or venous cause, Systemic causes, Thyroid and drugs.
- **Fever — H-S-S-C.** Host risk, Source, Severity and sepsis, Cultures before treatment.
- **Weight loss — A-M-C-T.** Appetite, Muscle versus fat versus fluid, Clinical examination, Tests.

#### Distractor patterns in presentation-based items

The "no further investigation, observe" option is the correct answer when the pre-test probability is low and the patient is well, and the wrong answer when the presentation is time-critical — the option exists so that you choose deliberately rather than by reflex. The test with no consequence is wrong because a correct answer must change management. The number-chaser answers treat an abnormal value as the whole diagnosis, and treat the blood pressure rather than the chest pain. The premature-closure answers stop the differential at the first positive result, or close it with a normal early test in a patient whose story is still dangerous. The right answer for the wrong question is the commonest near-miss: naming the definitive therapy when the question asked for initial management, or naming a diagnosis when it asked what you would do next.

#### ⚡ Quick Exam Checklist

- Tempo classified before any other feature of the vignette
- Cardiac and other immediately fatal causes of chest pain excluded first
- Urinalysis used to split jaundice, and obstruction distinguished from hepatitis
- Oedema classified by distribution rather than assumed to be heart failure
- Fever: source sought, cultures taken before antibiotics in the stable patient, non-infectious causes considered
- Weight loss: appetite asked about, and muscle versus fat versus fluid separated
- Every investigation has a stated question, a pre-test probability and an action attached
- Stabilisation offered when the patient is unstable; definitive treatment offered when that is what is asked
- Answer written as an action, not as a label
