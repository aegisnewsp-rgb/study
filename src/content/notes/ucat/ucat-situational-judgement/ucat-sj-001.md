The Situational Judgement subtest is the one most candidates treat as a personality quiz. It is the one where that treatment loses you marks. Each item asks you to judge two distinct things, and confusing them drops you a band on the final report. This note unpacks what "appropriateness" and "importance" really mean in the UCAT, gives you the four-point scale you actually answer on, and shows you how to read a scenario for the dimension the question is testing.

Standard facts to keep straight: the SJT has 69 questions in 26 minutes, after a one-and-a-half-minute instruction section. The mark is reported as a band from 1 to 4, with band 1 the highest. Each correct answer that matches the ideal response scores full marks, and a response close to the ideal scores partial — that is the published marking rule and the source of the small differences between band 2 and band 3 candidates. Any other number — band cut-offs for specific universities, average scores by applicant category, year-on-year banding shifts — should be checked on ucat.ac.uk, since the Consortium publishes those numbers each admissions cycle.

### Quick revision

- **Two question types, not one.** "Appropriateness" tests whether an action is right or wrong. "Importance" tests whether a factor should weigh heavily.
- **Four-point scale.** Very, somewhat-but-not-ideal, opposite-very, opposite-somewhat. The middle two are the questions.
- **The professional, not the personal.** The answer is what a panel of doctors, not what you personally, would choose.
- **The scenario is short on purpose.** Re-read it once. Then commit.
- **Partial marks exist.** A "close to ideal" answer is worth more than a wild guess, so partial-credible calibration beats confident-but-wrong.

### Standard conceptual deep dive

#### Appropriateness questions — what to judge

The stem pattern is roughly: "A junior doctor notices X. How appropriate is each of the following responses?"

You have to pick, for each response, one of four ratings. The Consortium's published scale is:

- **A — Very appropriate:** the action is the right thing to do in this situation.
- **B — Appropriate, but not ideal:** the action is reasonable but there is a slightly better one.
- **C — Inappropriate, but not awful:** the action is wrong but understandable in the moment, or only mildly so.
- **D — Very inappropriate:** the action is clearly wrong, harmful or unprofessional.

The single most useful reframe: an action that protects the patient's safety or dignity, or that protects the team's professional standards, is at least appropriate. An action that puts either at risk is at least inappropriate.

#### Importance questions — what to judge

The stem pattern is: "A junior doctor is deciding what to do. How important are each of the following considerations?"

The published scale runs from very important through to not important at all. The fix is to separate *what to do* (the appropriateness question) from *what should weigh in* (the importance question). Often the two appear together: a scenario that asks you to rate actions for appropriateness, then asks you to rate the considerations that led to those actions. They reward different readings.

| | Appropr., action | Importance, factor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Asks | Is this the right thing to do? | Should this weigh heavily in deciding? |
| Right answer keys on | Professional standards (GMC, ABPI, NHS trust policies) | Patient safety and dignity, then honesty and consent |
| Common trap | "I would do this" instead of "the panel would do this" | Treating a factor as important because it is relevant, not because it matters most |

#### Reading the scenario for the question dimension

Each scenario is short — usually four to seven sentences. The trap is to do too much with it. A repeatable read is:

1. **One pass for the situation.** Who is involved, what is at stake, who is at risk.
2. **One pass for the question stem.** Appropriateness, or importance. Action, or factor.
3. **Re-read the options.** Some options will invert the question by treating the action as appropriate (when the stem asks importance), or by raising a factor the scenario did not invoke.
4. **Decide, do not deliberate.** The 26-minute timer forbids deliberation. First calibrated instinct usually lands in the right band.

The four-point scale on both question types rewards calibration. If you find yourself swinging between A and D in quick succession, you have picked the wrong reading. Stop and re-read the stem.

#### Common confusions that move you down a band

Confusion 1 — Appropriateness judged as personal preference. "I would do this" is not the question. The question is what the professional standards say. A patient has just been told bad news. Option A: "Take the patient aside privately." Option B: "Discuss with the patient's relative first because the relative already called and asked you to." Option B reads as family-friendly but puts relatives' interests above the patient's right to privacy. Most candidates rate B as appropriate on instinct; the panel scores it inappropriate because the patient's consent is the higher standard.

Confusion 2 — Importance judged as relatedness. A factor can be related to the scenario and still be unimportant. "How important is the patient's religion?" is important if it changes the treatment plan (a Jehovah's Witness refusing blood products, say) and unimportant if it does not. Do not rate a factor as important just because it is mentioned.

Confusion 3 — Treating "appropriate but not ideal" and "very appropriate" as interchangeable. The Consortium scoring gives full marks for matching the ideal and partial marks for being close. Calibration between A and B is where bands 1 and 2 separate.

Confusion 4 — Treating "inappropriate" and "very inappropriate" as interchangeable. A doctor runs late for a clinic and does not tell anyone: inappropriate. A doctor falsifies a patient's notes to cover a mistake: very inappropriate. Calibration between C and D matters for partial-credit scores.

#### Worked example — appropriateness

Scenario:

```
  You are a third-year medical student on a surgical placement. The
  consultant asks you to scrub in for an operation. In the changing
  room, you overhear two other students making a joke about a
  patient on the list. The patient is anaesthetised in the next
  room and has not consented to be discussed.
```

Question (appropriateness): "How appropriate is each of the following responses?"

```
  Response 1: "Politely tell the other students the patient has
  not consented to discussion and ask them to stop."

    Answer: Very appropriate. The action protects the patient's
    dignity and addresses the issue directly.

  Response 2: "Say nothing, because the consultant is senior and
  is more responsible for the learning environment than you are."

    Answer: Inappropriate, but not awful. Avoids confrontation but
    misses the opportunity to advocate for the patient at low risk
    to yourself.

  Response 3: "Report the other students to the consultant in
  private after the list."

    Answer: Appropriate, but not ideal. Escalating is reasonable,
    but the simpler conversational correction comes first and is
    usually credited higher in the published guidance.

  Response 4: "Laugh along to avoid being seen as someone who
  cannot take a joke."

    Answer: Very inappropriate. Adds to the harm, normalises it,
    and undermines the patient-team trust the role requires.
```

The published guidance would put R1 closest to ideal (full marks), R3 next (partial), R2 next (partial), R4 last. Calibration across the four matters for the partial marks.

#### Worked example — importance

Same scenario, importance question: "How important are each of the following considerations in deciding how to respond?"

```
  Consideration A: "Your professional duty to protect patient
  confidentiality and dignity."

    Answer: Very important. Directly cited in GMC and ABPI
    guidance, and the scenario is set up around it.

  Consideration B: "The consultant's preference for a quiet
  operating list."

    Answer: Of minor importance. Likely true but not a deciding
    factor; it does not change whether to act.

  Consideration C: "Your own position in the team as a student."

    Answer: Of minor importance. Relevant to the *approach* you
    take but not to whether the patient's dignity matters.

  Consideration D: "The other students' career implications if
  reported."

    Answer: Not important at all. The patients' interests come
    first; the consequences for colleagues are a secondary
  consideration, never the deciding one.
```

#### Worked example — calibration across the four-point scale

Take one response that is clearly right and one that is clearly wrong, then rate two in the middle and see if you can defend the rating with one sentence each. If you cannot defend with one sentence, you have not yet found the right anchor for that question.

```
  Scenario: A patient asks you about a treatment you are not
  qualified to explain.

  Response 1: "Explain what you can, name what you are uncertain
  about, and direct them to the qualified clinician."

    Anchor: appropriate and complete. Rating: A (Very appropriate).

  Response 2: "Explain what you can, omitting any uncertainty."

    Anchor: protects the patient from confusion but misleads
    about your limits. Rating: C (Inappropriate, but not awful)
    — silent on a professional standard.

  Response 3: "Refer them to the qualified clinician without
  saying anything else."

    Anchor: not wrong, but loses an opportunity. Rating: B
    (Appropriate, but not ideal).

  Response 4: "Make up an answer because the patient looks
  anxious."

    Anchor: dishonest. Rating: D (Very inappropriate).
```

#### Worked example — the "close to ideal" pattern

A patient asks for a second opinion. Two responses:

- A: "Tell the patient they have a right to a second opinion and offer to arrange one." This is the ideal — full marks.
- B: "Tell the patient second opinions are usually unnecessary and try to reassure them." Close, in that it addresses the anxiety, but it discourages the patient from exercising a right. Partial marks.

The published scoring description is "partial marks if your response is close to the correct answer." "Close to correct" means the same intent, with the same outcome — not the same wording. The calibration check: would a senior clinician describe both responses as recommending the same action? If yes, you are close. If no, you are not.

#### Anti-patterns to flag in your own answers

- **Personal moral reflex.** The most common anti-pattern. The test is not asking what you would do.
- **Confusing importance with relatedness.** A factor is mentioned, so you think it must be important. It might be.
- **Confusing appropriateness with reassurance.** Patients want to be reassured, but reassurance without honesty is not appropriate.
- **Group loyalty over patient outcome.** Protecting colleagues, refusing to escalate, deferring to seniority when the patient is at risk — these read as loyalty and score as inappropriate or very inappropriate.

### Memory anchors

- **Two reading modes.** Appropriateness asks what should be done. Importance asks what should weigh in.
- **Four points on the scale.** Very, somewhat-but-not-ideal, opposite-very, opposite-somewhat.
- **Patient safety, dignity, consent, honesty, professional standards.** These are the five highest-rated considerations.
- **Calibration over certainty.** A and B both correct, just one more so; calibration is where the partial marks come from.
- **Senior clinician anchor.** "Would a senior clinician on the panel describe this response as the same approach?" If yes, both responses are close to ideal.

---

### Common traps

- "I would do this, so it is appropriate." Personal instinct is not the panel.
- Treating relatedness as importance. A factor mentioned in the scenario is not automatically important.
- Failing to re-read the stem on importance items. They look like appropriateness items and get answered as such.
- Choosing A or D when B or C is calibrated. Confidence is not the same as the published answer.
- Skipping the partial-credible option in favour of a high-confidence wrong answer. Partial marks are real.

### What to do next

1. Take ten official SJT scenarios and rate the four responses on each. Without looking at the answer key, write one sentence defending each rating.
2. Build a flashcard set of professional standards phrases — GMC Good Medical Practice duties, ABPI code clauses, NHS consent guidance. Each card gives a duty and asks whether an action upholds or breaks it.
3. After each set, count how often you rated A vs B (or C vs D). If the ratio is asymmetric, you are over- or under-using the moderate categories.
4. Sit one full SJT mock under timed conditions, then review your calibration. The official UCAT practice tests are the right benchmark; current band-level descriptions and any year-on-year cohort figures are on ucat.ac.uk at the published Situational Judgement guidance pages.
