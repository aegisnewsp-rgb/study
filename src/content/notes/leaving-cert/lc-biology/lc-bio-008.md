# Information of Life: Genetic Inheritance — Leaving Certificate (Established) Biology Notes

Unit 1.4 is the mathematics unit of Strand 1, and that is an advantage rather than a chore: the marks come from method, not from memory. Almost every Higher-level question in this unit is one of four shapes — a monohybrid cross, a dihybrid cross, a cross showing incomplete dominance, or a pedigree carrying a sex-linked trait. If you can set up a Punnett square without hesitating and can justify each step, the content portion of this unit is largely done.

What makes it harder than a Punnett square is the layer above: **nuclear versus non-nuclear inheritance**, **linkage**, **epigenetics**, and the specification's explicit demand that you state the benefits *and the limitations* of Mendelian genetics. The limitation question is worth memorising separately, because it is the single most common way this unit is left half-answered — students write a page on predictive power and nothing on why Mendel's laws fail for complex traits.

---

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Allele** — an alternative form of a gene. **Genotype** — the allele combination an organism has (TT, Tt, tt). **Phenotype** — the observable characteristic that genotype produces.
- **Homozygous** (TT, tt) has two identical alleles; **heterozygous** (Tt) has two different alleles.
- **Dominant** allele is expressed in a heterozygote; **recessive** is masked. **Mendel's Laws**: *Law of segregation* — the two alleles for a gene separate during gamete formation, so each gamete carries only one of them. *Law of independent assortment* — alleles of different genes assort independently during gamete formation, provided the genes are on different chromosomes or far enough apart on the same one.
- **Monohybrid F2 ratio** 3:1 (phenotype), 1:2:1 (genotype). **Dihybrid F2 ratio** 9:3:3:1 when the two genes are unlinked.
- **Incomplete dominance** gives an intermediate phenotype rather than a dominant/recessive pair. The specification's worked example is the **codfish**: homozygous red × homozygous white gives all heterozygotes that are pink, and the F2 is 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white.
- **Sex determination in humans.** Female XX, male XY. The Y chromosome carries the genes for male development; the father's X goes to daughters and his Y to sons.
- **Sex-linked traits** are carried on the X chromosome. Because a male (XY) has only one X, a single recessive allele on it is expressed — the trait is **hemizygous**. Red–green colour blindness and haemophilia are the specification's named examples, both X-linked recessive.
- **Chromosome structure.** DNA double helix wrapped around histone proteins forms **chromatin**, which condenses into visible chromosomes. Each replicated chromosome has two **chromatids** joined at the **centromere**. Coding DNA specifies a protein; non-coding DNA includes introns and regulatory sequences.
- **Nuclear vs non-nuclear inheritance.** Nuclear (Mendelian) inheritance passes on genes on chromosomes in the nucleus and gives predictable ratios. Non-nuclear inheritance passes on genes in **mitochondrial DNA** and **chloroplast DNA**; it is maternal (mitochondria come from the egg cytoplasm) and cytoplasmic organelles are inherited whole, so it shows no segregation ratio.
- **Linkage.** Genes on the same chromosome are inherited together and do *not* assort independently, so a dihybrid cross of linked genes gives a ratio closer to 3:1 than 9:3:3:1. The specification says crossing over is not required.
- **Epigenetics.** Changes in **gene expression** caused by factors other than a change to the DNA sequence — environment, diet, stress, behaviour. Some epigenetic changes are reversible.

#### Examiner traps

- **Confusing genotype and phenotype ratio.** For a monohybrid cross the phenotype ratio is 3:1 but the genotype ratio is 1:2:1. Writing "3:1" for both loses a mark.
- **Getting the monohybrid ratio wrong in an F1 cross.** A cross between two heterozygotes gives 3:1; a cross between two homozygotes gives all heterozygotes and no 3:1 anywhere.
- **Assuming sons can inherit an X-linked trait from their father.** He gives sons his Y, so an X-linked trait on the father's X goes to his daughters.
- **Calling every affected male in a pedigree a carrier.** Males are hemizygous — they are affected, not carrying, because they have no second X.
- **Writing "chromosomes carry genes" and stopping.** The specification asks you to describe the structure: DNA, histones, chromatids, centromere.
- **Treating Mendel as a complete description of inheritance.** The specification explicitly requires the limitations.

#### 30-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Can you produce 3:1, 1:2:1 and 9:3:3:1 on demand without a table?
- [ ] Can you write the gametes for TtBb, Tt and tt in a crossed-out arrangement showing all combinations?
- [ ] Can you trace an X-linked recessive trait through a three-generation pedigree and identify the carriers?
- [ ] Can you define linkage and state the ratio it replaces 9:3:3:1 with?
- [ ] Can you give one real example of epigenetic inheritance and one limitation of Mendelian genetics?

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### The three crosses you must be able to do cold

**1. Monohybrid.** Pea plants with tall (T) and short (t) stems. Tall is dominant.

- *Cross:* Tt × Tt. Gametes: T and t from each parent.
- *Punnett square:* TT, Tt, Tt, tt → genotype ratio **1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt**.
- *Phenotype:* three tall : one short = **3:1**.
- *Test cross:* to find out the genotype of a tall plant you cross it with a homozygous recessive tt. If all offspring are tall the unknown is TT; if any are short it is Tt. The test cross is the classic application of segregation.

**2. Dihybrid, unlinked.** Round (R) and yellow (Y) seeds, both dominant.

- *Cross:* RrYy × RrYy. Gametes: RY, Ry, rY, ry from each parent.
- *Phenotype ratio:* **9 round yellow : 3 round green : 3 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green**.
- *Worked question.* Two round yellow peas from the RrYy plant are crossed. Their offspring include some round green seeds. What is the minimum possible genotype of each round yellow parent, and what proportion of offspring are round green?

*Answer.* A round yellow parent could be RRYY, RRYy, RrYY or RrYy. Because some offspring are round green, at least one parent must be carrying r, and because the offspring are yellow at least one parent must carry Y. The minimum genotype consistent with the data is **RrYy** for both parents. In an RrYy × RrYy cross, round green is the **rrY_** class = 3/16. Note the wording: the *minimum* possible genotype is what the evidence forces; you cannot claim RrYy from the data alone.

**3. Incomplete dominance.** The codfish allele for red (R) and white (W). Heterozygotes RW are pink.

- *Cross:* RR × WW → all offspring RW, all pink.
- *F2 cross:* RW × RW → RR, RW, RW, WW = **1 red : 2 pink : 1 white**.
- *Why it matters:* there is no dominant and no recessive allele, so the terms are meaningless here. Writing "pink is recessive to red" is the error the mark scheme penalises.

#### Sex-linked inheritance — the reasoning that gets you the pedigree marks

Take red–green colour blindness, X-linked recessive. Write the alleles as Xᴺ (normal vision) and Xⁿ (colour blind).

| Cross | Offspring |
| --- | --- |
| XᴺXᴺ × XᴺY | All normal vision |
| XᴺXⁿ × XᴺY | All normal vision; **all daughters carriers**, none colour blind |
| XᴺXⁿ × XⁿY | Daughters: ½ XᴺXⁿ (carriers), ½ XⁿXⁿ (colour blind). Sons: ½ XᴺY normal, ½ XⁿY colour blind |
| XⁿXⁿ × XᴺY | Daughters all XᴺXⁿ carriers; **sons all colour blind** |

Two rules generate every pedigree answer: **a son always gets his X from his mother**, and **a daughter always gets one X from her father**. Use them to read a pedigree backwards and forwards.

*Worked question.* A phenotypically normal woman has a colour-blind son. Her mother is colour-blind. Identify the genotypes of the woman and her mother.

*Answer.* The son is XⁿY, so his Xⁿ must have come from the woman. She is phenotypically normal, so she cannot be XⁿXⁿ; therefore she is **XᴺXⁿ, a carrier**. Her mother is colour-blind, so the woman also received an Xⁿ from her, which is consistent.

#### Linkage and non-Mendelian inheritance

**Linkage.** Genes located close together on the same chromosome are transmitted as a single unit because the chromosome is inherited intact. In a cross involving two linked genes the 9:3:3:1 ratio collapses to **3:1** for the two combinations in coupling (AB/ab), and further recombinations are rare. The reason recombination *can* occur is crossing over during meiosis — the specification explicitly says this detail is not required, so state the effect and leave the mechanism alone.

**Non-nuclear (cytoplasmic) inheritance.** Mitochondria and chloroplasts contain their own DNA and replicate independently. At fertilisation the egg contributes almost all the cytoplasm, so mitochondrial DNA is inherited **maternal only**: an affected mother transmits to all her children, an affected father transmits to none. Chloroplast DNA in plants shows a similar but less absolute pattern because of the chloroplasts present in pollen in some species. Contrast with nuclear inheritance, which follows Mendelian ratios and in which both parents contribute equally.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Benefits and limitations of Mendelian genetics — memorise both halves

The specification asks you to state both. Answers that give only one half are the most common failure in this unit.

**Benefits.** Mendelian genetics is predictive: given parental genotypes you can state the probability of each offspring genotype before fertilisation occurs, which is the basis of genetic counselling and of selective breeding programmes in agriculture. It is also unifying — the same rules describe a pea plant, a fruit fly and a human, because the mechanism of meiosis is shared across eukaryotes. It is a parsimonious model: two laws account for a very large amount of observed pattern.

**Limitations.** Mendel's laws describe single genes with clear dominance in large populations, and they predict *probabilities*, not certainties — in a family of four children the 3:1 ratio is a long-run average, not a promise for one family. They do not apply cleanly to **complex traits** such as height, skin colour or blood pressure, which involve multiple gene interactions plus environmental factors, and which therefore show a continuous distribution rather than discrete categories. They say nothing about how those multiple loci interact, and nothing about gene–environment effects. And they do not describe non-nuclear inheritance at all.

#### Worked Higher-level questions with answers

**Q1.** A pedigree shows an unaffected father and an unaffected mother with four sons, two of whom have haemophilia, and one unaffected daughter. Which grandparent is the most likely source of the allele, and why?
*A.* Haemophilia is X-linked recessive, so an affected son (XʰY) must have received Xʰ from his mother. The unaffected daughter rules out neither parent, but the most parsimonious explanation is that the mother's mother is a carrier XᴴXʰ — a carrier is unaffected, so the pedigree shows no further evidence. If the mother's father were affected, his Xʰ would go to every daughter, making the mother an obligate carrier, which is a stronger but less economical explanation because an obligate carrier carrier-line through two unaffected generations is not required by the data. State which explanation is *most likely* and why — the mark scheme rewards the parsimony argument.

**Q2.** Explain why mitochondria are described as semi-autonomous.
*A.* Mitochondria have their own DNA and their own 70S ribosomes and can replicate independently of the cell division cycle, so they control some of their own proteins. But the great majority of their proteins are encoded by nuclear DNA, imported from the cytoplasm, and they cannot exist without nuclear and cytoplasmic components supplied by the cell. They are therefore autonomous only in part, and they are transmitted only through the egg — which is exactly why non-nuclear inheritance shows no Mendelian ratio.

**Q3.** A plant with purple flowers (P) is crossed with a plant with white flowers (p). Offspring are 3 purple : 1 white. All offspring grow up and are allowed to self-pollinate. What proportion of the white-flowered grandchildren have purple flowers?
*A.* From a 3:1 ratio the purple parent must be heterozygous, Pp. White offspring are pp. Self-pollination of pp gives pp only. Therefore **zero** of the white-flowered offspring's progeny have purple flowers — the recessive allele is homozygous and cannot express. This is the commonest trap in the unit: a question that looks like it needs a Punnett square across generations but resolves in one line.

**Q4.** Describe one way in which epigenetic inheritance differs from inheritance by mutation.
*A.* A mutation is a change to the DNA base sequence and is permanent and heritable to all subsequent cells and usually to offspring. Epigenetic change modifies how a gene is expressed — through DNA methylation or histone modification, with environment, diet and stress among the triggers — without altering the sequence. It is therefore potentially reversible: when the environmental trigger is removed, expression can return to normal. It explains cases where identical twins with identical DNA show different phenotypes, and why the specification says some epigenetic changes are reversible.

#### How this unit pays elsewhere

- **Unit 2.3** reuses the difference between mitosis (genetic information unchanged) and meiosis (variation), which is where the physical mechanism of segregation and independent assortment sits.
- **Unit 2.5** applies these rules to gamete production and the X/Y system in human reproduction.
- **Unit 3.3** applies them to DNA profiling, where the same ideas about alleles and heterozygosity underpin paternity and forensic matching.
- **The Biology in Practice Investigation** is 40% of the grade and is assessed on designing an investigation, gathering primary data and justifying conclusions — the same discipline the pedigree questions test.

#### Mistakes students actually make

1. **Leaving out the limitations of Mendelian genetics.** Write one sentence on multi-gene and environmental interaction and the loss stops.
2. **Confusing "genotype" and "phenotype" in a ratio question.** Label both explicitly: phenotype 3:1, genotype 1:2:1.
3. **Forgetting that the ratio applies to the F2, not the F1.** An F1 from two homozygous parents is genetically uniform; there is no ratio to read.
4. **Treating the Punnett square as the whole answer.** The square is the method; the conclusion sentence is the mark. Finish with "so approximately 25% of the offspring will be short".
5. **Slipping on a sex-linked cross.** Write the parent's full gametes — Xᴺ, Xⁿ (or Xᴺ, Y) — rather than trying to reason without them.
6. **Describing a chromosome without its parts.** DNA, histones, two chromatids, centromere: the specification lists them and reads for them.
7. **Ignoring non-nuclear inheritance entirely**, because the cross looks like an ordinary one. Mitochondrial traits are detected by an affected mother passing to *all* children, not by a 3:1 ratio.
