# Apply co-ordinate geometry methods in solving problems (91256) — NCEA Level 2 Mathematics Notes

Achievement Standard 91256 is the first of fourteen NCEA Level 2 Mathematics standards. It is internally assessed, carries 2 credits, and tests co-ordinate geometry with straight lines. Most schools schedule 91256 early in the year so the skills carry into 91257 (graphical methods) and 91261 (algebraic methods). The standard is small in credit but dense in content — every topic here is reused in later Level 2 and Level 3 standards.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **2 credits**, internally assessed by the school, NZQA moderated.
- Topics: gradient, midpoint, distance, parallel and perpendicular lines, equations of straight lines (y = mx + c and ax + by + c = 0 form), linear interpolation and extrapolation.
- **Merit** requires relational thinking — connecting gradient and distance, using the geometric meaning of the slope.
- **Excellence** requires extended abstract thinking — justifying why a method is the most efficient, generalising the result.

#### Examiner traps

- Sign errors on gradient when the run is negative — (−5)/(−3) = 5/3, not −5/3.
- Mixing up parallel and perpendicular — parallel lines have the same gradient; perpendicular lines have gradients that multiply to −1.
- Confusing midpoint (average of x and y) with distance (Pythagoras-style calculation).
- Forgetting to write the equation in the form requested (e.g., y = mx + c when the form required is ax + by + c = 0).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Gradient

Gradient m between two points (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂):

m = (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁)

Note the order: subtract y₂ − y₁ and x₂ − x₁ in the same direction. If you swap the order of subtraction, the sign flips but the magnitude is unchanged.

**Special cases:**

- Horizontal line: m = 0 (y₂ = y₁).
- Vertical line: gradient is undefined (x₂ = x₁).
- Positive gradient: line goes up from left to right.
- Negative gradient: line goes down from left to right.

#### Midpoint

Midpoint M of (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂):

M = ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2)

The midpoint is the average of the x-coordinates and the average of the y-coordinates. This is the point that is equidistant from both endpoints.

#### Distance

Distance d between (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) using Pythagoras:

d = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²)

Notice the squares — distance is always positive. The distance formula is Pythagoras' theorem applied to the horizontal and vertical separations.

#### Parallel and perpendicular lines

- **Parallel lines** have equal gradients: m₁ = m₂.
- **Perpendicular lines** have gradients that multiply to −1: m₁ × m₂ = −1 (or, equivalently, m₂ = −1/m₁).

A horizontal line (m = 0) is perpendicular to a vertical line (undefined gradient). A line with gradient 2/3 is perpendicular to a line with gradient −3/2.

#### Equations of straight lines

**Slope-intercept form:** y = mx + c, where m is the gradient and c is the y-intercept.

**Point-slope form:** y − y₁ = m(x − x₁), where (x₁, y₁) is a known point on the line.

**General form:** ax + by + c = 0, where a, b, c are integers (often with a > 0).

To convert ax + by + c = 0 to slope-intercept form, solve for y:

by = −ax − c

y = (−a/b)x − c/b

So m = −a/b and c = −c/b.

#### Linear interpolation and extrapolation

- **Interpolation** — using two known points to estimate a value that lies between them.
- **Extrapolation** — using two known points to estimate a value that lies outside them.

Both rely on the assumption that the relationship is linear. Extrapolation carries more risk because the linearity may not hold far from the known points.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Why gradient is the heart of co-ordinate geometry

Gradient is the single concept that connects to every other topic in 91256:

- Parallel and perpendicular lines are defined by gradient relationships.
- The equation of a line is built around its gradient.
- Distance problems use the gradient and the Pythagorean separation together.

A Merit-grade student can find the gradient of a line given two points and use it to check parallelism or perpendicularity. An Excellence-grade student can also derive the relationship between the gradients of perpendicular lines and explain why it is what it is: if one line makes angle θ with the x-axis, the other makes angle θ + 90°, and tan(θ + 90°) = −cot(θ) = −1/tan(θ), giving m₁ × m₂ = −1.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Parallel lines have gradients that add to zero." No — perpendicular lines have gradients that multiply to −1. Parallel lines have gradients that are equal.
- "Midpoint and distance are the same." No — midpoint is the average position; distance is the separation.
- "All straight lines can be written in y = mx + c form." Yes, but vertical lines cannot — x = 3 is a vertical line, and there is no y = mx + c representation.
- "Gradient is just the slope." More than that — gradient is the rate of change of y with respect to x, which connects to calculus in later standards.
