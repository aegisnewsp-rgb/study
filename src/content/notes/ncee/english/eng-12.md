# Idioms and Simple Proverbs

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

An **idiom** is a fixed group of words whose meaning cannot be read from the individual words, such as "kick the bucket" meaning *to die*. A **proverb** is a short traditional saying that expresses a truth or advice, such as "A stitch in time saves nine."

For NCEE English, three quick rules cover most items: decode idioms **figuratively**, not literally; pick the proverb whose **moral matches the context**; and use **surrounding words** to guess any unfamiliar expression.

- Idioms = figurative phrases; proverbs = complete wise statements.
- NCEE tests idioms/proverbs through meaning choice and sentence completion.
- Mismatching proverb morals is the most common reason candidates lose marks here.

| Term | Meaning in NCEE context |
| --- | --- |
| Idiom | Figurative fixed expression (e.g., *spill the beans* = reveal a secret) |
| Proverb | Short wise saying giving advice (e.g., *Honesty is the best policy*) |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### What Makes an Idiom Different
An idiom works as a single semantic unit. The words "hold your horses" literally describe animals, but the idiom means *be patient*. NCEE items underline the idiom and offer four meaning options; only one preserves the **figurative** sense. Students who pick the literal option lose the mark.

#### How Proverbs Function
Proverbs are complete sentences that carry a moral. The proverb "Unity is strength" teaches that people working together achieve more than individuals. In NCEE sentence-completion items, the blank in most keys needs a proverb whose **moral aligns** with the surrounding story, not just any proverb about a related topic.

#### Categories Worth Learning

- Body-part idioms: *cold feet, lend a hand, keep an eye on*.
- Animal idioms: *hold your horses, let the cat out of the bag*.
- Colour idioms: *green with envy, once in a blue moon*.
- Food idioms: *piece of cake, spill the beans*.

#### Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Reading idioms word-for-word instead of decoding them.
- Confusing proverbs with idioms and treating them as the same thing.
- Picking a proverb based on keyword overlap rather than matching the exact moral.

| Idiom | Figurative meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Beat around the bush | Avoid the main point |
| Hit the nail on the head | State something exactly right |
| Bite the bullet | Accept something difficult bravely |
| Burn the midnight oil | Work late into the night |

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Context-Clue Strategy for NCEE Passages
Unfamiliar idioms in NCEE comprehension passages can be decoded by reading one sentence before and one after the expression. Look for **contrast signals** (*but, however, though*) which in most keys point to the opposite meaning, or **example signals** (*for example, such as*) which point to a synonym. This skill also helps in the objective section, where option wording in many papers mirrors the passage's tone.

#### Distinguishing Proverbs From Idioms in Exam Stems
NCEE stems sometimes ask: "Which of the following proverbs best suits the passage?" A proverb must (1) be a full statement, (2) carry advice or a moral, and (3) match the passage's theme. Idioms fail all three tests. Candidates who memorise proverbs by theme — honesty, hard work, unity, patience, caution — answer faster.

#### Connections to Adjacent NCEE Topics
This topic overlaps with **vocabulary in context**, **sentence completion**, and **comprehension summary** questions. Strong idiom/proverb recognition also improves performance in **oral English** items that test fixed expressions used in everyday speech.

#### Practice Prompts

1. In a passage where a trader refuses to lower his price even after bargaining, choose the idiom that fits: *beat around the bush, hit the nail on the head, stand one's ground, spill the beans.*
2. A short story describes pupils who mocked a classmate and later faced the same problem themselves. Pick the proverb whose moral matches: *United we stand, divided we fall / He who laughs last laughs longest / Two heads are better than one / A friend in need is a friend indeed.*
