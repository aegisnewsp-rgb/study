# The Constitution as Supreme Law

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Read this section first if you have less than an hour before the paper.

Kenya's Constitution of 2010 sits at the top of every legal pyramid in the country. Article 2(1) declares it the **supreme law of the Republic**, and Article 2(4) wipes out any inconsistent rule — but only to the extent of the clash, not in its entirety.

- **Article 2(1)** — Constitution is supreme law.
- **Article 2(2)** — binds every person and every State organ (national + county).
- **Article 2(4)** — inconsistent law is **void to the extent of inconsistency**.
- **Article 2(5)–(6)** — ratified treaties and international custom apply unless they contradict the Constitution.
- **Article 259** — interpret purposively, promoting spirit, purport and objects.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** "**2-1-2-4-259**" — Supreme, Binds-all, Voids-conflict, Interpret-purposefully.

**Hierarchy to recall:** Constitution > Acts of Parliament > Delegated legislation > County bylaws.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Working knowledge for the two-week-to-two-month revision window.

#### What supremacy actually means

Supremacy is not a slogan; it is a set of legal consequences. When Article 2(1) calls the Constitution supreme, three things follow: (1) no other law can override it, (2) every other law derives its validity from it, and (3) courts must prefer it where conflict arises. Article 2(2) extends the binding force downward — every State organ at both levels of government, and every natural or juristic person, must read the Constitution as the baseline of legality.

Article 2(4) is the enforcement teeth: an inconsistent law is invalid, but **only to the extent of the inconsistency**. A statute with one offending clause does not vanish wholesale; the surviving portions continue. Parliament, not the Judiciary, is named as the body that must pass enabling legislation to give effect to constitutional rights.

#### Comparison matrix — easily confused concepts

| Feature | Article 2 (Supremacy) | Article 1 (Sovereignty) | Article 259 (Interpretation) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core idea | Constitution overrides all law | People hold sovereign power | Read the Constitution purposively |
| Source of authority | Legal hierarchy | Political legitimacy | Judicial method |
| Test effect | Voiding inconsistent laws | Limits who can exercise state power | Guides how courts resolve ambiguity |
| Common trap | Treated as identical to Article 1 | Confused with supremacy | Ignored in MCQ reasoning |

#### Ranking the legal sources

The order of authority in Kenya is fixed: Constitution first, then Acts of Parliament, then delegated (subsidiary) legislation, then county assembly bylaws. Common law and customary law operate below the Constitution and are invalid to the extent they conflict with it.

#### Worked scenario with full resolution

A county assembly passes a bylaw requiring market traders to surrender trading licences on demand to any county inspector. Parliament had earlier enacted the Traders Licensing Act capping such powers and requiring a court order. A trader is arrested for refusing surrender without a court order. He claims the bylaw is unconstitutional.

**Step 1 — Identify the conflict.** The bylaw removes the court-order safeguard the Act requires.
**Step 2 — Apply Article 2(4).** A bylaw that contradicts a higher-ranking Act is invalid to the extent of the inconsistency. The confiscation clause dies; the rest of the bylaw can stand.
**Step 3 — Apply Article 259.** Interpret the bylaw in line with the Traders Licensing Act and the Constitution's protection of property (Article 40).
**Step 4 — Outcome.** The arrest is unlawful; the bylaw clause is void; the trader can sue for unlawful deprivation of property under Article 23.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Candidates in many papers say the bylaw is "completely null and void." Wrong. Article 2(4) limits invalidity to the inconsistent portion — partial invalidity, not total nullity.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> For deep revision, edge cases, and exam-style strategy over months.

#### Edge cases that surface in tough MCQs

1. **Pre-2010 statutes saved by Schedule 6.** Laws in force on 27 August 2010 continue, but any inconsistent provision is read down after five years. Watch for questions asking when a colonial-era statute finally fell.
2. **Treaty vs Constitution clash.** Article 2(5) makes ratified treaties part of Kenyan law, but Article 2(6) submits them to the Constitution. The Constitution always wins; the treaty is applied only if consistent.
3. **Customary law ceiling.** Customary law applies only where it does not conflict with the Constitution — Article 2(6) read with the Fifth Schedule. Custom that violates a fundamental right is excluded.
4. **Amendment rigidity.** Article 255–257 demands a referendum for clauses touching sovereignty, devolution, the Bill of Rights, term limits, and the amendment procedure itself. An ordinary Bill cannot touch them — this is where supremacy meets entrenchment.

#### Advanced traps worth memorising

| Trap | Why students fall for it | Correct reading |
|---|---|---|
| Saying the entire statute is void on inconsistency | Reads "invalid" as "null" | Void only to the extent of inconsistency |
| Treating Parliament's enactment duty as automatic | Skips the gap between declaration and implementation | Article 2(4) obliges Parliament to act |
| Confusing supremacy with sovereignty | Both sound like the same word | Supremacy is legal rank; sovereignty is the people's power |
| Forgetting purposive interpretation | Picks the literal meaning in MCQs | Article 259 demands spirit, purport and objects |

#### Advanced practice prompts

1. A 1962 statute still on the books contradicts a 2014 constitutional provision protecting freedom of expression. Apply Article 2(4) and Schedule 6 to determine what survives and when.
2. Parliament ratifies a treaty requiring detention without trial for terrorism suspects. Show how Article 2(5)–(6) and Article 25 (non-derogable rights) interact, and name the body that must resolve the clash.

#### Exam strategy

This topic contributes roughly 3% of the paper weight and in most keys appears as one scenario-based MCQ plus a direct Article-recall item. Mark the supremacy clauses first, then check the trap wording — "void" versus "void to the extent of inconsistency" decides most options.
