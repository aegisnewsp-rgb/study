# Understanding Implied Meaning (Inference) — OSSLT Reading 2

Reading 2 is the second-largest cluster of items on the OSSLT. The EQAO Framework describes it as the reading skill of understanding ideas and information that are stated indirectly and that require the reader to make reasonable inferences. Inference items sit beside Reading 1 items on the same selection, but they ask a different question: not "what does the selection say?" but "what does the selection mean without saying it?"

### Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

#### What Reading 2 actually tests

- **Drawing conclusions.** The selection implies a conclusion; the item asks for the most reasonable statement that follows from the selection.
- **Identifying the writer's tone.** Is the writer being sarcastic, sympathetic, frustrated, hopeful, amused? The selection signals this through word choice, not labels.
- **Identifying the writer's attitude toward the topic.** Does the writer approve, disapprove, or stay neutral? Items ask "the writer would most likely agree that..." or "the writer's view is best described as..."
- **Inferring what a detail suggests.** A specific fact in the selection points to a broader pattern. Items ask "the detail about X suggests that..."

#### One-pass drill

1. Read the selection once for sense.
2. Identify the items that ask for inference (look for words like "most likely," "suggests," "implies," "the writer's attitude").
3. For each item, write down in your own words what the selection implies. Then compare to the choices.
4. The right answer is the choice that is *reasonable and supported by the selection* — not just any plausible statement.

#### Examiner traps on Reading 2

- A choice that is *plausible in real life* but not supported by the selection. Reading 2 still requires the inference to be grounded in the text.
- A choice that goes too far. Inference items ask for the most reasonable conclusion, not the most dramatic one.
- A choice that contradicts an explicit statement in the selection. If the selection states X, an inference that means "not X" is wrong.

### Standard — Regular Study (2d-2mo)

#### Strategy for Reading 2 passages

**Mark inference items separately from Reading 1 items.** Use a different mental tag (or different colour if writing on paper). The mental set for Reading 1 is "find the detail"; the mental set for Reading 2 is "what does this detail lead to?"

**Identify the writer's tone through word choice.** Tone is set by adjectives, verbs, and the phrases the writer uses to describe things. A writer who calls a city "vibrant" sounds approving; a writer who calls the same city "cramped" sounds disapproving. List two or three tone words as you read and use them to predict the answer.

**For conclusion items, ask "what must be true?"** Not "what could be true?" or "what might be true?" — what *must* be true given the details in the selection. The right answer is the choice the selection makes unavoidable.

**For attitude items, separate topic from stance.** The writer's attitude is the stance toward the topic, not the topic itself. "The writer is talking about city traffic" is a topic. "The writer disapproves of the new road" is an attitude.

#### Practice routine for Reading 2

- Take one Real-Life Narrative. Cover the Reading 1 items and do only the Reading 2 items. Time yourself on inference alone.
- Take one News Report. News Report Reading 2 items often ask about the writer's purpose — to inform, to alert, to advocate. Identify the verbs and adjectives that signal purpose.
- Take one Dialogue. Dialogue Reading 2 items ask about the relationship between speakers and what one character implies about another. Read the subtext, not just the literal lines.
- Take one Information Paragraph. Reading 2 in information text is often about *why* the writer chose to explain something — what the writer wants the reader to take away.

#### How Reading 2 differs from Reading 3

- Reading 2 is *within the selection*: the inference is supported by the selection alone.
- Reading 3 is *across the selection and the reader's knowledge*: the item asks the reader to link the selection to their own experience.
- Reading 2 answer choices are grounded in the selection. Reading 3 answer choices often mention the reader's experience.

### Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo-6mo)

#### How Reading 2 connects to the other OSSLT items

- **Reading 1** gives you the explicit facts that Reading 2 then extends. If Reading 1 is shaky, Reading 2 is harder because you are inferring from facts you have not pinned down.
- **Reading 3** builds on Reading 2. Reading 2 infers what the writer means; Reading 3 connects that meaning to the reader's own knowledge.
- **The long-writing tasks** also depend on inference. The Series-of-Paragraphs task asks for an opinion supported by reasons drawn from the source — the reasons are inferred from the source's claims, not directly stated.

#### Common errors and the correction

- **"The answer must be in the selection."** For Reading 2, the answer is *supported* by the selection, not stated in it. The selection contains the clues; the answer states the conclusion.
- **"Any reasonable inference works."** The inference must be the *most reasonable* one and must be supported by the selection. A choice that requires adding new information is wrong.
- **"I am putting in my own opinion."** Reading 2 is the writer's view, not yours. The item asks what the writer implies, not what you think.

#### Exam technique

- Underline the words in the selection that signal the writer's tone or attitude. Use them to test each answer choice.
- For "most likely" items, ask which choice is *least* likely to be wrong. Eliminate the choice that contradicts an explicit statement first.
- If two choices seem equally supported, pick the one that uses the more general language ("many," "often") rather than the more specific ("all," "always"). The selection often supports a general inference but not a universal one.
- Use the platform's flag-for-review function. Do not leave blanks on selected-response items.
