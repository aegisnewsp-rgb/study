# Pythagoras, Area and Volume — Junior Cycle Mathematics Notes

Three ideas that look separate and are not: Pythagoras connects lengths, the area formulas are built from those lengths, and the volume formulas are built from the areas. The strand's learning outcomes ask you to select the appropriate formula from a diagram and to reason about why an answer is plausible — so the marks are split between the computation and the choice.

## Pythagoras' theorem

For a right-angled triangle, the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares on the other two sides: *a*² + *b*² = *c*².

**Identify the hypotenuse first.** It is the side opposite the right angle — always the longest side. Both other terms are positive, so the hypotenuse's square is the sum, never the other way round. Candidates who substitute in the wrong order get a negative under the square root and conclude the diagram is impossible.

**All three forms are needed.** Given two sides, find the third:

- *c* = √(*a*² + *b*²) — hypotenuse missing
- *a* = √(*c*² − *b*²) — a shorter side missing

Subtracting a larger square from a smaller one gives a negative number; that tells you immediately which side you mis-identified.

**The converse** is assessed: if *a*² + *b*² = *c*²* then the triangle is right-angled. The specification expects you to use it to justify that a shape has a right angle before you use Pythagoras on it.

**Isosceles triangle shortcut** — worth knowing because it halves the work: for an isosceles triangle split down the middle into two right triangles, find the height, and then add the two halves for the original area.

**3D Pythagoras.** The same theorem applied twice. In a cuboid with dimensions *a*, *b* and *c*, the base diagonal is √(*a*² + *b*²) and the space diagonal is √(*a*² + *b*² + *c*²). Draw the intermediate triangle — the mark scheme wants to see the two applications.

## Area

| Shape | Formula | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Rectangle | *lw* | |
| Triangle | ½ *bh* | *b* is the base, *h* the **perpendicular** height |
| Trapezium | ½ (*a* + *b*) *h* | Parallel sides *a* and *b* |
| Parallelogram | *bh* | *h* perpendicular to the base |
| Circle | π*r*² | Or ¼π*d*² using the diameter |
| Sector | (θ/360) π*r*² | *θ* in degrees |
| Circle | 2π*r* | Circumference |

Three recurring errors:

**The height must be perpendicular.** In a slanted triangle the base you choose need not have its opposite vertex directly above it. If the question gives you a slanted side, the perpendicular height is a different length. Test: if you drop a perpendicular from the opposite vertex and it falls outside the base, you have the wrong pair.

**Units.** If the side is in centimetres, the area is in square centimetres and the volume in cubic centimetres. Stating the unit is a mark.

**Compound shapes.** Split into rectangles, triangles and trapezia, calculate each, add, and subtract any cut-outs. Show the split with lines on the diagram — the marking schemes expect the decomposition to be visible.

## Volume and surface area

**Volume of a cuboid** = *lwh*. **Area of a cuboid** = 2(*lw* + *lh* + *wh*).

**Cylinder**: volume = π*r*²*h*; curved surface area = 2π*r h*; total surface area = 2π*r*² + 2π*r h*. The distinction between curved and total surface area is a named outcome and is asked directly — check which one the question wants.

**Cone**: volume = (1/3)π*r*²*h*; curved surface area = π*r l* where *l* is the slant height. The factor of one third is the whole difference between a cone and a cylinder of the same base and height, and candidates who omit it lose the central mark.

**Sphere**: volume = (4/3)π*r*³; surface area = 4π*r*².

**Prism** volume = base area × perpendicular height. This is the general form that the cuboid and the cylinder are both instances of, and the cylinder appears because its base is a circle — which is why the cylinder's volume is π*r*²*h*. Recognising the general form makes the formulas memorable rather than arbitrary.

**Cone as a third of a cylinder** is the same idea: it occupies one third of the cylinder that encloses it, which is another way of remembering the factor.

## Estimating and reasonableness

Because this is a specification outcome and not just an examination habit, the exam asks you to check whether an answer is sensible. A door with area under a square metre is implausible; a swimming pool with a surface area of a few square centimetres is wrong. If your answer is off by a factor of a hundred, you have probably confused radius with diameter or multiplied the base area by the radius instead of the height — and the estimate tells you so before you write it down.

Two checks that catch most errors: **the radius is half the diameter**, and **volume units are cubic while area units are square**.

## Self-check

1. A right-angled triangle has sides 5 and 12. What is the third side, and why is it not √(144 − 25) = √119? *(5 and 12 must be the two shorter sides, because √(25 + 144) = 13 is a whole number while √119 is not, and because in a right triangle the hypotenuse is the longest. Starting from 144 − 25 assumes 12 is the hypotenuse, which would mean 12 > 5 and the geometry does not work out.)*
2. A cone and a cylinder have the same base and the same height. What is the relationship between their volumes? *(The cone's volume is exactly one third of the cylinder's — V_cone = (1/3)πr²h* against *V*_cylinder = πr*²*h*. Omitting the factor of a third is the single most expensive error in this block.)*
3. A triangular flag has base 40 cm and a slanted side 30 cm. Can you use 30 as the height? *(No. The height must be perpendicular to the base. Whether the perpendicular falls inside or outside the base depends on the third side, and either way the given 30 cm is a slanted length, not the perpendicular height.)*
4. A cuboid is 2 × 3 × 6. What is the space diagonal? *(First √(2² + 3²) = √13 for the base diagonal, then √(13 + 36) = √49 = 7. Pythagoras applies twice, and the intermediate step is what earns the mark.)*
5. Why is surface area of a sphere 4πr² while its volume is (4/3)πr³? *(They are different quantities with different dimensions: surface area has units of length squared and volume has units of length cubed. Memorising them as a pair without understanding the units is what produces the frequent swap.)*
