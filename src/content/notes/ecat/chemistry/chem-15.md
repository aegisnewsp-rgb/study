# Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your ECAT Chemistry paper.

**Alcohols** carry an –OH group on a saturated sp³ carbon; **phenols** carry it directly on a benzene ring; **ethers** have a C–O–C linkage with no O–H bond. The –OH hydrogen enables hydrogen bonding, which is why methanol, ethanol, and water mix completely, and why C₂H₅OH boils at 351 K while dimethyl ether boils at 248 K despite the same molecular mass (46 g mol⁻¹). Acidity runs **phenol > water > 1° alcohol > 2° alcohol > 3° alcohol** because the phenoxide ion is resonance-stabilised over the ring.

- **Acidity comparison:** pKa(phenol) ≈ 10, pKa(water) ≈ 15.7, pKa(ethanol) ≈ 16.
- **Lucas test (ZnCl₂/HCl):** 3° alcohol → cloudiness immediately; 1° → no reaction at room temperature.
- **Williamson synthesis:** R–ONa + R′–X (SN2) → R–O–R′; aryl halides fail because C(sp²)–X is too strong for SN2.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students who have a few days to a couple of months.

#### Classification and Bonding

Monohydric alcohols (ethanol), dihydric glycols (HOCH₂CH₂OH), and polyhydric glycerol (CH₂OH–CHOH–CH₂OH) are distinguished by the number of –OH groups. Primary, secondary, and tertiary alcohols are defined by whether the C bearing –OH is bonded to one, two, or three carbon atoms. Phenols always show the –OH directly bonded to an sp² aromatic carbon, while the C–O–H bond angle in alcohols sits near 104.5° (tetrahedral, sp³).

#### Acidity and Hydrogen Bonding

Hydrogen bonding raises the boiling point sharply and explains water solubility of small alcohols. Acidity, however, follows conjugate-base stability: phenoxide delocalises the negative charge across ortho and para positions of the ring, whereas an alkoxide (RO⁻) has no such stabilisation, so phenol is roughly a million times more acidic than ethanol — strong enough to react with NaOH but not with NaHCO₃.

#### Key Reactions at a Glance

| Reaction | Reagent / Condition | Product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acid-catalysed dehydration (intramolecular) | conc. H₂SO₄, 443 K | Alkene + H₂O |
| Acid-catalysed dehydration (intermolecular) | conc. H₂SO₄, 413 K | Symmetrical ether + H₂O |
| Oxidation of 1° alcohol | K₂Cr₂O₇/H⁺ or KMnO₄ | Carboxylic acid |
| Oxidation of 2° alcohol | K₂Cr₂O₇/H⁺ | Ketone |
| Oxidation of 3° alcohol | K₂Cr₂O₇/H⁺ | No reaction |
| Williamson ether synthesis | R–ONa + R′–X (SN2) | R–O–R′ |
| Kolbe's reaction | Phenol + NaOH, then CO₂, 390 K, 4–7 atm | Salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid) |

- Acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ turns orange dichromate to green Cr³⁺ with primary and secondary alcohols only.
- Phenol + neutral FeCl₃ gives a violet-blue complex — a textbook identification test.
- Bromine water with phenol forms a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol instantly.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Mechanisms and Selectivity

Dehydration of alcohols proceeds through a protonated –OH intermediate that loses water to give a carbocation; tertiary alcohols dehydrate fastest because the (CH₃)₃C⁺ cation is the most stabilised. Williamson synthesis is strictly SN2, so the alkyl halide must be primary (or methyl); using a tertiary halide with a bulky alkoxide produces an elimination product (alkene) instead — a classic examiner trap.

#### Worked Example — Williamson Synthesis

Target: prepare ethyl methyl ether (CH₃–O–C₂H₅) from methanol and ethanol.

1. Form sodium ethoxide: C₂H₅OH + Na → C₂H₅ONa + ½H₂↑.
2. React with methyl iodide: C₂H₅ONa + CH₃I → CH₃–O–C₂H₅ + NaI.

This route works because methyl iodide is unhindered; reversing the roles and using C₂H₅I + CH₃ONa also succeeds, but aryl halides such as C₆H₅Cl fail entirely under SN2 conditions, so aryl ethers (e.g. anisole) must be made via the Ullmann reaction, not Williamson.

#### Common Traps and Connections

- 1° alcohol oxidation goes all the way to the carboxylic acid with strong oxidisers; PCC or DIBAL-H at low temperature is required to stop at the aldehyde.
- Intermolecular dehydration gives an **ether**, intramolecular gives an **alkene** — the temperature (413 K vs 443 K) controls which pathway dominates.
- Diethyl ether and butan-1-ol are C₄H₁₀O isomers but ether boils at 308 K while butanol boils at 390 K; do not confuse them.

#### Practice Prompts

1. Rank in order of acidity: phenol, ethanol, water, acetic acid, tert-butanol. Justify using pKa values.
2. Identify the product when (CH₃)₃C–OH is treated with conc. H₂SO₄ at 443 K, and explain why the 3° alcohol reacts fastest.
