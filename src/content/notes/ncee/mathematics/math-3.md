# Factors and Multiples (HCF and LCM)

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Factor** of N is any integer that divides N exactly; **multiple** of N is any integer of the form N × k (where k is a whole number). The **Highest Common Factor (HCF)** is the largest integer dividing two or more numbers without remainder. The **Lowest Common Multiple (LCM)** is the smallest positive integer exactly divisible by each of them.

> **Memory anchor:** HCF = "biggest shared divisor"; LCM = "smallest shared dividend".

#### Must-know identity
For any pair of positive integers a and b: **HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b**. Use it as a quick cross-check whenever you compute both.

- Co-prime numbers (e.g. 8 and 9): HCF = **1**, LCM = **product**.
- When one number is a multiple of the other: HCF = smaller, LCM = larger.

---

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Definitions and core distinction
A **factor** of N satisfies N = a × b, so both a and b are factors of N. A **multiple** of N is N, 2N, 3N, … produced by scaling. A **common factor** divides every number in a set; a **common multiple** is divisible by every number in a set.

#### Prime factorisation method
Write each number in prime form: N = p₁^a₁ × p₂^a₂ × … × pₖ^aₖ. Then:

- **HCF** = product of primes common to all numbers, each raised to the **lowest** power occurring.
- **LCM** = product of every prime appearing, each raised to the **highest** power occurring.

For example, 72 = 2³ × 3² and 90 = 2 × 3² × 5. Common primes are 2 and 3. HCF = 2¹ × 3² = 18. LCM = 2³ × 3² × 5 = 360.

#### Euclidean (long-division) method for HCF
Repeatedly replace the larger number by the remainder: HCF(a, b) = HCF(b, r) until r = 0. The last non-zero remainder is the HCF. This avoids writing out full prime factorisations and works for large values.

#### Worked comparison table
| Situation | Numbers | HCF | LCM |
|---|---|---|---|
| One is multiple of the other | 12, 36 | 12 | 36 |
| Co-prime pair | 8, 9 | 1 | 72 |
| General case | 72, 90 | 18 | 360 |

#### Typical NCEE question patterns
1. Direct computation — "Find the HCF and LCM of 24 and 36."
2. Arrangement — "What is the largest number that divides 84 and 120 exactly?" (HCF use).
3. Synchronisation — "Two bells ring every 6 and 8 seconds; when do they ring together again?" (LCM use).
4. Fill-the-blank — "If HCF(18, x) = 6 and LCM(18, x) = 36, find x." Apply the identity.

#### Scoring tip
> **Trap alert:** HCF × LCM = a × b works only for **two** positive integers, not three or more. For three numbers, compute HCF and LCM individually and verify, never multiply.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge cases and counter-intuitive results
Two numbers can have the **same** HCF and LCM only when the numbers are equal; otherwise HCF ≤ smaller number ≤ larger number ≤ LCM, with equality on each side reached only in the special cases above. The identity HCF × LCM = a × b fails if either number is 0 or negative, so NCEE questions always restrict to positive integers — read the stem carefully.

#### Connections to adjacent topics
HCF and LCM underpin later work in **fractions** (finding common denominators uses LCM), **ratio simplification** (dividing by HCF), and **modular arithmetic** (the LCM governs when cycles realign). Mastery here pays off in algebra and number-theory questions at senior secondary level.

#### Common mistakes examiners exploit
- Giving the LCM when HCF is asked (the classic 2-mark loss).
- Forgetting the highest vs lowest power rule, producing a swapped pair.
- Leaving the answer as a product of primes instead of computing 18 or 360.
- Applying the pair identity HCF × LCM = a × b to three numbers and trusting the result.

#### Worked micro-example
Find HCF and LCM of 48 and 180. Factor: 48 = 2⁴ × 3, 180 = 2² × 3² × 5. HCF = 2² × 3 = 12. LCM = 2⁴ × 3² × 5 = 720. Cross-check: 12 × 720 = 8640 = 48 × 180 ✓.

#### Practice prompts
1. Use the Euclidean algorithm to find HCF(252, 198), then compute LCM from the identity.
2. Three alarms ring at 6, 8, and 12 minutes. After how many minutes will all ring together, and how many rings will each have sounded in that interval?
