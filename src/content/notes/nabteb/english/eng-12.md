# Idioms and Figurative Expressions

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Idioms** are fixed word groups whose meanings cannot be deduced from the individual words — *kick the bucket* means "to die," not to strike a container. **Figurative expressions** (figures of speech) deliberately depart from literal language to convey meaning vividly. The core categories NABTEB tests are: **simile** (comparison using *like/as*), **metaphor** (direct statement that X is Y), **personification** (human traits given to non-humans), **hyperbole** (exaggeration), **irony** (meaning opposite or outcome contrary to expectation), **oxymoron** (two contradictory words joined), **paradox** (self-contradictory truth), **euphemism** (mild substitute for harsh term), **pun** (wordplay on similar sounds), and **proverb** (traditional wisdom saying).

**Exam pointers:** NABTEB English Paper II (Objective) usually sets 2–3 questions from this topic. Master the *like/as* rule for simile vs. metaphor and the "two-word contradiction" rule for oxymoron vs. paradox. Context determines meaning — never interpret in isolation.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Definitions and Core Mechanism
An **idiom** is a conventionalised phrase stored and retrieved as a single unit in memory; native speakers recognise *spill the beans* = "reveal a secret" without analysing each word. A **figurative expression** uses words in a non-literal, comparative, or emotive sense to amplify meaning or paint mental pictures (imagery).

#### The Major Figures of Speech
- **Simile:** explicit comparison using *like* or *as* — *"Her voice rang like a bell."*
- **Metaphor:** implicit identification — *"The world is a stage."*
- **Personification:** *"Opportunity knocked at the door."*
- **Hyperbole:** *"I've waited an eternity."*
- **Irony:** verbal (saying the opposite), situational (expectation vs. outcome), dramatic (audience knows what character does not).
- **Oxymoron:** two contradictory words side by side — *deafening silence, living dead*.
- **Paradox:** a whole statement that contradicts itself yet holds truth — *"The child is father of the man,"* *"Less is more."*
- **Euphemism:** softens harsh reality — *passed away* replaces *died*; *between jobs* replaces *unemployed*.
- **Pun:** humour from homophones or double meaning — *"Time flies like an arrow; fruit flies like a banana."*

#### Idioms vs. Proverbs
Idioms are fixed phrases with idiomatic meaning (*bite the bullet* = endure hardship). **Proverbs** are complete traditional sayings that offer advice or wisdom (*"A stitch in time saves nine"*). Both are culturally bound; literal decoding fails.

#### Typical NABTEB Question Patterns
Candidates should expect (a) choosing the correct figurative label for an underlined expression, (b) selecting the meaning of an idiom in context, and (c) identifying the figure used in a short passage.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Fine Distinctions
**Oxymoron vs. Paradox:** *oxymoron* is a compressed two-word contradiction (*cruel kindness*); *paradox* extends across a clause or sentence (*"I must be cruel to be kind"*). Oxymorons are usually clever compression; paradoxes expose philosophical tension.

**Hyperbole vs. Lie:** hyperbole is knowingly false exaggeration accepted by speaker and audience for emphasis; a lie is deceptive. *"I'm so hungry I could eat a horse"* is hyperbole, not dishonesty.

**Irony vs. Sarcasm:** sarcasm is biting verbal irony intended to mock; situational irony (the fire station burns down) is structural and unintentional. NABTEB typically tests **situational/verbal irony**, not dramatic irony, so focus there.

**Euphemism vs. Understatement:** both soften impact, but euphemism replaces harsh words (*collateral damage* for civilian deaths), whereas understatement minimises scale (*"It was a bit wet"* during a flood).

#### Worked Micro-Examples
*"The silence in the courtroom was deafening."* — **oxymoron** (silence cannot be loud; two contradictory nouns).
*"He is a lion in battle."* — **metaphor** (no *like/as*; direct identity).
*"He fought like a lion."* — **simile** (explicit comparison marker *like*).
*"He kicked the bucket last night."* — **idiom** meaning "died."

#### Common Mistakes
Treating *oxymoron* and *paradox* as interchangeable; reading *kick the bucket* literally; assuming any comparison is a simile — metaphors compare without markers; confusing *euphemism* with *irony*.

#### Practice Prompts
1. Label and explain: *"The wind whispered through the pines."*
2. What does *to hold your horses* mean, and why can't the meaning be guessed word-by-word?
