# Data Interpretation and Clinical Reasoning in PLAB 1

PLAB 1 is 180 single-best-answer questions in a single three-hour examination, and each question opens with a short scenario followed by five answers. That is one minute per question including reading, and the minute is mostly spent reading. The GMC describes the paper as assessing "the knowledge and skills expected of a doctor entering the second year of the UK Foundation Programme", which is why the scenario is a patient rather than a topic — you are being asked what you would do, not what a disease is called.

The scenario text is not decoration. Every clinical detail in it is there either to point at a diagnosis or to be a distractor you must consciously discard. Candidates who read quickly to reach the question get the distractors wrong, because they were never identified as distractors.

## Reading a scenario in order

Start at the sentence that gives the problem. Age, sex, onset, duration, severity and the key modifier — "on exertion", "at night", "after eating", "sudden" — carry most of the diagnostic weight. Then read what the scenario deliberately withholds: no fever, no weight loss, no rash, no travel history. The absence of a feature is evidence, and in a five-option question it is usually what separates the fourth-best answer from the correct one.

Then find the clinical detail that does not fit every option. "Blood pressure 96/58 despite fluids" tells you something the age and the complaint do not. Isolating that single line is faster than reading forward three times.

Finally, before you read the options, say what you think is going on. Thirty seconds spent forming a hypothesis shortens the option-reading, because you then know which option you are looking for and which you are looking for reasons to reject.

## The four presentations that recur

Chest pain, breathlessness, abdominal pain and altered consciousness between them appear in a large share of any safe-practice paper, and they are worth learning as decision trees rather than as lists of causes. For each, know the two or three you cannot miss, the one feature that excludes each of them, and the first investigation and first management step.

That framing matters more than it looks. A question asking for the "most appropriate next step" is not asking for the diagnosis; it is asking whether you would investigate further, treat immediately, refer, or admit. Candidates who jump straight to naming a diagnosis frequently pick the option that is diagnostically impressive and clinically unsafe — the rare disease over the common one, or the drug that treats the complication instead of the cause.

## Results, charts and films

A question that supplies a blood gas, a full blood count, a renal profile or a drug chart is testing three things: can you read the value, do you know the reference range, and do you know what the abnormality means for the patient in front of you.

The commonest trap is a value that is technically abnormal and clinically irrelevant for that patient — a mildly low haemoglobin in someone with an alternative explanation, a borderline creatinine in a patient who is otherwise well. The reverse trap is more dangerous: a value that is unremarkable in isolation and serious in context, such as a normal potassium in a patient with Addisonian features.

## Common mistakes

Answering with the most likely diagnosis when the question asked for the next management step. Read the verb. "Most appropriate next step", "most likely cause", "best initial investigation" and "which finding would distinguish" want different answers, and picking the diagnosis for a management question is the single highest-frequency error in this paper.

Selecting the rarest option because it sounds more clinical. PLAB 1 tests safe practice at the Foundation level, not differential diagnosis at consultant level.

Ignoring a deliberately stated negative. It is in the scenario because it excludes something.

Reading the stem twice and the options once. Options are where a stem is misread, so reverse that.

Spending more than a minute on one question. With 180 items, three over-runs cost you six items you could have answered in the time.

Carrying a home-country practice assumption into a UK answer. Guidance thresholds and referral routes differ; where the GMC gives a figure, use it.
