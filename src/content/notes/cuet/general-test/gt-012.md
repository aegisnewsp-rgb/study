# Analytical Reasoning

Analytical Reasoning is the part of the General Test where you are given a *set* of facts and asked to work out what must be true. The difference from straightforward logical reasoning is that the facts are numerous, they interact, and the answer depends on combining them rather than applying a single rule. A syllogism, a seating arrangement, a ranking with conditions, a set of constraints — each looks easy in isolation, and each becomes error-prone the moment a second constraint is added.

The reason marks are lost is almost always the same: a constraint is read slightly differently than it was written, or a conclusion is accepted because it is *possible* when the question asked for *necessary*. Both are discipline failures, not intelligence failures, and both are fixed by a strict method.

**Represent → combine → separate what is necessary from what is possible.** Draw the structure the facts describe, add each new condition to the drawing rather than to your memory, and then read the answer type off the question before you look at the options. Analytical reasoning is a bookkeeping problem before it is a thinking problem.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

**The five analytical question families in CUET UG**

| Family | What you are given | Tool required |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Syllogism** | Two or three universal statements | Venn boxes |
| **Conditional statements** | Several "if / only if / unless" clauses | Direction arrows |
| **Ranking** | Positions or ages with two-sided information | Number line + position formula |
| **Seating arrangement** | People, seats, facing direction, conditions | Grid or ring sketch |
| **Venn / overlapping sets** | Membership of people in groups | Region shading |

**Syllogism: the three statements and what each permits**

- **All A are B.** Draw A entirely inside B. The arrow is A → B.
- **Some A are B.** Draw a small region of A inside B. This permits *no* conclusion about the whole of A.
- **No A is B.** Draw A and B disjoint, not touching.
- **Some A are not B.** Draw a region of A outside B.

Then the three conclusion types:

- **Definitely follows** — true in every picture the statements allow.
- **Possibly follows** — true in at least one allowed picture, and not contradicted.
- **Does not follow** — false in at least one allowed picture.

The two rules that decide most items: *no conclusion about the whole of A can be drawn from "some A are B"*, and *a conclusion about the intersection of two sets you have already separated is impossible*.

**Conditional statements: the arrow discipline**

Draw an arrow from the condition to the consequence. Then read every statement in the same direction.

- "A implies B" → A → B.
- "A only if B" → A → B (B is necessary for A).
- "A if B" → B → A (B is sufficient for A).
- "A unless B" → if B is false, then A is true, i.e. ¬B → A.
- "Neither A nor B" → ¬A ∧ ¬B.
- "Either A or B" → at least one holds; it does not say exactly one.
- "All A are B, and some B are C" → nothing follows about A and C unless another link exists.

Always draw the arrow in the *causal* direction and never infer backwards from an arrow you drew. A question testing this will offer you the reverse implication as the attractive option.

**Ranking and positions**

- Position from one end = **(rank₁ + rank₂) − (n + 1)**.
- Position from the left = **n + 1 − position from the right**.
- Always check three things: the result is a whole number, it lies between 1 and n, and no two people share a position.
- When three rankings interlock (class, roll number, marks), convert everything to a single number line and place each person once.

**Seating arrangement: the setup that prevents most errors**

- **Linear row:** fix "left to right" once. Slots 1, 2, 3, …
- **Circular:** fix a start and a direction — say, clockwise — and never change them. There is no first seat; pick one arbitrarily and rotate at the end if the question demands a particular person at a particular position.
- **Facing inwards vs outwards** changes the meaning of left and right. Never mix; decide at the start and annotate the sketch.
- **"Immediate left"** means the very next seat. **"Second to the left"** is a genuine offset of two. They are not interchangeable.
- **Do not assume symmetry.** A condition placing one person never determines where a second person is unless the arrangement is stated to be symmetric.

**Age problems**

Oldest is the largest number; the phrase "A is older than B" becomes A > B. A quick loop: convert everything to "X is the *n*th from the top/bottom", then use the position formula. And the direction trap is constant — "the youngest is third from the left" means a *rank from one end*, not a position in the other.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

**The Venn method in full**

Draw one rectangle per term. Add each statement as a *region*, not as a whole:

| Statement | What you draw |
| --- | --- |
| All A are B | A completely inside B |
| Some A are B | a lens where A and B overlap, both still having parts outside |
| No A is B | the two rectangles disjoint, with a visible gap |
| Some A are not B | a region of A sitting outside B |
| All A are B and all B are C | A inside B inside C, transitively |

The reading rule that makes this work: after drawing, ask what has been **forced**. Anything the drawing does not force is at best a possibility, whatever the option says.

*Worked example.* Statements: "All pens are tools. All tools are objects." Conclusion: "All pens are objects." The drawing puts pen inside tool inside object, so the pen region is entirely within the object region — the conclusion **definitely follows**, by transitivity. Conclusion: "Some objects are not pens." The drawing does not force this, because it depends on whether the object rectangle has anything outside the pen region; it **may** follow but does not definitely follow. Conclusion: "Some tools are not pens." This is **definitely false** in the drawn picture, because the pen region covers all of the pen set, and the tool rectangle may be exactly the pens. The "does not follow" and "must be false" categories are different, and examiners use the difference.

**A three-step rule for testing any candidate conclusion**

- **Step 1 — Could it be false?** Try to draw a picture where the statements are true and the conclusion is false. If you succeed, the conclusion does not definitely follow. This is the counter-example test, and it is the single most powerful tool in this topic.
- **Step 2 — Is it contradicted outright?** If the conclusion claims something the drawing makes impossible — for instance that two sets you were told are disjoint share members — it is false, not merely unproven.
- **Step 3 — Does it introduce a new word?** A conclusion mentioning a category, name or attribute that no statement mentioned cannot be a necessary consequence. It may be a possibility, never a certainty.

**Multi-constraint problems: the intersection technique**

When a question gives you four conditions and four options, do not read them all at once. Build a table of candidates and strike them out one at a time — this is a filtering method, not a memory method.

*Worked example.* "Six people P, Q, R, S, T, U sit in a row. P is at an extreme end. Q sits second to the right of P. R is not adjacent to P. S is exactly in the middle. T sits third to the left of S. U sits second to the right of S." Start by placing the certain things: S in slot 4 of 6, U in slot 6, and T third to the left of S in slot 1. P is at an extreme end, and slot 1 is taken, so P is at slot 6 — but slot 6 is U, a contradiction. So P must be at an extreme end that is still free; the only free extreme is slot 6, which conflicts, so recheck T: third to the left of slot 4 is slot 1, which is an extreme end, so if P is at an extreme end it must be slot 6, conflicting with U. This is exactly the situation in which candidates go blank — the resolution is that you placed U wrong: second to the *right* of S in a left-to-right numbering is slot 5, not slot 6. With U at 5, T at 1, P at 6, Q second to the right of P is impossible, so P is at slot 1, which is T's slot — again a conflict, which tells you the constraint set forces the alternative reading. The point of walking this through is not the particular answer; it is that **striking candidates out one at a time always resolves it**, while trying to hold the whole arrangement in your head never does.

**The order of operations that saves time on every arrangement**

- **Place the fixed things first.** "Exactly in the middle", "at an extreme end", "immediate left of" — any of these fixes a slot or fixes a relationship to a fixed slot.
- **Then place the chained things.** Any person whose position is defined relative to someone already placed goes next.
- **Then place the rest by elimination,** trying each remaining slot against the remaining conditions.
- **Only then consider the facing direction,** which is usually the last thing the question asks about.

Doing it in a different order means re-drawing every time a new person lands, and that is where errors enter.

**Syllogism with three statements: the chained and the independent cases**

Three-statement syllogisms come in two shapes, and recognising which you have saves most of the work.

**Chained:** the conclusion links the two ends of a chain. "All A are B. All B are C. Therefore all A are C" — the diagram is a nest and the conclusion is forced.

**Independent:** the two statements touch different sets and the conclusion links them anyway. "All A are B. All C are B. Therefore all A are C" — this does *not* follow, because B could contain A and C as separate islands. The moment the middle set is shared but not used transitively, the conclusion fails. This "shared middle" error is the single most common lost mark in three-statement syllogisms, and the fix is one sentence: *the middle set is common, not connected.*

**Conclusions that are always suspect**

- A conclusion about *all* of a set when only *some* was given.
- A conclusion of the form "no A is C" when A and C were never declared disjoint and one is not contained in the other.
- A conclusion asserting existence — "some A are B" — when nothing in the statements guarantees both sets have a common member.
- A conclusion about a "third" category, such as "all pens are metal", when the statements only ever relate pens to tools.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

**Mixed-family problems: the five-step general algorithm**

Harder items combine families. When they do, the algorithm is fixed and the order matters more than the difficulty.

- **Step 1 — Transcribe every condition as a relation in one notation.** Use *x = n* for a fixed position, *y = x + 2* for an offset, *x → y* for a conditional, *A ⊂ B* for a set relation. If you cannot write a condition in this notation, you have not understood it yet.
- **Step 2 — Sort the conditions into *fixed*, *chained* and *free*.**
- **Step 3 — Place the fixed conditions first, then the chained ones, resolving the free ones by elimination.**
- **Step 4 — Check every condition against the completed arrangement,** including conditions you used and ones you thought were redundant.
- **Step 5 — Only now answer the question,** and confirm it asks about a *necessary* truth rather than a possible one.

The reason this is worth internalising: a mixed problem is not harder because it requires more thought, it is harder because it requires *more bookkeeping*. The algorithm supplies the bookkeeping; your attention goes to the one step that actually needs reasoning.

**Negation, and the questions that hinge on it**

"Which of the following is not true" is a trap-laden variant, and the safe technique is to positively evaluate all four options and choose the one that fails. Doing it by intuition inverts your logic at exactly the moment you are least able to check it.

Negation also has grammar worth knowing, because the subject is general reasoning and the pattern is recursive:

- "All A are B" negated is "Some A are not B".
- "Some A are B" negated is "All A are not B" (more precisely, "No A is B").
- "No A is B" negated is "Some A is B".
- "A is not B" negated is "A is B".

Notice that a universal statement negates into an *existential* one, and an existential negates into a universal. That asymmetry is why "which does not follow" questions about "all" statements so often produce a "some" answer.

**Counting questions: the three-way Venn**

When a question asks how many are in A but not B, or in both A and B but not C, count **regions**, not sets. Build a full three-circle Venn and label every region: A only, B only, C only, A∩B only, A∩C only, B∩C only, all three, and none. Every counting answer is then a sum of region labels, and the "only" conditions tell you which regions to include.

The two rules that catch most errors: *"only A"* means outside every other set, and *"A or B"* is inclusive unless the question says "but not both". An inclusive or sums both regions and the overlap; an exclusive or sums the two regions and subtracts the overlap. Reading "or" correctly is worth marks in every counting variant.

**Assumption questions: the "negative test" method**

"Where does a conclusion deviate from the statements?" — the standard phrasing for assumption-based items — asks you to find the assumption that, if false, would make the conclusion collapse. The method: take the conclusion, state what must be true for it to work, and that is the assumption.

*Worked example.* Statement: "The city has increased bus frequency, yet complaints about waiting have not fallen. Therefore bus frequency is not the cause of long waits." The conclusion works only if the two are causally related at all. The assumption being tested is that **more buses would reduce waiting time**. If that were false, the conclusion would have no support, so it is the load-bearing assumption. A weaker assumption such as "the city cares about waiting time" does not support the conclusion and is rejected. The pattern is always the same: find the minimum assumption without which the argument fails.

**Worst-case and best-case questions**

These ask what *must* be true or what *could* be true, and they reward building the arrangement rather than reasoning about it.

- **"Which must be true?"** is a necessity test. Build one legal arrangement. If your arrangement makes the option false, the option is eliminated. If the option is true in your arrangement, it is *possible*, not necessary, and you must build a second arrangement to eliminate it. Two arrangements distinguish "possible" from "necessary"; one never does.
- **"Which could be true?"** needs only one legal arrangement in which the option holds — but it must be legal in *every* respect, so check all conditions, not just the ones the option mentions.

The habit to build: whenever a question says *must*, plan for two arrangements. That single adjustment converts a large number of "must" questions from coin flips into solved items.

**Timing and the paper-level strategy for this section**

Analytical items are not uniformly expensive. A single-set syllogism takes about a minute; a five-condition seating problem can take four. Sort by *constraint count*: one-statement items first, two-statement next, three-plus last. Then set a hard ceiling per item — two minutes for a set item, three for a mixed one — and move on when you hit it. A blank left behind for the last five minutes of the paper is usually recovered; a lost pattern run is not.

Two habits that measurably reduce errors:

- **Re-read the stem before you commit.** Most wrong answers in this section answer a nearby question — the number of people who sit *next to* rather than *between*, or the third from the right rather than the left.
- **Verify with the unused condition.** If a question gave four conditions and you only used three, one of them is doing work you have not seen, and the answer you chose is probably wrong. Using every condition is a strong self-check.

### 🧠 Memory Anchors

```mermaid
flowchart TD
    A[Analytical reasoning item appears] --> B[Read the ASK first: definitely / possibly / does not follow]
    B --> C[Transcribe every condition in one notation: fixed, offset, arrow, set relation]
    C --> D[Sort: fixed conditions, chained conditions, free ones]
    D --> E[Syllogism or set question?]
    E -->|Yes| F[Draw Venn regions, not whole circles]
    F --> G{Counter-example test: can the conclusion be false?}
    G -->|Yes| H[Does not definitely follow]
    G -->|No and never possible| I[Definitely follows]
    E -->|No| J[Arrangement question: place fixed slots first, then chained, then free by elimination]
    J --> K[Build a second arrangement if the ask is "must be true"]
    K --> L[Check every condition, including the one you did not use]
    H --> M[Answer]
    I --> M
    L --> M
```

- **"Read the ask before the options."** "Definitely", "possibly" and "does not follow" are three different questions and the same option can be right for one and wrong for the others.
- **"Draw regions, not circles."** "Some A are B" is a small overlap, not a whole circle inside another. The distinction is the whole of syllogism.
- **"A shared middle is not a connection."** "All A are B, all C are B" licenses nothing about A and C.
- **"Arrows point forward only."** Never infer the reverse of a conditional you drew.
- **"Two arrangements distinguish must from may."** One legal arrangement proves possibility, never necessity.
- **"If a condition is unused, your answer is wrong."** A check that costs ten seconds.
- **"Or is inclusive unless stated."** "But not both" is the only thing that makes it exclusive.
- **Flashcard Q&A:**
  - *All A are B, all B are C. Does all A are C follow?* → Yes, transitively; the diagram nests.
  - *All A are B, all C are B. Does all A are C follow?* → No; the shared middle does not connect the ends.
  - *Some A are B. Can you conclude all A are B?* → No; "some" gives no direction at all.
  - *Row of 40, ranks 15 from left and 20 from right — same seat?* → 15 + 20 = 35 vs n + 1 = 41, so 15th from the left is the 26th from the left, meaning the two are 11 apart. Check with the formula: position = 15 + 20 − 41 = −6, which is impossible, so re-read the statement; a negative result tells you the reading was wrong.
  - *In a circle, how many "lefts" return you to start?* → Depends on the number of seats, which is exactly why you must fix a start seat and count.
  - *Negation of "all A are B"?* → "Some A are not B" — a universal negates into an existential.

### 🎯 Exam Traps & Error Log

1. **Drawing a whole circle for "some A are B".** A set that is entirely inside another is "all", not "some". One of these two misdrawings causes most syllogism errors.
2. **Inferring the reverse of a conditional.** "A implies B" says nothing about B implying A, and the reverse-implication option is deliberately attractive.
3. **Confusing a universal with an existential in the answer.** "Definitely follows" options and "possibly follows" options are drawn from the same statement set and test the same picture.
4. **Ignoring the word "only" in a counting question.** "Only A" excludes every other set, and the inclusive sum is wrong.
5. **Assuming a seating arrangement is symmetric.** One condition about A and B says nothing about C, and symmetrical answers are the trap.
6. **Using only some of the conditions.** If a spare condition remains, the answer you built is not the arrangement the question describes.
7. **Failing to check that a rank result is a whole number in range.** A negative or fractional result means the positional phrase was misread, not that the formula is wrong.
8. **Answering "must" with a single arrangement.** One arrangement proves possibility. Necessity needs a second.
9. **Confusing path length with displacement in a multi-step arrangement.** Count the slots you moved through, not the ones you passed.
10. **Letting a "not true" question flip your reading.** Evaluate all four options positively and pick the one that fails, rather than trying to see which is false by feel.

### 🧪 Self-Test — 8 Questions with Worked Answers

Answer all eight on paper first. Read the ask before the options in every case.

1. **"Statements: All A are B. Some B are C. Which conclusion definitely follows? (I) Some A are C. (II) No A is C. (III) None of these."**
   *Worked answer:* **(III) None of these.** Method — draw the regions. A is entirely inside B, and a region of B overlaps C, but nothing connects that region to A. Conclusion I fails because the C-members could all be outside the A region. Conclusion II fails because there is no separation between A and C at all. So neither definitely follows and the answer is "none of these". The trap is I, which students pick because "some B are C" and "all A are B" sound like they join up.

2. **"In a row of 45 students, Kavya is 16th from the left and is 30th from the right. What is her position from the left?"**
   *Worked answer:* **16.** Method — apply the formula. 16 + 30 = 46, and 46 − (45 + 1) = 0, which is impossible, so the two ranks cannot belong to the same person. Recognising that a result of zero means "these two ranks are *not* the same seat" is the point. If a question says 16th from the left and 30th from the right of the *same* person in a row of 45, the second rank should be 45 − 16 + 1 = 30 — which it is. So she is 16th from the left and 30th from the right, and the answer to "position from the left" is simply **16**. Always do the consistency check: the sum of the two ranks must equal n + 1 for the same person.

3. **"Eight people A to H sit around a circular table facing the centre. A sits third to the left of B. C sits second to the right of A. Where does C sit relative to B?"**
   *Worked answer:* **Immediately to the left of B.** Method — fix a direction before placing anyone. Count clockwise as "right" for people facing the centre; then A is third to the left of B, which is three seats counter-clockwise from B. With B at seat 1, the counter-clockwise seats are 8, 7, 6, so A is at seat 6. C is second to the right of A, which is two seats clockwise: seat 6 → 7 → 8, so C is at seat 8. Seat 8 is immediately counter-clockwise from seat 1, i.e. **immediately to the left of B**. The trap is mixing clockwise and counter-clockwise, which reverses every position and gives a plausible but wrong answer.

4. **"Statements: P only if Q. R only if P. Which must be true if R is true? (a) Q is true. (b) Q may be false. (c) P is false. (d) None."**
   *Worked answer:* **(a) Q is true.** Method — draw the arrows in the causal direction. "P only if Q" is P → Q. "R only if P" is R → P. So the chain is R → P → Q, and if R is true then P is true and then Q is true. Chaining the arrows is the entire solution. Note that the reverse is not available: Q being true does not let you conclude P is true, which is why (b) is wrong and why the "only if" wording is the whole point of the question.

5. **"In a class of 40, Sita is 12th from the top and Meera is 31st from the bottom. How many students sit between them? (Also: Sita is shorter than Meera.)"**
   *Worked answer:* **3.** Method — put everything on one number line. Counting from the top: Sita is 12th, so Meera's rank from the top is 40 − 31 + 1 = 10. Sita is 12th and Meera is 10th from the same end, so Meera stands one place above Sita and there are **zero** students between them — and since Meera is ranked higher and taller than Sita, the rank order is consistent. The valuable part is the consistency check: had the question said Sita is taller than Meera, the two pieces of data would contradict and you would know a phrase had been misread. Never apply a formula without checking that the other facts agree with it.

6. **"Statements: No A is B. No B is C. Which conclusion follows? (I) No A is C. (II) Some A are C. (III) No conclusion about A and C."**
   *Worked answer:* **(I) No A is C.** Method — this is a chained disjointness. B is disjoint from A, and B is disjoint from C. A and C are therefore on the same side, away from B, and the picture forces them apart as well. The reasoning is transitivity of separation. Note the tempting wrong answer (III), which assumes that because the statements never mention A and C together, nothing follows — but the whole point of the B conditions is to tie them together. Set relationships propagate through a shared set even when the shared set is empty of overlap.

7. **"Five girls sit in a row. P and Q are adjacent. R sits at an extreme end. S sits third from the left. T is between P and Q. Who cannot be at an extreme end?"**
   *Worked answer:* **R cannot be at the right end, and T cannot be at either end.** Method — apply the fixed positions first. S occupies slot 3. R is at an extreme end, so slot 1 or slot 5. T sits *between* P and Q, so T is not at either extreme end by definition. P and Q are adjacent to each other, and T is between them, which requires three consecutive seats occupied by P, T, Q or Q, T, P. The only triple of consecutive seats avoiding slot 3's occupant pattern is slots 3-4-5 or 1-2-3, and S at slot 3 rules one of them out. The reasoning that matters for the paper: the word **"between"** itself already guarantees T is not at an end, so the answer follows from a single condition regardless of the rest. Read the question's own words before doing any arranging.

8. **"Conclusions: (I) All literate people are educated. (II) Some educated people are literate. (III) No educated person is illiterate. Which follows from: 'All literate people are educated'? (a) only I (b) only II (c) I and II (d) all three."**
   *Worked answer:* **(a) only I.** Method — one statement, one drawing: the literate set sits entirely inside the educated set. Conclusion I is exactly the statement, so it follows. Conclusion II says *some* educated people are literate; the drawing does not force the literate set to be non-empty, so it may follow but does not definitely. Conclusion III is about *illiterate* people, a term no statement introduces, so it cannot be a necessary conclusion — it is at most possible. Testing each conclusion against the same single picture, and marking which are forced, which are possible and which introduce a new term, is the whole method.

### 💡 Pro Tips

1. **Read the ask word first: definitely, possibly, or does not follow.** The same statement set produces options from all three categories, and the category is the mark.
2. **Draw regions inside circles, never whole circles inside circles,** unless the statement literally says *all*.
3. **Use the counter-example test before anything else.** Try to make the conclusion false. If you can, stop.
4. **Place fixed conditions first, chained conditions second, free ones last.** Every re-draw is an opportunity to make an error.
5. **Check your answer against every condition, and treat an unused condition as a red flag.**
6. **Build a second arrangement whenever the question says "must".** One arrangement cannot prove a necessity.
7. **Evaluate "not true" questions by confirming all four options positively** and picking the one that fails.
8. **Set a two-minute ceiling and move on.** A skipped analytical item at minute 55 is recoverable; a lost pattern run is not.
