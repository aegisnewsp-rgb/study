# Rise of British Power and the Rebellion of 1857

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> Quick recall for the night before Prelims — causes on one line, leaders on the next.

British dominance crystallised at **Plassey (1757)** and **Buxar (1764)**, then expanded through **Subsidiary Alliance** (Wellesley) and **Doctrine of Lapse** (Dalhousie). The cartridge greased with cow and pig fat tipped a seething army into open mutiny at Meerut on **10 May 1857**, spreading to Delhi, Lucknow, Kanpur, Jhansi and Bihar within weeks.

| Layer of Cause | Specific Trigger (must-memorise) |
|---|---|
| Political | Annexation of Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur, Awadh (1856) — broke treaty obligations |
| Military | Enfield rifle cartridges (Feb 1857); General Service Enlistment Act 1856; racial pay gap |
| Economic | Drain of Wealth; ruin of handloom weavers after Industrial Revolution imports |
| Religious | Sati ban (1829), Widow Remarriage Act (1856), missionary activity, racial insults |
| Spark | Mangal Pandey at Barrackpore, 29 March 1857 |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook — "P-M-M-R-S"** for causes: **P**olitical, **M**ilitary, **M**onetary (economic), **R**eligious-Social, **S**park. Mnemonic survives a 60-second recall drill.

Leaders and centres pair as: **Delhi–Zafar, Lucknow–Begum Hazrat Mahal, Kanpur–Nana Saheb/Tantia Tope, Jhansi–Lakshmibai, Bihar–Kunwar Singh, Faizabad–Maulvi Ahmadullah**. **Result:** Company rule ended, **Government of India Act 1858**, **Queen Victoria's Proclamation (1 Nov 1858)**, Doctrine of Lapse buried.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

> Working depth for Mains — mechanism of conquest, why the revolt exploded, why it failed.

#### Consolidation of British Power, 1757–1856

The East India Company began as a trading body chartered in 1600, but three battlefield verdicts reshaped it into a territorial sovereign. Robert Clive's victory at **Plassey (23 June 1757)** over Siraj-ud-Daulah installed a puppet at Murshidabad; **Buxar (22 October 1764)** broke the combined forces of Shuja-ud-Daulah, Shah Alam II and Mir Qasim, yielding the **Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa (1765)** — tax collection rights that converted commerce into government.

Two policy engines then annexed or coerced the remaining 560-odd princely states. **Subsidiary Alliance** (Lord Wellesley, 1798–1805) bound a state to the Company by paying for its defence, surrendering foreign policy, and stationing British troops — Avadh, Hyderabad, Mysore and the Marathas were all drawn in, mostly at ruinous cost to their treasuries. **Doctrine of Lapse** (Dalhousie, 1848–1856) refused to recognise adopted heirs unless the prior ruler had obtained Company sanction, swallowing Satara (1848), Jaitpur, Sambalpur, Baghat, Udaipur, Jhansi (1853), Nagpur (1854) and Awadh (1856). The doctrine explicitly *targeted* states without natural heirs — almost every annexation generated an aggrieved claimant.

#### Why 1857 Exploded — The Multi-Causal Stack

Examiners reward a stacked answer, not a single cause. The cartridge controversy was the **spark**, not the fuel. Fuel had accumulated over decades:

- **Political**: annexation of Awadh (declared misgovernance, not lapse) in 1856 alienated a loyal aristocracy overnight; Nana Saheb (adopted son of Baji Rao II) and Rani Lakshmibai (adopted son Damodar Rao denied) became rallying figures.
- **Military**: the **General Service Enlistment Act (1856)** required sepoys to serve overseas — violating caste-bound service rules. Pay ratios were roughly 1:7 between Indian and British soldiers of equal rank, and the **Indian Army** was disproportionately drawn from upper-caste Hindus of Awadh and Bihar, precisely the regions that exploded.
- **Economic**: Dadabhai Naoroji's **Drain of Wealth** (formalised later in *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India*, 1876) quantifies the unidirectional transfer — Indian revenue funded British wars, pensions and imports. Handloom exports collapsed after 1813; rural indebtedness soared.
- **Religious-Social**: **Sati ban (1829)**, **Widow Remarriage Act (1856)**, the **Religious Disabilities Act (1850)**, missionary activity, and the **Affirmation Act (1850)** allowing converts to inherit Hindu ancestral property — these were read as forced Christianisation.

#### Comparison Matrix — Causes vs. Common Misreadings

| Misreading | Correction Examiners Test |
|---|---|
| "Greased cartridge was the cause" | It was the spark; the four causes above had been building for a century |
| "Doctrine of Lapse caused 1857" | Doctrine operated 1848–1856; it was *halted* after 1857, not the trigger of the revolt |
| "It was a planned national war" | NCERT treats it as a revolt with disconnected local uprisings; no unified command, no common political goal beyond restoring old regimes |
| "Bahadur Shah Zafar led India" | He was declared Emperor by sepoys at Delhi but exercised no authority outside the walled city |
| "It ended Company rule because of sepoy failure" | Company rule ended primarily because the revolt proved *political* bankruptcy — the British elite concluded direct Crown administration was safer than commercial misrule |

#### Worked Resolution — A Typical Mains Question

*"Critically examine the political causes of the Rebellion of 1857."*

A high-mark answer opens by separating the **spark** (cartridge, Mangal Pandey, Meerut) from the **causes**, then ranks them. Begin with **Awadh (1856)**, because it alone explains *why sepoys mutinied together* — Awadh's annexation displaced the largest single block of sepoy families, court nobles, and talukdars without consultation. Follow with the Doctrine of Lapse, naming the annexed states and showing that Dalhousie's annexations erased **titles, pensions and court patronage** in one stroke. Then the subsidiary alliance's long tail: Hyderabad, Mysore, Maratha states had been financially hollowed for decades before 1857, creating a ready pool of dispossessed princes. Conclude by stating that British misreading — treating the revolt as sepoy grievance alone — is precisely what delayed the discovery that **landowners, peasants and civilian populations** joined the mutiny in Bundelkhand, Bihar and Rohilkhand.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Candidates who lead with the greased cartridge lose the first half-mark, because the question asks for *political* causes. The cartridge belongs in an answer on military or immediate causes. Pairing the cartridge with Awadh's annexation — rather than placing it first — scores higher.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

> Mains-grade depth — historiography, suppression chronology, policy reversal, and the links to later nationalism.

#### Nature Debate — Historiographical Triangle

Three readings dominate Indian historiography, and RPSC RAS Mains rewards knowing which view underwrites which claim:

| Historian | Tag for 1857 | Argument Anchor |
|---|---|---|
| V.D. Savarkar (1909) | First War of Independence | United anti-British national sentiment across regions |
| S.N. Sen | Sepoy Mutiny | Military grievance, no common political programme |
| R.C. Majumdar | A feudal reaction | Local risings stitched together by chance, not design |

NCERT adopts the S.N. Sen–R.C. Majumdar position, but asks students to *engage* with Savarkar. The safe Mains formula is: state Savarkar's claim, cite the counter-evidence (no centralised command, separate treaties of surrender, divergent aims), then conclude that the 1857 uprising was the **largest pre-1947 anti-colonial eruption** even if it lacked ideological coherence.

#### Suppression Chronology and Field Commanders

Speed of suppression matters because it conditioned British reforms. **Sir Hugh Wheeler** was besieged at Kanpur and capitulated in June 1857 (the Bibighar massacre followed). **Henry Lawrence** died defending the Lucknow Residency (July 1857); his besieged garrison was relieved first by Henry Havelock and James Neill, then fully by **Sir Colin Campbell** in November 1857. **Sir Hugh Rose** took Jhansi (April 1858) and then Gwalior. **Tantia Tope** evaded capture until **April 1859** — his long flight through Bundelkhand into Rajasthan and into the forests of central India kept rebellion alive for nearly two years after Delhi, Lucknow and Kanpur had fallen. **Bahadur Shah Zafar** was captured at Humayun's Tomb (September 1857), tried in the **Delhi Durbar case (Red Fort, January–March 1858)**, exiled to **Rangoon** where he died in 1862 — formal extinction of the Mughal dynasty.

#### Aftermath — From Company to Crown

Three instruments ended Company rule: the **Government of India Act 1858** (passed by Parliament, 2 August 1858); **Queen Victoria's Proclamation** issued at Allahabad on **1 November 1858**; and the creation of the office of **Viceroy** (earlier title Governor-General became Viceroy after 1858) as the Crown's direct representative. Four concrete reversals followed: the **Doctrine of Lapse was explicitly disavowed**; adopted heirs were recognised; the policy of **non-interference in Indian social customs** was announced (a U-turn from 1829–1856 reformism); and a **general amnesty** was offered except to those "directly concerned in the murder of British subjects". Indian revenues no longer guaranteed Company dividends — a quiet end to the shareholder-state model that had been British India since Plassey.

#### Adjacent-Topic Links and Edge Cases

- **Causes of Indian Nationalism (1885–1905)**: 1857 seeded both the Congress claim to anti-colonial legitimacy *and* the British habit of communal triage.
- **Causes of Deindustrialisation**: the same drain-of-wealth mechanism that funded British railways is the root for handloom collapse — examiners link the two notes.
- **Awadh as a micro-case**: the annexation of Awadh explains *both* sepoy mutiny and the later **talukdari movement**; this is a frequent cross-topic Mains question.
- **Edge case 1**: Jhansi was *not* annexed under Doctrine of Lapse for lack of an heir — Rani Lakshmibai had adopted Damodar Rao; Dalhousie refused recognition. Many state-level examinations mark this distinction.
- **Edge case 2**: Begum Hazrat Mahal proclaimed **Birjis Qadr** as ruler of Lucknow — a rare case of a female rebel regent declaring a male heir, which surfaces in gender-history optional questions.

#### Two Advanced Practice Prompts

1. *"Did the British create the conditions for 1857, or did the conditions exist independently?"* — Force a ranking: subsidiary alliance (financial ruin), doctrine of lapse (political dispossession), and Awadh (sepoy alienation) together explain the geography of revolt; outside Bengal and Madras, the rebellion broke out exactly where British intervention had been deepest.
2. *"Was 1858 a reform or a reconquest?"* — Pair the disavowal of lapse with the retention of the army, the revenue system and the Princely States under paramountcy; argue that **structure** was preserved while **personnel** changed.
