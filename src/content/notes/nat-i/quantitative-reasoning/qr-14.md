# Probability and Permutations

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Probability measures how likely an event is, ranging from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). Permutations count **ordered** arrangements, combinations count **unordered** selections, and both depend on factorials (n!). The NAT-I Quantitative section in standard papers tests 1–2 questions from this area, in most keys as MCQs on letter arrangements, committee selection, or simple dice/card probability.

- **Classical probability:** P(E) = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes.
- **Permutation (order matters):** nPr = n! / (n − r)!.
- **Combination (order ignored):** nCr = n! / [r! (n − r)!].
- **Complement trick:** P(at least one) = 1 − P(none).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Probability Rules

The **sample space** is the set of all equally likely outcomes, and an **event** is any subset of it. For independent events A and B, the joint probability is the product P(A)·P(B). For the union, you must subtract the overlap: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). When events cannot occur together (e.g., rolling a 3 and a 5 on one die), they are **mutually exclusive** and the intersection term drops.

#### Permutation vs Combination

A permutation respects order, so "AB" and "BA" count as two outcomes; a combination does not, so they count once. Passwords, rankings, and seating plans use permutations; committees, lottery picks, and hand-of-cards use combinations. Identical objects require division by their factorials, e.g., arrangements of the letters of "MISSISSIPPI" use the divisor 4!·4!·2!·1!.

#### Conditional Probability

When one event influences another, use P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B). The **multiplication rule** generalises to P(A and B) = P(A)·P(B|A), which feeds directly into Bayes' theorem.

| Concept | Formula | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Classical probability | P(E) = favourable / total | Equally likely outcomes |
| Independent events | P(A and B) = P(A)·P(B) | Coin tosses, separate dice |
| Mutually exclusive union | P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) | Disjoint outcomes |
| General union | P(A or B) = P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B) | Overlapping outcomes |
| Complement | P(not A) = 1 − P(A) | "At least one" problems |

#### Typical NAT-I Pattern

Expect one MCQ asking for the probability of drawing a specific card/colour, and one on arranging letters or forming numbers from given digits. Answer choices in most keys differ by a factor of 2 or 6, reflecting common order-matters mistakes.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Traps

**Circular permutations** count distinct seatings around a round table as (n−1)!, because rotating everyone by one seat produces the same arrangement. **Repetition allowed** (e.g., a 4-digit PIN from digits 0–9) uses 10⁴, not nPr. For words with repeated letters, divide n! by the factorial of each repeat block.

**Common NTS traps** include assuming independence where the sample size shrinks (drawing without replacement), forgetting that "at least one" almost always demands the complement rule, and double-counting when the question says "or" between overlapping categories.

#### Worked Micro-Example

A bag holds 5 red and 3 blue balls. Two balls are drawn without replacement. Find the probability both are red.

- P(1st red) = 5/8.
- P(2nd red | 1st red) = 4/7.
- P(both red) = (5/8) × (4/7) = 20/56 = 5/14.

A committee of 3 is to be formed from 6 men and 4 women such that it contains at least 2 women. Total valid = C(4,2)·C(6,1) + C(4,3)·C(6,0) = 36 + 4 = 40; total ways = C(10,3) = 120; required probability = 40/120 = 1/3.

#### Practice Prompts

1. How many 5-letter arrangements can be formed from the letters of "BANANA" where no two vowels are adjacent?
2. Three coins are tossed. What is the probability of getting at least two heads, expressed as nCr-style ratio?

| Mistake | Correction |
| --- | --- |
| Using nCr for ranked lists | Use nPr when position matters |
| Forgetting complement | "At least one" → 1 − P(none) |
| Treating draws as independent | Without replacement changes denominator |
| Counting circular as n! | Divide by n: result is (n−1)! |
