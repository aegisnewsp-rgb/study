# I. Macroeconomic Problems and Policies — HKDSE Economics Notes

This topic covers Topic I of the Compulsory Part of HKDSE Economics. It addresses inflation (types, causes, measurement, costs); unemployment (types, causes, the natural rate); economic growth and the business cycle; fiscal policy (budget deficit, public debt, balanced budget); and monetary policy (interest rates, money supply, exchange-rate considerations in Hong Kong).

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Inflation**: a sustained rise in the general price level.
- **Types of inflation**:
  - **Demand-pull**: AD shifts right beyond potential output.
  - **Cost-push**: SRAS shifts left (e.g. oil price shock).
  - **Built-in (wage-price spiral)**: expectations of inflation become self-fulfilling.
- **Measurement of inflation**:
  - **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**: measure of the prices of a typical basket of consumer goods and services.
  - **GDP deflator**: broadest price index (covers all domestically produced goods and services).
  - **Producer Price Index (PPI)**: measure of wholesale prices.
- **Costs of inflation**:
  - **Menu costs**: costs of changing prices (printing new menus, etc.).
  - **Shoe-leather costs**: costs of economising on cash (more frequent trips to the bank).
  - **Uncertainty**: inflation makes future planning harder.
  - **Redistribution**: inflation redistributes wealth from creditors to debtors.
  - **International competitiveness**: high inflation makes exports less competitive.
- **Hyperinflation**: very high inflation (typically > 50% per month). Devastating to the economy.
- **Unemployment types**:
  - **Frictional**: workers transitioning between jobs.
  - **Structural**: mismatch between workers' skills and jobs.
  - **Cyclical**: due to a recessionary gap.
  - **Natural rate**: the rate of unemployment consistent with potential output.
- **Business cycle**: the short-run fluctuations in real GDP around its long-run trend (expansion, peak, recession, trough).
- **Budget deficit**: G > T.
- **Public debt**: the accumulation of past budget deficits.
- **Balanced budget**: G = T.

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing the natural rate of unemployment with zero unemployment — even at the natural rate, frictional and structural unemployment exist.
- Treating "balanced budget" as "no public debt" — the public debt is the stock; the balanced budget is the flow.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Inflation

**CPI**: a measure of the prices of a typical basket of consumer goods and services. Calculated as a weighted average (weights based on expenditure shares). The CPI basket in Hong Kong includes housing, food, transport, etc.

**GDP deflator**: nominal GDP / real GDP × 100. The broadest price index, covering all domestically produced goods and services.

**Differences between CPI and GDP deflator**:

- **Scope**: CPI covers consumer goods and services; GDP deflator covers all domestically produced goods and services.
- **Import prices**: CPI includes import prices; GDP deflator does not.
- **Capital goods**: CPI excludes capital goods; GDP deflator includes them.

**Costs of inflation**:

- **Menu costs**: costs of changing prices.
- **Shoe-leather costs**: costs of economising on cash.
- **Uncertainty**: makes future planning harder.
- **Redistribution**: creditors lose, debtors gain (if inflation is unexpected).
- **International competitiveness**: higher inflation makes exports less competitive (under floating exchange rates).

**Hyperinflation**: very high inflation. Examples: Weimar Germany (1923), Zimbabwe (2007-2008), Venezuela (2018-2021).

#### Unemployment

**Measurement**: the unemployment rate = (unemployed / labour force) × 100%. The labour force includes employed and unemployed (those actively seeking work).

**Types**:

- **Frictional**: workers between jobs (e.g. recent graduates). Short-term.
- **Structural**: workers' skills do not match available jobs. Long-term.
- **Cyclical**: due to insufficient aggregate demand. Reduced by expansionary policy.
- **Seasonal**: due to seasonal variation (e.g. agricultural workers, ski instructors).

**Natural rate of unemployment**: the rate of unemployment consistent with potential output. Includes frictional and structural but not cyclical.

**Okun's Law**: a 2% rise in unemployment above the natural rate is associated with a 4% fall in real GDP (approximate relationship).

#### Economic growth and the business cycle

**Economic growth**: sustained increase in real GDP per capita. Sources: capital accumulation, labour force growth, productivity growth.

**Business cycle**: short-run fluctuations in real GDP around its long-run trend.

- **Expansion**: real GDP rises.
- **Peak**: real GDP reaches a temporary high.
- **Recession**: real GDP falls for two consecutive quarters.
- **Trough**: real GDP reaches a temporary low.

**Fluctuations**: driven by shocks (technology, policy, financial crises, oil prices, pandemics).

#### Fiscal policy

**Budget position**:

- **Surplus**: T > G.
- **Deficit**: G > T.
- **Balanced**: G = T.

**Public debt**: the stock of government debt outstanding. The accumulation of past deficits (minus surpluses).

**Debt-to-GDP ratio**: public debt / GDP. A measure of debt sustainability.

**Effects of fiscal policy**:

- **Expansionary** (↑G or ↓T): AD shifts right → output rises, unemployment falls, inflation rises.
- **Contractionary** (↓G or ↑T): AD shifts left → output falls, unemployment rises, inflation falls.

**Fiscal multiplier**: the change in GDP / the change in G or T. Greater than 1 if there is slack in the economy; less than 1 at full employment.

**Automatic stabilisers**: built-in stabilisers (e.g. progressive income tax, unemployment benefits) that dampen fluctuations without explicit action.

**Crowding out**: government borrowing raises interest rates, reducing private investment. Limits the effectiveness of expansionary fiscal policy.

#### Monetary policy

**Tools**:

- **Interest rate policy**: setting the policy rate.
- **Open market operations**: buying/selling securities.
- **Reserve requirements**: minimum reserves (not used in Hong Kong).
- **Discount window**: lending to banks.

**Transmission mechanism**: monetary policy → interest rates → investment and consumption → AD → output and inflation.

**Hong Kong's monetary policy**: Hong Kong has no independent monetary policy because of the Linked Exchange Rate. Interest rates follow US rates.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Cross-topic links

#### Exam technique

- **For inflation questions**: identify the type (demand-pull or cost-push); state the appropriate policy response.
- **For unemployment questions**: identify the type; distinguish cyclical from natural.
- **For fiscal policy questions**: identify the type (expansionary or contractionary); trace the effect on AD.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Zero unemployment is the goal." The natural rate of unemployment is the sustainable rate; lower unemployment leads to inflation.
- "A balanced budget is always desirable." A balanced budget may be inappropriate in a recession (deficit spending is expansionary).
- "Hong Kong has independent monetary policy." Hong Kong's monetary policy is constrained by the Linked Exchange Rate.
