# Dual Nature of Radiation and Matter

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> Fundamental equations, threshold invariants, and core experimental relationships for rapid JEE Advanced revision.

Electromagnetic radiation and microscopic matter both demonstrate wave-particle duality depending on the experimental probe.
- **Wave Manifestations:** Interference (Young's Double Slit), Diffraction (Fresnel/Fraunhofer), and Polarisation require spatial wave superposition.
- **Particle Manifestations:** Photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, and Blackbody radiation require localized packet energy exchange ($E = h\nu$, $p = h/\lambda$).

**Key Photoelectric Invariants:**
1. **Einstein's Photoelectric Equation:**
   $$K_{max} = h\nu - \phi = h(\nu - \nu_0) = hc\left(\frac{1}{\lambda} - \frac{1}{\lambda_0}\right)$$
   where $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\cdot\text{s} = 4.136 \times 10^{-15}\,\text{eV}\cdot\text{s}$, $\nu_0$ is the threshold frequency, $\lambda_0$ is the threshold cutoff wavelength, and $\phi = h\nu_0$ is the work function.

2. **Stopping Potential ($V_0$):**
   $$e V_0 = K_{max} \implies V_0 = \left(\frac{h}{e}\right)\nu - \frac{\phi}{e}$$
   *(The slope of the $V_0$ versus $\nu$ graph is universal: $m = h/e \approx 4.14 \times 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\cdot\text{s}$, completely independent of target metal composition).*

3. **Radiation Pressure ($P_{rad}$):**
   For a collimated beam of intensity $I$ striking a planar surface at normal incidence:
   - Perfectly absorbing surface ($R = 0$): $P_{rad} = \frac{I}{c}$, Force $F = \frac{I A}{c}$.
   - Perfectly reflecting surface ($R = 1$): $P_{rad} = \frac{2I}{c}$, Force $F = \frac{2I A}{c}$.
   - Arbitrary reflection coefficient $R$: $P_{rad} = \frac{(1+R)I}{c}$.
   - Oblique incidence at angle $\theta$ to the normal: $P_{rad} = \frac{I \cos^2\theta}{c}(1 + R)$.

4. **de Broglie Matter Wavelengths:**
   $$\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mK}}$$
   - Non-relativistic electron accelerated across voltage $V$:
     $$\lambda_e = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_e e V}} = \frac{1.227}{\sqrt{V}}\,\text{nm} = \frac{12.27}{\sqrt{V}}\,\text{Å}$$
   - Thermal neutrons at temperature $T$ ($K_{avg} = \frac{3}{2}k_B T$):
     $$\lambda_{neutron} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{3 m k_B T}} = \frac{0.286}{\sqrt{T(\text{K})}}\,\text{Å}$$

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

> Microscopic mechanics, photo-current dynamics, Davisson-Germer electron diffraction, and comparison frameworks.

Classical Maxwellian electrodynamics failed completely to explain three foundational observations of the photoelectric effect:
1. **The Instantaneous Emission Paradox:** Classical wave theory predicted that energy distributed continuously across a wavefront would require minutes or hours to accumulate enough energy on a single atomic cross-section ($10^{-20}\,\text{m}^2$) to eject an electron. Experimentally, photoemission occurs within $10^{-9}$ seconds of illumination even at ultra-low light intensities.
2. **The Frequency Cutoff Paradox:** Classical physics predicted that light of any frequency should eject electrons provided intensity (electric field amplitude) is sufficiently elevated. Experimentally, if $\nu < \nu_0$, zero photoelectrons are emitted regardless of beam power.
3. **Kinetic Energy Independence from Intensity:** Classical physics predicted electron kinetic energy would scale with Poynting flux intensity. Experimentally, $K_{max}$ is governed solely by photon frequency $\nu$, while intensity governs the rate of photon arrival (saturation photocurrent $I_{sat} \propto I_{beam}$).

#### Photocurrent Characteristics: Retarding & Accelerating Potentials
In a vacuum phototube, emitted electrons possess kinetic energies spanning from $0$ up to $K_{max}$.
- **Forward Accelerating Voltage ($V > 0$):** As collector plate potential increases, more electrons reaching the collector overcome space charge repulsion. Current rises smoothly until all emitted electrons are collected, reaching **saturation current ($I_{sat}$)**.
- **Retarding Voltage ($V < 0$):** As collector potential becomes negative, slower electrons are repelled back toward the emitter cathode. At the exact **stopping potential ($V_0$)**, even the fastest electron with kinetic energy $K_{max}$ is brought to rest before touching the collector plate:
  $$e V_0 = K_{max} = \frac{1}{2}m v_{max}^2$$
- When beam intensity is doubled at constant frequency $\nu$:
  - Saturation current doubles: $I_{sat}' = 2 I_{sat}$.
  - Stopping potential remains completely identical: $V_0' = V_0$.

#### Davisson-Germer Experiment & Electron Wave Interference
In 1927, Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer directed a collimated beam of electrons accelerated through potential $V$ onto a single crystal of nickel.
- Electrons undergo Bragg scattering from crystalline lattice planes with interplanar spacing $d$.
- Constructive interference occurs at scattering angle $\theta$ according to Bragg's law:
  $$2d \sin\theta = n \lambda \quad (n = 1, 2, \dots)$$
- For nickel, atomic spacing $D = 0.215\,\text{nm}$. The relationship between scattering angle $\phi$ (between incident and scattered beams) and Bragg angle $\theta$ is:
  $$\theta = 90^\circ - \frac{\phi}{2}$$
- At an accelerating voltage $V = 54\,\text{V}$, a prominent diffraction peak appeared at scattering angle $\phi = 50^\circ$:
  $$\theta = 90^\circ - 25^\circ = 65^\circ$$
  Using Bragg's equation for first-order diffraction ($n=1$):
  $$\lambda = 2d \sin\theta = 2(0.091\,\text{nm})\sin(65^\circ) = 0.165\,\text{nm}$$
  Comparing this with de Broglie's theoretical prediction:
  $$\lambda_{de\,Broglie} = \frac{1.227}{\sqrt{54}}\,\text{nm} = \frac{1.227}{7.348}\,\text{nm} = 0.167\,\text{nm}$$
  The $0.6\%$ agreement provided irrefutable empirical verification that macroscopic matter exhibits wave nature.

#### Compton Scattering: Relativistic Photon Kinematics
When an incident X-ray photon of wavelength $\lambda$ collides with a stationary free electron, the scattered photon emerges at angle $\theta$ with lengthened wavelength $\lambda'$.
Treating this as an elastic relativistic collision:
1. **Conservation of Relativistic Momentum:**
   $$\vec{p}_{photon} = \vec{p}_{photon}' + \vec{p}_e \implies \frac{h}{\lambda}\hat{i} = \frac{h}{\lambda'}(\cos\theta\hat{i} + \sin\theta\hat{j}) + \vec{p}_e$$
2. **Conservation of Total Relativistic Energy:**
   $$h\nu + m_e c^2 = h\nu' + \sqrt{p_e^2 c^2 + m_e^2 c^4}$$

Eliminating electron momentum $\vec{p}_e$ yields the Compton shift equation:
$$\Delta \lambda = \lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_e c}(1 - \cos\theta) = \lambda_C (1 - \cos\theta)$$

where the **Compton wavelength of the electron** is:
$$\lambda_C = \frac{h}{m_e c} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{(9.11 \times 10^{-31})(3 \times 10^8)} \approx 2.426 \times 10^{-12}\,\text{m} = 0.02426\,\text{Å}$$
- At forward scattering $\theta = 0^\circ$: $\Delta \lambda = 0$.
- At right angles $\theta = 90^\circ$: $\Delta \lambda = \lambda_C = 0.0243\,\text{Å}$.
- At backscattering $\theta = 180^\circ$: $\Delta \lambda = 2\lambda_C = 0.0485\,\text{Å}$ (maximum wavelength shift and maximum electron recoil energy).

## Worked JEE Advanced Exam Problems

### Problem 1: Two-Wavelength Photoelectric Determination of Planck's Constant

**Statement:** When light of wavelength $\lambda_1 = 300\,\text{nm}$ illuminates a clean metallic emitter, the stopping potential is measured to be $V_1 = 1.85\,\text{V}$. When the wavelength is increased to $\lambda_2 = 400\,\text{nm}$, the measured stopping potential drops to $V_2 = 0.82\,\text{V}$.
Taking the elementary charge as $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$ and speed of light $c = 3.00 \times 10^8\,\text{m/s}$:
1. Determine the experimental value of Planck's constant $h$.
2. Calculate the work function $\phi$ of the metal in electron-volts ($\text{eV}$).
3. Find the maximum speed $v_{max}$ of photoelectrons emitted under $300\,\text{nm}$ illumination.

**Step-by-Step Solution:**

1. **Formulate Einstein's Equations for Both Wavelengths:**
   $$e V_1 = \frac{hc}{\lambda_1} - \phi \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)}$$
   $$e V_2 = \frac{hc}{\lambda_2} - \phi \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)}$$

2. **Subtract Equation 2 from Equation 1:**
   $$e(V_1 - V_2) = hc \left(\frac{1}{\lambda_1} - \frac{1}{\lambda_2}\right) = hc \left(\frac{\lambda_2 - \lambda_1}{\lambda_1 \lambda_2}\right)$$
   Substitute known values:
   $$V_1 - V_2 = 1.85 - 0.82 = 1.03\,\text{V}$$
   $$\lambda_1 = 300 \times 10^{-9}\,\text{m}, \quad \lambda_2 = 400 \times 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$$
   $$\frac{\lambda_2 - \lambda_1}{\lambda_1 \lambda_2} = \frac{(400 - 300) \times 10^{-9}}{(300 \times 10^{-9})(400 \times 10^{-9})} = \frac{100 \times 10^{-9}}{1.20 \times 10^{-13}} = \frac{1}{1.20 \times 10^{-6}} = \frac{10^6}{1.20} = \frac{5}{6} \times 10^6\,\text{m}^{-1}$$

3. **Solve for Planck's Constant ($h$):**
   $$h = \frac{e(V_1 - V_2)}{c \cdot \left(\frac{\lambda_2 - \lambda_1}{\lambda_1 \lambda_2}\right)} = \frac{(1.602 \times 10^{-19})(1.03)}{(3.00 \times 10^8)\left(\frac{5}{6} \times 10^6\right)}$$
   $$h = \frac{1.650 \times 10^{-19}}{2.50 \times 10^{14}} = 6.60 \times 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\cdot\text{s}$$
   *(Remarkable agreement with the accepted literature constant of $6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\cdot\text{s}$).*

4. **Calculate Metal Work Function ($\phi$):**
   Using Equation 1:
   $$\phi = \frac{hc}{\lambda_1} - e V_1$$
   $$\frac{hc}{\lambda_1} \text{ in eV} = \frac{(6.60 \times 10^{-34})(3.00 \times 10^8)}{(300 \times 10^{-9})(1.602 \times 10^{-19})} = \frac{1.980 \times 10^{-25}}{4.806 \times 10^{-26}} = 4.120\,\text{eV}$$
   $$\phi = 4.120\,\text{eV} - 1.85\,\text{eV} = 2.27\,\text{eV}$$
   *(Matches potassium/cesium alloy characteristics).*

5. **Maximum Photoelectron Velocity ($v_{max}$):**
   $$K_{max} = e V_1 = 1.85\,\text{eV} = 1.85 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = 2.964 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$$
   $$\frac{1}{2}m_e v_{max}^2 = K_{max} \implies v_{max} = \sqrt{\frac{2 K_{max}}{m_e}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 2.964 \times 10^{-19}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}}} = \sqrt{6.507 \times 10^{11}} \approx 8.07 \times 10^5\,\text{m/s}$$

### Problem 2: Relativistic Momentum & de Broglie Wavelength of an Accelerated Particle

**Statement:** A particle of rest mass $m_0$ and charge $q$ is accelerated from rest through a very large potential difference $V$, such that its kinetic energy is comparable to its rest mass energy ($K = m_0 c^2$).
1. Derive the exact relativistic expression for the de Broglie wavelength $\lambda_{rel}$ as a function of accelerating voltage $V$.
2. Show how this expression reduces to the non-relativistic formula when $qV \ll m_0 c^2$.
3. Compute the ratio of the true relativistic de Broglie wavelength to the non-relativistic approximation for an electron accelerated through $V = 511\,\text{kV}$ (where $m_0 c^2 = 511\,\text{keV}$).

**Step-by-Step Solution:**

1. **Relativistic Energy-Momentum Invariant:**
   The total relativistic energy $E$ is related to rest mass $m_0$ and momentum $p$ by:
   $$E^2 = p^2 c^2 + m_0^2 c^4$$
   The total energy is also the sum of kinetic energy $K = qV$ and rest energy $m_0 c^2$:
   $$E = K + m_0 c^2 = qV + m_0 c^2$$

2. **Express Momentum $p$ in terms of Accelerating Potential:**
   $$(qV + m_0 c^2)^2 = p^2 c^2 + m_0^2 c^4$$
   $$(qV)^2 + 2 qV m_0 c^2 + m_0^2 c^4 = p^2 c^2 + m_0^2 c^4$$
   $$p^2 c^2 = (qV)^2 + 2 m_0 c^2 (qV) = 2 m_0 c^2 (qV) \left[1 + \frac{qV}{2 m_0 c^2}\right]$$
   $$p = \frac{\sqrt{2 m_0 q V}}{c} \cdot c \sqrt{1 + \frac{qV}{2 m_0 c^2}} = \sqrt{2 m_0 q V} \sqrt{1 + \frac{qV}{2 m_0 c^2}}$$

3. **Formulate de Broglie Wavelength:**
   $$\lambda_{rel} = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2 m_0 q V}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \frac{qV}{2 m_0 c^2}}}$$

4. **Non-Relativistic Limit:**
   When $qV \ll m_0 c^2$, the factor $\frac{qV}{2 m_0 c^2} \rightarrow 0$, yielding:
   $$\lambda_{classical} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2 m_0 q V}}$$
   This proves that the relativistic wavelength is always **strictly shorter** than the classical prediction:
   $$\frac{\lambda_{rel}}{\lambda_{classical}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \frac{qV}{2 m_0 c^2}}}$$

5. **Numerical Evaluation for $V = 511\,\text{kV}$ Electron:**
   Here $qV = 511\,\text{keV}$ and $m_0 c^2 = 511\,\text{keV}$:
   $$\frac{qV}{2 m_0 c^2} = \frac{511}{2 \times 511} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.50$$
   $$\frac{\lambda_{rel}}{\lambda_{classical}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + 0.50}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1.50}} = \frac{1}{1.2247} \approx 0.8165$$
   The relativistic de Broglie wavelength is $18.35\%$ shorter than the naive classical formula would suggest. Neglecting relativistic kinematics in high-energy TEM calculations introduces fatal diffraction indexing errors.

## Critical JEE Advanced Traps & Heuristic Checkpoints

- **Trap 1: Photon Momentum vs. Particle Momentum**: For a photon, $E = pc \implies p = E/c = h\nu/c$. For a particle with rest mass, $E = \sqrt{p^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4} \ne pc$. Never equate particle momentum to $E/c$ unless its kinetic energy dominates rest mass by orders of magnitude.
- **Trap 2: Collector Plate Work Function ($\phi_c$)**: In standard phototubes, the measured stopping potential $V_0$ across the external battery depends on the **collector's work function** if the voltmeter measures contact potential difference: $e V_{ext} = h\nu - \phi_c$. However, standard textbook convention assumes identical electrodes unless contact potential difference is explicitly specified.
- **Trap 3: Intensity and Beam Broadening**: If a point isotropic source of power $P$ is placed at distance $r$ from an emitter, intensity drops as $I = P/(4\pi r^2)$. Doubling the distance halves electric field $E_0$ but quarters intensity $I \propto 1/r^2$, reducing saturation photocurrent by a factor of $4$ while leaving stopping potential $V_0$ completely unchanged.
- **Trap 4: de Broglie Wavelength of Uncharged Molecules**: For an ideal gas of monoatomic molecules at temperature $T$, average translational kinetic energy per molecule is $\langle K \rangle = \frac{3}{2}k_B T$. The mean thermal de Broglie wavelength is $\lambda_{th} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{3 m k_B T}}$, which depends on absolute temperature in Kelvin, not Celsius.
