# Fundamental Rights and Duties

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Six enforceable rights in Part III, eleven-plus moral duties in Part IV-A — one is justiciable, the other is not.

Part III (Articles 12–35) of the Indian Constitution holds Fundamental Rights, enforceable through courts. Part IV-A (Article 51-A) lists Fundamental Duties, added by the 42nd Amendment, 1976, on the Swaran Singh Committee's recommendation — moral obligations only, not legally enforceable.

Six clusters of rights:

| Cluster | Articles | Core Subject |
|---|---|---|
| Right to Equality | 14–18 | Non-discrimination, abolition of untouchability, reservation |
| Right to Freedom | 19–22 | Six freedoms, life & liberty, protection against conviction |
| Right against Exploitation | 23–24 | Forced labour, child labour |
| Right to Freedom of Religion | 25–28 | Religious practice, religious education |
| Cultural & Educational Rights | 29–30 | Minorities' language, culture, institutions |
| Constitutional Remedies | 32 | Five writs through Supreme Court |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook — "E-FACER":** Equality, Freedom, Anti-exploitation, Culture/Education, Religion, Remedies — the six heads of Part III in order.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Right to Equality, the writ structure, and Article 32 vs 226 are where UPPSC PCS repeatedly frames its MCQs.

#### Six Freedoms Under Article 19

Article 19 grants six freedoms, available to **citizens only**:

1. Speech and expression (19(1)(a))
2. Assembly without arms (19(1)(b))
3. Association (19(1)(c))
4. Movement (19(1)(d))
5. Residence (19(1)(e))
6. Profession (19(1)(f))

Reasonable restrictions follow in clauses (2)–(6) on grounds like sovereignty, public order, decency, and the interests of Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.

#### Right to Equality — Articles 14–18

Article 14 fuses two principles: American "equal protection of the laws" and British "rule of law." Article 15 forbids discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth, with carve-outs: 15(4) for SC/ST/OBC reservation (added by the 1st Amendment, 1951, after *Champakam Dorairajan*), 15(5) for EWS reservation (103rd Amendment, 2018). Article 16 governs public employment; sub-clauses 16(4), 16(4A), and 16(6) permit reservation in initial recruitment and promotion, including for EWS.

Article 17 abolishes untouchability, enforced originally by the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1976, and now the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989.

#### Five Writs — When the Court Issues What

| Writ | Meaning | Typical Use |
|---|---|---|
| Habeas Corpus | "to have the body" | Illegal detention |
| Mandamus | "we command" | Compel a public authority to perform a duty |
| Prohibition | "to forbid" | Stop an inferior court from exceeding jurisdiction |
| Certiorari | "to be certified" | Quash orders of inferior courts/tribunals |
| Quo Warranto | "by what authority" | Challenge unlawful holding of a public office |

Article 32 makes writ jurisdiction itself a Fundamental Right before the Supreme Court. Article 226 gives High Courts wider writ power but is a constitutional right, not a fundamental one. Mandamus cannot lie against the President or Governor (Article 361) or against private individuals.

#### Reservation Matrix — Often Confused

| Provision | Scope | Amendment |
|---|---|---|
| Art. 15(4) | SC/ST/OBC in educational institutions | 1st Amendment, 1951 |
| Art. 15(5) | EWS reservation in admissions | 103rd Amendment, 2018 |
| Art. 16(4) | Reservation in promotion for SC/ST | Original 1950 text |
| Art. 16(4A) | Promotion with consequential seniority | 77th Amendment, 1995 |
| Art. 16(6) | EWS in public employment | 103rd Amendment, 2018 |

#### Fundamental Duties — Article 51-A

Originally 11 duties in 1976. The 86th Amendment (2002) added duty (j) — parents/guardians to provide education opportunities to children aged 6–14. Two further duties were added by the 103rd Amendment, 2018: duty (k) to promote scientific temper, and duty (l) to protect monuments and places of national importance. The current count is **14 duties**, all non-justiciable, applicable to citizens only.

> 📌 **Exam Reality Check:** UPPSC PCS Prelims has tested the count of duties, the article that adds them, and the name of the recommending committee. Memorise 11 → 14 and the three amendments that did the adding.

#### Worked MCQ — Article 32 vs 226

**Statement:** "Article 32 is the only Fundamental Right that guarantees a remedy for the violation of other Fundamental Rights."

#### Resolution:

- Article 32 gives the Supreme Court power to issue writs (habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto) for enforcement of Fundamental Rights.
- Dr. B.R. Ambedkar called it the "heart and soul of the Constitution" (Constituent Assembly Debates, 1948).
- Article 226 grants similar writ power to High Courts, but its scope is wider because it extends beyond Fundamental Rights to "any other purpose" — yet 226 is **not** itself a Fundamental Right.
- Statement is correct: 32 is the only article in Part III that is simultaneously a right and a remedy.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Many candidates mark this wrong because they equate 226 with 32. The distinction is constitutional status — only 32 sits inside Part III.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Article 21 expanded in 1978. Article 19 disappears in a National Emergency. Read together, they show the limits of justiciability.

#### Article 21 — Post-Maneka Gandhi Horizons

Until 1978, Article 21 ("No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law") was read narrowly. *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978) fused it with Article 14, requiring that any procedure be fair, just, and reasonable — not merely "established."

The post-Maneka expansion includes:

- Livelihood and livelihood-related rights
- Right to privacy (*K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India*, 2017)
- Right to clean environment and ecology (*M.C. Mehta* cases)
- Right to education via Article 21A (86th Amendment, 2002, operationalised by RTE Act, 2009)
- Right to medical care, legal aid, fair trial, and speedy trial

Article 21A covers children aged **6–14 years** for free and compulsory elementary education.

#### Suspension During Emergency

| Article | Effect During National Emergency |
|---|---|
| Art. 358 | Article 19 is automatically suspended when emergency is declared on grounds of war or external aggression |
| Art. 359 | Other Fundamental Rights can be suspended by presidential order |
| Art. 20 | **Cannot** be suspended (ex post facto, double jeopardy, self-incrimination) |
| Art. 21 | **Cannot** be suspended (post-44th Amendment, 1978) |

The 44th Amendment, 1978, was the legislative answer to *A.D.M. Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla* (1976), where the Supreme Court had held that even Article 21 stood suspended during Emergency.

#### Basic Structure and the Limits of Amendment

*Minerva Mills v. Union of India* (1980) struck down Section 4 of the 42nd Amendment, holding that the harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is part of the Constitution's basic structure — Parliament cannot destroy either to elevate the other.

Advanced traps for Mains and Prelims:

1. Confusing *A.D.M. Jabalpur* (1976, rights suspended) with *Minerva Mills* (1980, basic structure protected) — opposite rulings on the reach of emergency/state power.
2. Stating 42nd Amendment added Fundamental Duties — correct — but quoting it as the source of rights is wrong; rights predate it.
3. Listing writs of Article 226 as exclusive to High Courts in their original-jurisdiction sense, forgetting that the Constitution expressly grants them.
4. Counting the original 1976 duties as 12 or 13 — they were 11; the current total is 14.
5. Treating Article 25(2)(b) as anti-minority — it specifically targets Hindu religious institutions to throw open temple entry, historically for eradication of untouchability.

#### Practice Prompts

1. *"Right to Property was removed from Part III by the 44th Amendment and made a legal right under Article 300A. Discuss its implications for state acquisition and the right to compensation."* — Examine how the shift from a fundamental right to a statutory right altered judicial review standards, especially post-*Vineet Narain* and *Jilubhai Nanbhai Khachar*.
2. *"Critically examine whether Fundamental Duties can be enforced without statutory backing, particularly duties (a), (k), and (l) of Article 51-A."* — Reference the Verma Committee Report (1999), which proposed duty enforcement, and the practical non-justiciability that Parliament has maintained.
