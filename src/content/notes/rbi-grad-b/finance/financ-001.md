# Financial Markets and Institutions

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> One screen, sixty seconds — what an examiner in most keys tests from this chapter.

The **RBI** polices banks and the **money market**; **SEBI** polices the **capital market**, mutual funds, and stock exchanges. That single line settles roughly half the MCQs on regulatory jurisdiction.

| Market / Institution | Maturity Window | Regulator | One-line Identity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Money Market | ≤ 1 year | RBI | Wholesale short-term funds: T-Bills, CP, CD, Repo, CBLO |
| Capital Market | > 1 year | SEBI | Equity (BSE/NSE), debt, hybrids, IPOs |
| Banks | All tenors | RBI | Accept deposits, create credit, run payments |
| NBFCs | All tenors | RBI (Dept. of NBFC Regulation) | Twin 50% test: financial assets > 50% AND financial income > 50% |
| Insurers | Long-term liabilities | IRDAI | Life, general, health, reinsurance — indemnity & insurable interest |
| Pension Funds / NPS | Very long-term | PFRDA | Retirement corpus, point-of-presence architecture |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"R-S-I-P"** = **R**BI (banks, money, NBFC), **S**EBI (stocks, MFs, IFSC), **I**RDAI (insurance), **P**FRDA (pension). Match the institution to the regulator in this order and you answer every "regulator of X" question correctly.

The must-know money-market identity: **CP is corporate paper (₹5 lakh minimum, 15 days–1 year), CD is bank paper (7 days–1 year)** — never reverse the two.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> The working layer you revise from the night before Phase-II.

Financial intermediation is the transfer of purchasing power from surplus units (households holding idle cash) to deficit units (firms needing working capital or capex). The transfer happens through **financial markets** (price discovery venues) and **financial institutions** (intermediaries that warehouse the risk). RBI's *Report on Trend and Progress of Banking in India* repeatedly frames the system as a four-corner triangle: savers, investors, intermediaries, and regulators.

#### Core Concepts and Definitions

An **NBFC** is registered under the Companies Act and is a company whose **principal business is financial activity**. The RBI twin test demands that both (a) financial assets constitute more than 50% of total assets (net of intangibles), and (b) income from financial assets constitutes more than 50% of gross income. NBFCs split into **NBFC-D (Deposit-taking)** — must hold a minimum investment-grade credit rating and comply with CRR/SLR — and **NBFC-ND (Non-Deposit-taking)**, further tagged **NBFC-ND-SI** when assets cross ₹500 crore. Specialist flavours: NBFC-MFI (microfinance), NBFC-HFC (housing), NBFC-IFC (infrastructure), NBFC-IDF (infrastructure debt fund), NBFC-Factors, and the newer **Account Aggregator (AA)** licence under DPNB.

The **money market** trades instruments with original maturity up to one year. Sub-segments are the **call money market** (overnight, no collateral), **notice money** (2–14 days), and **term money** (15 days–1 year). The **capital market** includes the **primary market** (IPO, FPO, Rights, Bonus — fresh issue of securities) and the **secondary market** (trading of already-issued securities on BSE and NSE). A common descriptive question asks students to "differentiate between primary and secondary market" — the cleanest answer frames primary as capital formation and secondary as liquidity provision.

Mutual funds operate under a four-part structure: **Sponsor** sets up the fund, **Trust** (with a Board of Trustees) holds it, **AMC** (Asset Management Company) manages it, and the **Custodian** safekeeps assets. SEBI categorises schemes into Equity, Debt, Hybrid, Solution-oriented, Index/ETF, and Fund of Fund.

The insurance architecture runs through **IRDAI**: insurers (life, general, health, standalone health, reinsurance), intermediaries (brokers, web aggregators), claim managers (TPAs), and policyholders. Two principles recur in exam answers: **insurable interest** (you must stand to lose from the event insured) and **utmost good faith** (*uberrima fides* — both parties disclose material facts).

#### Comparison Matrix — Commonly Confused Pairs

| Concept | Feature | Risk of Confusion |
|---|---|---|
| Commercial Paper (CP) | Issued by corporates and NBFCs; ₹5 lakh minimum; 15 days–1 year | In many papers swapped with CD; CP issuer is **not** a bank |
| Certificate of Deposit (CD) | Issued only by scheduled banks and select all-India FIs; 7 days–1 year |
| Repo | RBI lends to banks against sovereign collateral | Direction confusion — repo is RBI injecting liquidity |
| Reverse Repo | RBI absorbs liquidity from banks |
| FDI vs FII | FDI = ≥ 10% equity in a company (DPIIT/RBI); strategic, controlling | "Both foreign capital" — but jurisdiction differs |
| FII | Portfolio investment (SEBI-registered FPI route); no control |
| Universal Bank vs Narrow Bank | Universal: full-services bank (deposits, lending, FX, investment) | In many papers treated as synonyms |
| Narrow Bank | Wholesale-only, no retail lending (RBI 2021 discussion paper) |
| Payments Bank vs Small Finance Bank | Payments Bank: deposit up to ₹2 lakh per account, **cannot lend** | Limits in many papers mis-quoted |
| Small Finance Bank | Full lending, 75% priority to small/marginal farmers, micro-enterprises |
| DFI vs NBFC | DFI: statutorily created (NABARD, SIDBI, NHB, NaBFID) | NBFC registration is separate; DFIs are not NBFCs |
| NBFC | Registered under Companies Act + RBI certificate of registration |

> 📌 **Formula Check:** Simple interest SI = (P × R × T)/100, where P in ₹, R in % per annum, T in years; dimension [₹]. Compound interest A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt), with A and P in ₹, r as decimal annual rate, n as compounding frequency per year, t in years. These appear in bond pricing and NPV preliminaries.

#### Worked Walk-Through on a Typical Phase-II Question

**Problem statement:** A 1-year Treasury Bill is issued at a discount of ₹9,000 per ₹100 face value. A banker wishes to rediscount it after 6 months at the prevailing 6% discount rate. Compute the bank's holding-period return.

Step 1 — Issue price. The bill sells at ₹100 − ₹9 = ₹91 per ₹100 face, so the implicit issue yield is 9/91 ≈ 9.89% annualised.

Step 2 — Rediscount value after 6 months at 6% discount. Discount for 6 months on ₹100 face = 6% × ½ × 100 = ₹3, so the bill is rediscounted at ₹100 − ₹3 = ₹97.

Step 3 — Bank's holding-period gain. The banker bought at ₹91 and sells at ₹97: gain = ₹6 on an outlay of ₹91, giving 6/91 = 6.59% for the half-year, annualised to roughly 13.18% on a simple basis.

Step 4 — Sanity check. The 6% discount applied to a half-year looks low, but the bank's actual yield is high because of the deep original discount. This is the examiner's hook: students who annualise 6% directly (12%) miss the carrying cost of the entry price.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Candidates forget that the discount rate applies to **face value**, not to the purchase price, so the true holding-period yield is computed on the outlay, not on ₹100. Mixing up the base wipes out almost two marks in a 6-mark descriptive.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Edge cases, regulatory quirks, and the questions that decide top ranks.

Start with the **regulatory perimeter**, because every descriptive answer is graded on whether you can name the right statute and the right body. **Banking Regulation Act, 1949** (now amended in 2020 to bring cooperative banks under RBI's direct control), **RBI Act, 1934** (monetary policy, currency, NBFC regulation), **SEBI Act, 1992** (markets, intermediaries, MFs, FPIs), **IRDA Act, 1999** (insurance, now IRDAI Act), and **PFRDA Act, 2013** (NPS, APY, pension funds). **IFSCA Act, 2019** sets up the **International Financial Services Centres Authority** in GIFT City, an umbrella regulator for financial services units located in IFSC. Listing an act that does not exist, or quoting the wrong year, is the most common reason a Phase-II descriptive answer loses a mark.

#### Edge Cases and Boundary Conditions

| Edge Case | What the Rule Actually Says | Why Students Slip |
|---|---|---|
| NBFC holding a deposit | Only NBFC-D can; NBFC-ND accepting deposits is a violation | Many assume every NBFC can take deposits |
| Repo eligibility | Only central government securities (and SDLs since 2014) | Students widen to "any security" |
| Payments Bank lending | Prohibited — can invest only in sovereign and Tier-1 bonds | Confused with Small Finance Bank |
| Primary Dealer role | Underwrites government auctions, runs proprietary G-Sec book; RBI-certified | Confused with brokers/sub-brokers |
| Account Aggregator (AA) | Licensed as NBFC under DPNB; transmits data, does not store funds | Confused with credit bureau |
| NBFC-MFI pricing | Margin cap and interest rate ceiling rules under RBI master direction | In many papers over-simplified to "any NBFC" |
| Co-operative bank under 2020 amendment | Brought under Banking Regulation Act; RBI can supersede boards | Frequently omitted in regulatory diagrams |
| NaBFID | Created via NABFID Act 2021; DFI for infrastructure financing | Mistaken for a subsidiary of NABARD |

#### Interest-Rate Corridor and LAF Plumbing

The **LAF corridor** is built around the policy repo rate. The **Standing Deposit Facility (SDF)** sits 25 bps below repo (RBI absorbs liquidity without collateral), the **Marginal Standing Facility (MSF)** sits 25 bps above repo (overnight window against SLR securities), and the **Bank Rate** is aligned to MSF for penalty lending. Call money bounces inside this corridor. **Open Market Operations (OMOs)** and **Variable Rate Reverse Repo auctions** adjust durable liquidity, while **CRR** (currently 4% of net demand and time liabilities) and **SLR** (18% of NDTL, plus the 2% incremental CRR buffer when applicable) are the structural levers. Bond prices move inversely to yields: when repo rises, the price of an existing fixed-coupon bond falls, because its cash flows are discounted at a higher rate.

#### Mutual Fund and Insurance Architecture — Memory Load

Sponsor (in standard papers the parent AMC promoter) → Trust (irrevocable, with SEBI-approved trustees) → AMC (investment decisions) → Custodian (in standard papers a bank, holds securities in demat). For insurance, the **insurer** underwrites the policy, the **broker or web aggregator** distributes, the **TPA** administers claims, the **reinsurer** (e.g., GIC Re) cedes part of the risk, and the **policyholder** carries insurable interest. In NPS, **PFRDA** is regulator, **PFC** (Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority-empanelled fund managers like LIC Pension Fund, SBI Pension Fund, UTI Retirement Solutions) manage the corpus, and **PoPs** (Points of Presence) onboard subscribers.

#### Recent Regulatory Developments Worth a Line

- **Account Aggregator framework (2021 onwards):** RBI-regulated NBFCs under the Digital Lending and NBFC-AA directions; consent-based data sharing between FIP (Financial Information Provider) and FIU (Financial Information User).
- **Digital Lending Guidelines (SEBI/RBI 2022, revised 2024):** every Digital Lending App must disclose the principal lender, all-in-cost, and recovery mechanism; disbursal and repayment must flow through a regulated bank or NBFC escrow.
- **IFSCA / GIFT City IFSC:** a unified regulator for units in the IFSC; offshore banking units, IFSC Insurance Office, IFSC Banking Unit, and capital-market intermediaries all sit under one roof.
- **Cooperative Banks under RBI (2020 Amendment):** Section 35A powers extended; cooperative banks now part of CRR, SLR, and priority-sector frameworks.
- **NaBFID (2021):** statutorily created DFI for infrastructure; initial government contribution and tax-free bond-raising window.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Writing "RBI regulates capital markets" or "SEBI regulates banks". The cleanest one-liner is: **RBI = money + banks + NBFCs; SEBI = capital market + MFs + FPIs + IFSC market intermediaries; IRDAI = insurance; PFRDA = pension; IFSCA = everything inside IFSC.**

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. A descriptive answer asks: *"Discuss the regulatory architecture of Indian financial markets with a note on the IFSCA umbrella model."* Outline five paragraphs — (i) RBI and money market, (ii) SEBI and capital market, (iii) IRDAI and PFRDA, (iv) IFSCA's unified IFSC regulator and its rationale, (v) recent convergence (AA framework, digital lending). Cite the statute for each body.
2. A numerical MCQ gives the NPV of a 3-year project with cash flows of ₹100, ₹120, ₹140 and discount rate 10%, asking which is closest. Compute NPV = −C₀ + 100/(1.10)¹ + 120/(1.10)² + 140/(1.10)³ = −200 + 90.91 + 99.17 + 105.18 = ₹95.26 approximately. Watch for traps where students drop the initial investment sign.
