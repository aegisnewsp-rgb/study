# Quantitative Reasoning and Problem Solving

The quantitative half of HPAT Section 1 is not a maths test. It does not ask whether you can do arithmetic faster than a calculator, and it does not reward advanced technique. It asks whether you can take an unfamiliar situation described in prose, work out what is actually being asked, and get to a defensible answer inside a time budget. Candidates with weaker mathematical backgrounds routinely outperform candidates with stronger ones on these items, because the failure is nearly always in the reading rather than the arithmetic.

## What these items actually are

The format is almost always the same shape: a short scenario, a table or schedule of figures, and a question with four or five options. The difficulty is concentrated in the first step — deciding what quantity the question wants and in what unit.

Three things arrive together and must be untangled separately:

- **The scenario** tells you what the numbers mean and what is fixed.
- **The data** gives the figures, frequently as a table where the relevant row is not the first one.
- **The question** specifies the quantity, the unit and the reference point.

Candidates reliably lose time by starting at the data and hunting for the answer there. Start at the question.

## The five-step procedure

1. **Read the question first.** Write the target quantity in your own words and note the unit. If the unit is not stated in the question, infer it and check it against the data.
2. **Scan the scenario for the constraint.** Rate, ratio, percentage change, per-capita, fixed total, two-stage — one of these is in play, and it determines which formula applies.
3. **Locate the data.** Find the specific row or column. Tables are usually distractor-dense: adjacent rows share headings and differ by one factor.
4. **Compute once, cleanly.** Write the operation before the numbers. Substitute the rounded figures shown in the table, not remembered or re-derived ones.
5. **Check the options.** Most items are designed so that two or three options correspond to specific errors — using a numerator instead of a denominator, forgetting to multiply by a second stage, applying the change in the wrong direction. Reading the options tells you which errors the item is built around, and a quick scan for your value among them often identifies your mistake.

## The constraint bank

Almost every item resolves to one of these. Recognise it from the question wording and the setup is half done.

| Constraint | Trigger wording | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Rate | "per", "per hour", "average rate" | Quantity = rate × time or quantity ÷ time; identify direction |
| Ratio and proportion | "twice", "three times as", "for every" | Multiply or divide by the ratio; check which direction |
| Percentage change | "increase by", "fall by", "compared with" | Multiply by (1 + r); the base is the reference, not the target |
| Part–whole | "of the total", "share", "breakdown" | The part must be scaled to the total before the rate is applied |
| Per-capita | "per person", "per 1000" | Divide by the population; check the multiplier used |
| Change over time | "between X and Y", "each year" | Compare like with like; annual change ≠ total change |
| Weighted average | "average", "combined", "overall" | Weight each part by its own count before summing |
| Two-stage | "of those, some proportion" | Do the first stage, carry the reduced base into the second |

## The option-as-diagnostic technique

Options in well-designed multiple-choice items are not random numbers. Each one is there because a particular mistake produces it. When your computed value is absent, do not immediately recompute — work out what each wrong option represents.

- Exactly your answer divided by two, or doubled → the per-capita or per-unit scaling was missed in one direction.
- Your answer with the percentage change applied backwards → the base was swapped.
- Your answer with one stage's proportion omitted → a two-stage item done as one stage.
- An answer that matches a raw figure straight from the table → the question asked for a derived quantity and you returned the given one.
- An answer close to your value but not equal → rounding, or the wrong row.

This turns a lost item into a named, fixable error, which is worth far more than the single mark.

## Time discipline

- **Budget per item and hold it.** These items are deliberately proportional. An item taking twice its budget is an item to mark and move from.
- **Do not let the scenario become an essay.** Two lines of working is enough. If you are writing a third, you have misunderstood the question.
- **Estimate before you compute.** Look at the options. If your intuition says the answer is in the low hundreds and you have set up a formula that will give you a number under ten, the setup is wrong — and you have found out in five seconds instead of three minutes.
- **Leave nothing blank.** A guess on a multiple-choice paper costs nothing but the option you eliminate.

## Common errors

1. **Answering the question that was near yours.** These items often contain a second quantity you could compute. Check the verb: *how many*, *what proportion*, *how much more*.
2. **Ratio direction.** "Twice as many doctors as nurses" is a statement about the larger group. Reversing it produces an answer that looks plausible and is wrong.
3. **Percentage base confusion.** A twenty per cent increase applied to the wrong figure is the single most common arithmetic error in the bank.
4. **Ignoring a second stage.** The question describes a filter, then a rate on the remainder. Applying the rate to the original total is a designed distractor and will be one of your options.
5. **Reading the table's units once.** Columns headed in thousands, percentages or per-capita figures require one more conversion, and that conversion is where the option space narrows.

## Self-check

1. An item gives a table of regional figures and a question about a national total. What is the trap? *(The national figure is not in the table. You must weight each regional value by its own population or count, and several options correspond to summing the column or averaging it instead.)*
2. Your computed value is not among the options. What is the first thing to do? *(Work out what each wrong option represents. The option set is a map of the designed mistakes, and identifying one of them usually locates your error immediately.)*
3. "The number of clinics rose by a quarter between the two years." What is the operation? *(Multiply the earlier figure by 1.25. The base is the earlier figure, and the direction of the change matters — applying the increase to the later figure produces a plausible but wrong option.)*
4. Why is estimating from the options before computing a time-saver? *(Because a gross mismatch — wrong order of magnitude, wrong unit — tells you the setup is wrong within seconds, rather than after a full calculation.)*
5. You are stuck on item twelve with four items after it, none marked. What is the correct action? *(Mark item twelve as unresolved, answer the four behind it, and return if time allows. Items are not ordered by difficulty; sinking time into one recovers at most one mark and can cost several.)*
