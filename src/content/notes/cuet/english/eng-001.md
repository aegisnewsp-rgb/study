# Vocabulary

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your CUET UG English slot.

Vocabulary in CUET UG English tests your ability to recognise and use words in context — synonyms, antonyms, **one-word substitutions**, **idioms**, **phrasal verbs**, **collocations**, and commonly confused word pairs. About 25–35% of the Section IA English paper draws on vocabulary items, both inside Reading Comprehension passages and as standalone language questions.

- **Synonym** = word with similar meaning; **Antonym** = word with opposite meaning (gradable, complementary, or relational).
- **One-word substitution** replaces a phrase with a single precise word (e.g., *polyglot* = person who knows many languages).
- **Idioms** and **phrasal verbs** have non-literal meanings (e.g., *kick the bucket* = die; *put off* = postpone).

Always read the surrounding sentence before locking an answer — context overrides dictionary recall.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months before the CUET UG English test.

#### Word Relationships Tested

CUET items cluster around four relationship types. Recognising the type tells you whether to expect similarity, opposition, or precision-based answers.

| Relationship | What it tests | Classic CUET trap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Synonym | Similar meaning | Near-synonym distractors (e.g., *happy* vs *elated*) |
| Antonym | Opposite meaning | Graded opposites offered as true opposites |
| One-word substitution | Single precise word for a phrase | Overlong phrases where one word fits |
| Idioms / Phrasal verbs | Non-literal meaning | Literal reading of the phrase |

#### Antonym Types You Must Distinguish

- **Gradable opposites** sit on a scale (*hot/cold*, *big/small*) — the answer is not a synonym of one pole.
- **Complementary opposites** are absolute pairs (*dead/alive*, *pass/fail*) — no middle ground exists.
- **Relational opposites** reverse a role (*buy/sell*, *teacher/student*) — these appear in many papers in CUET sentence-completion items.

#### Word Formation Toolkit

Prefixes (*un-*, *in-*, *dis-*, *re-*, *mis-*, *pre-*) and suffixes (*-tion*, *-ment*, *-able*, *-ous*, *-ive*) combine with Latin or Greek roots (*bene-*, *mal-*, *chrono-*, *bio-*) to decode unfamiliar words in RC passages. Spotting the root *bene-* (good) immediately signals a positive connotation in words like *benevolent*, *beneficial*, *benediction*.

#### Common Confusables

| Word pair | Quick memory cue |
| --- | --- |
| affect / effect | Affect = verb (to influence); effect = noun (the result) |
| stationary / stationery | Stationary = still; stationery = paper (both contain *e*) |
| principal / principle | Principal = head of school (person); principle = rule |
| fewer / less | Fewer = countable nouns; less = uncountable mass |

> Tip: Collocations punish literal translations. Say *make a mistake* (not *do*), *strong tea* (not *powerful*), *commit a crime* (not *perpetrate*).

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline targeting top CUET UG scores.

#### Contextual Meaning vs Dictionary Meaning

CUET frequently embeds unfamiliar words inside Reading Comprehension passages and asks for the meaning **as used in the passage**. Here, **connotation** (associative shade) matters as much as **denotation** (literal sense). *Home* denotatively means *dwelling*, but connotatively signals *warmth and family*. Two answer choices can share the literal sense; the correct one matches the passage's emotional register — formal, neutral, or informal.

#### Edge Cases Worth Drilling

1. **Idiomatic vs literal**: *Break the ice* means *initiate conversation*, not shatter frozen water. CUET options in many papers include a literal-sounding distractor.
2. **Collocational fit**: *Pungent smell* beats *strong smell*; *heavy rain* beats *strong rain*; *deep sleep* beats *strong sleep*. Natural pairings are scored, not logical ones.
3. **Register**: Academic passages lean on the Academic Word List (*analyse*, *convey*, *derive*, *establish*, *subsequent*). Formal items rarely clash with slangy choices, but informal registers can surface in narrative RC.

#### Worked Micro-Example

> "The minister's **pragmatic** approach to the crisis drew both praise and criticism."
> Q: A word closest in meaning to *pragmatic* is…
> A: *Realistic* (correct) — not *idealistic* (antonym distractor), not *practical* (near-synonym but weaker match), not *theoretical* (opposite shade).

Here the answer hinges on connotation matching: *realistic* carries the same problem-solving tone as *pragmatic*, while *practical* is too neutral.

#### Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

- Picking a synonym from a shared root (*benevolent* vs *beneficial*) without checking whether the surrounding clause calls for *kind* or *useful*.
- Treating *hot* and *warm* as antonyms — they are not; only true opposites earn the mark.
- Choosing an idiom's literal meaning when the idiomatic sense is demanded.

#### Practice Prompts

1. Replace the phrase with one word: *a government ruled by a single person* → **autocracy**.
2. Give the antonym type and a matching word for *employer* → **relational**; opposite is *employee*.

> Exam strategy: In CUET English, vocabulary items average 45–60 seconds each. Spend the first 10 seconds locating the **context clue** (contrast signal, definition signal, or example signal) before scanning the four options.
