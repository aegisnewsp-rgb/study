# Reading for Comprehension Under Time Pressure

The University of the Philippines does not publish a question count, a per-subtest duration or a weightage table for any of the four UPCAT subtests. The only timing figure it publishes is the whole thing: test administration "will last about five hours". That single fact should shape how you practise. Nobody knows how many Language Proficiency items you get, so the only defensible target is this one — you must be able to keep reading carefully and correctly for five hours without losing your place.

That is a stamina problem before it is a reading problem. Candidates routinely lose more in the last hour than in the first, not because the passages get harder but because attention degrades and they start skipping. If you have never sat for five hours of concentrated reading, your first attempt at the real paper will be slower than your practice, and you will discover that on the day.

## What the subtest actually asks

UP names Language Proficiency as testing English and Filipino together. In practice the items fall into a small number of families, and they reward different reading behaviours.

Some items put a word or a phrase in a sentence and ask you what it means or which word could replace it. Those are solved by reading the whole sentence, not the word. The trap is recognising a morphologically related word — *affect* and *effect*, *complement* and *compliment* — and picking the one that fits the grammar and the sense.

Some items ask you to repair a sentence: pick the correct verb form, fix a misplaced modifier, resolve a pronoun that has lost its referent. These punish reading past the end of the clause. The referent of a pronoun is in the previous sentence, and if you have not read that sentence you cannot answer, however good your grammar is.

Some items ask you to judge which of two or three sentences best combines with a preceding one. That is a cohesion question. The correct answer is the one whose connectives and pronouns pick up the same referents the original used — the one that keeps the actor of the sentence the same.

Some items are pure Filipino. The structure is different enough that English reading habits actively hurt you: Filipino verbs are aspect-oriented and encode the actor in the affix, so subject and object are marked on the verb rather than in the word order, and there is no article system to lean on.

## Practising under a real clock

Take a passage you have not seen, set a timer, and read it once. Do not go back and re-read for the first pass. Then answer the questions. Then re-read once, with the questions visible, and check.

The first pass is the one that counts, because it is the one that reproduces exam conditions. Watching your accuracy jump on the second read tells you nothing useful; what it actually measures is how much of your comprehension came from attention rather than from decoding.

Track the ratio, not just the score. If you get 60 per cent on the first read and 90 on the second, the problem is pacing or stamina, not knowledge — and you should fix it by doing longer timed blocks, not by learning more grammar.

## Three habits that measurably raise this subtest

Read the question first, then find the evidence. For an explicit item this halves the time you spend on the passage. For an inference item it tells you what kind of evidence you are hunting for, which is a single sentence rather than the whole paragraph.

Answer what is asked, not what you can support. Inference items in a language proficiency subtest are frequently more conservative than candidates expect. If the passage says a programme was extended "because of demand from applicants", the safe inference is about demand; the unsafe one is about the programme\'s quality.

Leave a hard item blank and move on. With no published negative-marking rule for UPCAT, the expected-value calculation is genuinely uncertain — but a blank costs you at most the item, while a stall costs you the items after it. On a timed passage the second cost is larger.

## Common mistakes

Reading every word of every sentence, including the parts that cannot carry the answer. Language items are buried in functional text — an instruction paragraph, a short narrative, a set of labelled statements. Skimming the framing is not laziness; it is the skill the subtest assumes.

Translating Filipino into English before you answer. You are being tested in Filipino, so the intermediate step costs you time and introduces error. Answer in the language of the item.

Confusing a near-synonym change with a real one. Two words can be interchangeable in ordinary use and carry different weight in an argument — *some* versus *most*, *may* versus *will*. When an option pair differs only by that word, that word is the whole point of the item.

Treating a grammar item as a memory test. Sentence-level competence is pattern recognition. If you can name why a sentence is wrong, you can usually fix it; if you are only certain that it "feels wrong", you are guessing.
