# General Physical Examination Techniques

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your DOH GP exam.

The general physical examination is the clinician's systematic head-to-toe assessment built on **four cardinal techniques**: **inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation (IPPA)**. The standard sequence is inspect → palpate → percuss → auscultate, but the **abdomen reverses** the last three steps (inspect → auscultate → percuss → palpate) so bowel sounds are not artificially altered.

- **Light palpation** (~1 cm) checks superficial tenderness, temperature, and muscle tone.
- **Deep palpation** (~4 cm) maps organs and deep masses; **bimanual** technique is used for kidneys, spleen, and liver.
- **Diaphragm** of the stethoscope detects high-pitched sounds (breath, bowel, S1, S2); **bell** detects low-pitched sounds (S3, S4, bruits) and must rest lightly.
- Five percussion notes to memorise: resonant, hyper-resonant, tympanic, dull, flat.

Exam traps: sequence reversal on the abdomen, bell-with-pressure, and skipping inspection.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### The IPPA Framework

Inspection is the **first and most underused step**: it requires good lighting, adequate exposure, and bilateral comparison. It documents colour, symmetry, movement, deformities, scars, and respiratory pattern before any touch. Skipping inspection is a common exam trap because findings such as jaundice, unilateral chest expansion, or asymmetry of the lower limbs are visually obvious but frequently missed.

Palpation layers progressively. **Light palpation** (~1 cm) identifies superficial tenderness, skin temperature, moisture, and turgor. **Deep palpation** (~4 cm) assesses organs (liver edge, spleen, kidneys), deep masses, and rebound tenderness. The fingertips, dorsum of the hand (for temperature), and **bimanual** technique (one hand supporting posteriorly while the other presses anteriorly) are standard for retroperitoneal organs.

#### Percussion Notes and Clinical Meaning

Percussion converts tissue density into audible notes; recognition is a frequent DOH MCQ topic.

| Note | Sound quality | Clinical example |
|---|---|---|
| Resonant | Loud, low-pitched, hollow | Normal lung |
| Hyper-resonant | Louder, longer than resonant | Emphysema, pneumothorax |
| Tympanic | Drum-like, musical | Gastric air bubble, pneumoperitoneum |
| Dull | Soft, short, thud-like | Consolidation, pleural effusion, mass |
| Flat | Very soft, dead | Muscle over thigh, bone |

#### Auscultation Rules

Use the **diaphragm firmly** for high-pitched sounds (breath sounds, normal S1/S2, bowel sounds). Use the **bell lightly** for low-pitched sounds (S3, S4, carotid bruits); firm pressure converts the bell into a diaphragm and filters out the very sounds you are listening for.

> **DOH scenario tip:** A 2-mark MCQ often asks which manoeuvre fits a finding — match the percussion note to the suspected pathology before reading the options.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Sequence Exceptions and Their Rationale

The abdomen is the **only region** where palpation and percussion follow auscultation. Stroking or tapping the abdominal wall stimulates peristalsis, which increases bowel-sound frequency and yields a falsely hyperactive reading. The correct order is therefore **inspect → auscultate → percuss → palpate**. The thorax, cardiovascular system, and musculoskeletal system keep the standard **IPPA** order because palpation/percussion do not measurably alter breath or heart sounds.

#### Edge Cases and Common Errors

- **Fist percussion** over the costovertebral angle is reserved for eliciting tenderness in suspected pyelonephritis; it is not a routine manoeuvre.
- **Threshold percussion** is a light technique used to detect deep tenderness, especially over the liver and gallbladder.
- **Warming hands and the stethoscope diaphragm** is not politeness — cold stimulus causes guarding, which masks the very signs you are seeking.
- Failing to compare contralateral sides misses unilateral findings such as reduced breath sounds, asymmetric oedema, or unilateral absent pulses.

#### Infection Control and DOH Scope

DOH standards require **hand hygiene before and after every patient contact**, glove use when exposure to body fluids is anticipated, and a documented chaperone policy for intimate examinations. Verbal informed consent must precede any palpation. When examination findings exceed the GP scope — for example, a new breast mass, suspected deep venous thrombosis requiring duplex ultrasound, or an abnormal neurological deficit — the clinician must initiate **appropriate referral** rather than continue work-up outside scope.

#### Practice Prompts

1. A patient presents with sudden unilateral chest pain and absent breath sounds. List the percussion note you expect over the affected side and the single inspection finding that should have raised suspicion earlier.
2. You are about to auscultate the precordium for an S3. Specify the stethoscope piece you select, the pressure you apply, and the patient position that maximises detection.
