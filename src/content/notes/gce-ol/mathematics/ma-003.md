### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study

The Department of Examinations schedules Mathematics as one of the longest single sessions on the 2026 O/L timetable: Paper I from 08:30 to 10:30 and Paper II from 13:00 to 16:10, so Paper II alone runs for just over three hours. That is unusual for a school-leaving examination and it has a direct effect on how you should revise: this subject rewards sustained working, not speed.

Geometry and mensuration is where the two-paper split bites hardest, because the constructions, circle theorems and trigonometric work in Paper I depend on the ratio and scale work in Paper II.

#### Angles, polygons and circle theorems

Learn the angle rules as consequences of a few facts rather than as a list. The interior angle of a regular $n$-gon is $180 - \dfrac{360}{n}$, and every cyclic-quadrilateral and tangent theorem follows from it. Exterior angle equals the sum of the opposite interior angles. Vertical angles are equal, alternate angles are equal when a transversal crosses parallel lines, co-interior angles sum to 180.

The circle theorems worth knowing without looking up: the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference standing on the same arc; angles in the same segment are equal; opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral sum to 180; the angle between a tangent and a chord equals the angle in the alternate segment.

The last one causes the most errors because the "angle in the alternate segment" is on the far side of the chord, not adjacent to it. Draw the diagram every time.

#### Trigonometry

At this level trigonometry is ratios and right triangles, not functions. The SOH-CAH-TOA ratios and the sine and cosine rules handle most questions.

The cosine rule $a^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc\cos A$ needs no right angle and covers non-right triangles, which is why it is on the syllabus. Use it whenever you know two sides and the included angle, or all three sides. Use the sine rule $a / \sin A = b / \sin B$ when you know one side and one angle and need another side.

The trap is angle mode. A degree symbol means degrees; a bare number means radians. On an O/L paper the two are used deliberately and mixing them is the single most common reason a trigonometric answer comes out as a decimal rather than a clean value.

#### Mensuration

Surface area and volume questions go wrong on units rather than on method. Keep square units for area and cubic for volume, and if the question is in centimetres and the answer you want is in the same units, do not convert twice.

Split solids and cylinders are where the marks are. A truncated pyramid is similar solids: the ratio of lengths is the cube root of the ratio of volumes, and the cube root is the step candidates forget. A cylindrical segment cut by a plane at an angle has volume $r^2 \times$ perpendicular height, not the slant length — using the slant is a classic and produces an answer larger than the cylinder it came from.

#### Constructions

Ruler, protractor, pair of compasses. Build perpendiculars, bisectors, parallel lines, and the standard triangles, and write down the justification for each step. Mark schemes reward the reason, not just the accurate line.

#### Common mistakes

Mixing degrees and radians. Write the unit every single time.

Dividing by the wrong sine. When the sine rule gives two possible triangles you must check the ambiguous case; for the cosine rule a negative answer for a length means you took the supplementary angle wrongly.

Forgetting to square-root the volume ratio in similar solids.

Measuring from a diagram instead of using the given dimensions. Diagrams in exam papers are not to scale.

Assuming a construction is correct because it looks right. The written justification is the mark.

Dropping a unit in a mensuration answer and losing the unit mark that is often explicitly allocated.

### 🔴 Extended — Mastery

#### What a three-hour Paper II does to your revision

Paper II running 13:00–16:10 is unusual for a school-leaving examination and it has a direct consequence: this subject rewards sustained working rather than speed. Geometry and mensuration sits where the two-paper split bites hardest, because the constructions, circle theorems and trigonometric work in Paper I depend on the ratio and scale work in Paper II. Practise holding an argument for three hours, not just producing the first correct step quickly.

#### Derive the angle rules instead of memorising them

Everything on this part of the syllabus follows from one formula. The interior angle of a regular $n$-gon is:

$$
\text{interior angle} = 180 - \frac{360}{n}
$$

Once you hold that, the cyclic-quadrilateral result and the tangent theorem are consequences rather than separate items to recall. The basic line facts sit underneath: exterior angle equals the sum of the opposite interior angles, vertical angles are equal, alternate angles are equal when a transversal crosses parallel lines, and co-interior angles sum to 180.

#### The alternate-segment theorem, and why it causes the most errors

The angle between a tangent and a chord equals the angle in the alternate segment. The failure is nearly always the same: the "angle in the alternate segment" is on the **far side of the chord**, not adjacent to it, so the candidate marks the near angle and gets a supplementary answer. **Draw the diagram every time.** This is a placement error, not a knowledge gap.

#### Choosing between the two rules

| You know | Use | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Two sides and the included angle | Cosine rule $a^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc\cos A$ | Needs no right angle; covers non-right triangles |
| All three sides | Cosine rule | Rearranged form |
| One side and one angle, need another side | Sine rule $\dfrac{a}{\sin A} = \dfrac{b}{\sin B}$ | The ratio form |

The cosine rule is on the syllabus precisely because real triangles on this paper are not right-angled.

#### Angle mode — the most common cause of a wrong answer

A degree symbol means degrees; a bare number means radians. On an O/L paper the two are used **deliberately**, and mixing them is the single most common reason a trigonometric answer comes out as an ugly decimal rather than a clean value. The decimal is the diagnostic: if you expected a tidy value and got 0.6155, check your mode before you check your formula. Write the unit every single time.

#### The ambiguous case, and what a negative answer tells you

The sine rule can give **two possible triangles** for the same data — the ambiguous case — and you must check for it. For the cosine rule, a negative answer for a length means you took the supplementary angle wrongly. Different symptom, same underlying habit: sanity-check the output against what a length must be.

#### Similar solids: the step candidates forget

For similar solids the ratio of lengths is the **cube root** of the ratio of volumes. Candidates take the square root by habit from the linear scale factor, forgetting the step from linear to volumetric. Both directions appear in papers, so state which one the question asks for.

#### The cylinder segment trap

A cylindrical segment cut by a plane at an angle has volume $r^2 \times$ **perpendicular height**, not the slant length. Using the slant produces an answer larger than the cylinder it came from — a free self-check, because no part can exceed the whole. When a question is in centimetres and the answer you want is in the same units, do not convert twice.

#### Diagrams are not drawn to scale

Measuring from a diagram instead of using the given dimensions is a listed fault. In mensuration the marks go on units anyway — square for area, cubic for volume — and a dropped unit is often an explicitly allocated mark, so it is a free mark given away at the end of a correct calculation.

#### Constructions: the reason is the mark

Ruler, protractor, pair of compasses. Build perpendiculars, bisectors, parallel lines and the standard triangles — and **write down the justification for each step**. Mark schemes reward the reason, not just the accurate line. Assuming a construction is correct because it looks right is a named fault.
