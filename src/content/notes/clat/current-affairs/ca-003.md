# Legal News

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Legal News in CLAT's Current Affairs section tests your awareness of recent Indian judicial pronouncements, statutory amendments, ordinances, and constitutional developments reported within the 12-month window before the exam. Carrying roughly **5% weightage** inside the 25-mark Current Affairs block, it appears as passage-based comprehension, factual MCQs, or principle-application items grounded in Supreme Court rulings or new legislation.

The single most-tested idea in 2025–26 is the **transition from the IPC/CrPC/Indian Evidence Act to the BNS, BNSS and BSA**, effective 1 July 2024. Old section numbers no longer apply — a question referencing Section 302 must expect you to recognise it as BNS Section 302, a freshly rewritten provision.

- **Must-read sources**: The Hindu / Indian Express editorials plus LiveLaw, Bar & Bench, and Supreme Court Observer.
- **High-yield topics**: Article 370 abrogation follow-ups, same-sex marriage jurisprudence, PMLA amendments, data privacy (DPDPA), and constitutional bench rulings.
- **Scoring tip**: Always record the bench strength, judge name, citation, and date alongside any judgment you revise.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### What examiners actually test

CLAT frames Legal News items in three formats: factual recall (which court, which section, when), analytical comprehension (a news passage with four inference questions), and principle-application (a recent ruling mapped to a legal maxim). The underlying skill is reading a judgment or statute for its **legal significance**, not its political headline.

#### Building the 12-month ledger

Maintain a running note divided by subject heads: criminal, constitutional, commercial, environmental, family, labour, tax, cyber, and IPR. For every entry, capture the case name, court, judges, statutory provisions interpreted, and the ratio. The Hindu's "Law & Order" column and LiveLaw's daily cause lists are the most efficient feeders.

#### New criminal codes you must internalise

The BNS, BNSS and BSA replaced the colonial-era codes on 1 July 2024. For CLAT 2026, treat the correspondence table below as non-negotiable revision material.

| Old provision | New provision | Subject |
| --- | --- | --- |
| IPC Section 302 | BNS Section 302 | Murder |
| IPC Section 376 | BNS Section 63 | Sexual offences |
| IPC Section 420 | BNS Section 318 | Cheating |
| CrPC Section 161 | BNSS Section 180 | Statement to police |
| Indian Evidence Act Section 65B | BSA Section 63 | Electronic evidence |

#### Distinguishing ratio from obiter

A common exam trap asks which part of a judgment is **binding law**. Only the ratio decidendi — the reason for the decision on the facts — binds lower courts under Article 141. Obiter dicta (observations made in passing) are persuasive at most. Confusing the two is a guaranteed mark-loser.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Mapping news to exam questions

Convert every major story into a potential question. A Supreme Court ruling on Article 32 writs becomes: *"Which Article empowers the apex court to issue writs for enforcement of fundamental rights?"* An ordinance promulgated under Article 123 becomes: *"The President's ordinance-making power is co-extensive with which parliamentary power?"* This micro-conversion forces you to extract the exam-relevant legal kernel rather than memorising the political slug.

#### Edge cases examiners exploit

1. **Law Commission Reports vs enacted law** — a 276th Report recommendation is not binding until Parliament acts; candidates who treat it as law lose marks.
2. **PIL admission vs disposal** — admission of a PIL signals the court's willingness to hear, not its view on merit; interim orders are often misunderstood as final rulings.
3. **Article 142 vs Article 32/226** — Article 142 grants *complete justice* in pending matters and is not a writ jurisdiction; candidates confuse it with habeas or mandamus.
4. **Ordinance lifespan** — an ordinance lapses at six weeks of the next session unless converted into an Act; question stems often test this sunset clause.

#### Common mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it costs marks |
| --- | --- |
| Citing IPC sections post-July 2024 | Shows outdated knowledge of criminal law |
| Quoting obiter as ratio | Misrepresents binding force of precedent |
| Skipping the 12-month window | Tests fall outside the syllabus reference period |
| Ignoring Ordinance Article 123 | Ordinance-making is a tested Parliament topic |
| Naming court without bench strength | Reduces precision expected of a law aspirant |

#### Practice prompts

1. Draft a 150-word note on the *Navtej Singh Johar* lineage (2018–2024) explaining how same-sex marriage rulings in *Supriyo* (2023) interacted with Article 21 and Article 25.
2. Compare PMLA pre-2024 and post-2024 amendment provisions on bail and attachment, citing the Supreme Court's rulings in *Vijay Madanlal Choudhary* and subsequent developments.
