# The Unit of Life: Cells — Leaving Certificate (Established) Biology Notes

Unit 1.3 rests on a single claim: **all life is organised into cells**. Everything from unit 1.1 (the cellular basis of life) through to unit 2.3 (how cells divide and make proteins) assumes it. The specification words this as organisation of life *in increasing complexity* — from a single prokaryotic cell with no internal compartments, through to a multicellular organism in which cells have lost the ability to live independently and depend on one another. That progression is the spine of the unit and it is how Higher-level questions are usually framed.

The learning outcome is deliberately built around *evidence* rather than recall: investigate, using primary data gathered with a light microscope and secondary data gathered from scanning electron microscope imagery, the structures and organelles of animal and plant cells and relate them to their functions. Two consequences follow. First, you must be able to **calculate** from an image — magnification, actual length from a scale bar, resolution. Second, you must be able to **compare** what an LM and an SEM each show you, because the same specimen looks completely different through each. A candidate who can label a diagram but cannot measure from one loses the marks the outcome actually asks for.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Prokaryote.** No membrane-bound nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles. DNA lies free in the cytoplasm in a nucleoid region, and small plasmids are present. Ribosomes are present but smaller (70S) than eukaryotic ribosomes (80S). *Escherichia coli*, *Staphylococcus*, cyanobacteria. Bacteria measure 0.5–5 µm.
- **Eukaryote.** DNA enclosed in a membrane-bound nucleus, with linear chromosomes associated with histone proteins. Membrane-bound organelles: mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, vacuoles. Animals, plants, fungi, protists. Size: 10–100 µm.
- **Animal cell** structures: cell membrane (no cell wall), cytoplasm, nucleus, mitochondrion, ribosome, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, lysosome, centriole. **Plant cell** adds: cellulose cell wall, large permanent vacuole with cell sap, chloroplasts, plasmodesmata. Plant cells also have a more regular rectangular shape.
- **Organelle functions to state under pressure**: mitochondrion — site of aerobic respiration and ATP production, double membrane with internal cristae; chloroplast — site of photosynthesis, contains chlorophyll and thylakoid membranes; ribosome — site of protein synthesis; rough ER — ribosomes attached, makes and transports protein; smooth ER — makes lipids, detoxifies; Golgi apparatus — modifies, sorts and packages proteins; lysosome — contains hydrolytic enzymes for digestion and recycling of worn-out organelles; vacuole — cell sap, storage of water, ions and sugars, maintains turgor pressure which supports the plant; nucleus — contains chromosomes and nucleolus, controls cell activity; cell wall — cellulose, fully permeable, supports and prevents bursting.
- **Cell membrane** is a phospholipid bilayer with proteins — selective, partially permeable, described by the fluid mosaic model.
- **Light microscope** magnification up to about ×1000–1500; resolution about 200 nm, so it can show cells but not most organelles. **Electron microscope** magnification up to about ×500,000; resolution about 0.2 nm, so organelles and viruses become visible. SEM gives a three-dimensional surface view of a specimen; TEM gives a thin-section two-dimensional view of internal structure.
- **Microscope order of use:** set up with the low-power objective first → centre the specimen → raise the objective → focus with the fine adjustment only. **Never** use the coarse adjustment on high power — it will drive the objective through the slide.

#### Examiner traps

- **Saying the cell wall is selectively permeable.** The cell wall is *fully* permeable; it gives shape and support and prevents osmotic bursting. It is the *membrane* that is selectively permeable.
- **Confusing the centriole with the chloroplast.** Centrioles are in animal cells and organise spindle microtubules; chloroplasts are in plant cells and photosynthesise.
- **Claiming prokaryotes have no DNA or no ribosomes.** They have both. What they lack is a *nuclear envelope* and membrane-bound organelles.
- **Using "prokaryote has no chromosomes".** Prokaryotic DNA is a single circular chromosome plus plasmids — chromosomes without a nucleus.
- **Calculating magnification wrong-way-round.** Magnification = image size ÷ actual size. Writing actual = image ÷ magnification loses the mark.
- **Mixing up SEM and TEM.** SEM = surface and three-dimensional. TEM = internal thin section.
- **Saying plants get energy from soil.** Plants get energy from light; they get *carbon* from CO₂ and *minerals* from soil.

#### 30-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Can you list five structures present in plant but not animal cells, with one function each?
- [ ] Can you compare LM and EM on magnification, resolution and what each can show?
- [ ] Can you calculate actual length of an object from an image size and a magnification?
- [ ] Can you write the correct order for using a light microscope and state which knob is forbidden at high power?
- [ ] Can you give one difference and one similarity between a prokaryotic and a eukaryotic cell?

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### Working from images — the calculations that carry marks

**Total magnification.** Objective magnification × eyepiece magnification. A ×40 objective with a ×10 eyepiece gives ×400. Exceeding ×1000 with a light microscope adds no further detail, because the resolution limit, not the magnification, is what stops you.

**Actual size of an object.**

*Example 1.* An image of a cheek epithelial cell is 40 mm long and the total magnification is ×400. What is the actual length?

*Answer.* Actual size = image size ÷ magnification = 40 mm ÷ 400 = **0.1 mm = 100 µm**. A cheek cell at 100 µm is the right order of magnitude, which is your check that the arithmetic is right. The specification expects you to convert to micrometres and give the unit.

*Example 2.* A scanning electron micrograph carries a scale bar labelled 10 µm which measures 25 mm on the page. The nucleus in the same image measures 5 mm. What is the diameter of the nucleus?

*Answer.* Scale factor = 25 mm ÷ 10 µm = 2.5 mm per µm. Nucleus = 5 mm ÷ 2.5 = **2 µm**. Always use the scale bar rather than magnification on an electron micrograph, because SEM images are reproduced at arbitrary print sizes.

**Resolution.** The best resolution with a light microscope is about 200 nm. Anything smaller than that merges into its neighbour. This single fact explains three separate observations that students otherwise try to memorise: why you cannot see ribosomes on a light micrograph (25–30 nm), why you cannot see viruses (20–300 nm, mostly below the limit), and why the detail in an LM image looks soft and grey.

#### Preparing and mounting a slide

The specification names this explicitly, and it is the easiest experimental question on the paper to answer well.

1. **Take a clean slide and a coverslip.** Smear a drop of water on the slide for a wet mount of plant material; use no water for cheek cells, which are already in a film of saliva.
2. **Place the material in the drop** and lower the coverslip at an angle onto it — at an angle, with a mounted needle in the drop — so air bubbles are not trapped.
3. **If staining**, add one drop of stain (methylene blue or iodine) at the edge of the coverslip and draw it through by capillary action using paper towel. Do not flood the slide.
4. **Place the slide on the stage**, start with low power, centre the specimen, then increase power, focusing with the **fine adjustment only** at high power.
5. **Common faults to name in an evaluation:** air bubbles (irregular dark-edged circles), material too thick, stain too dark, specimen moving when the coarse focus is used.

#### Prokaryote versus eukaryote

| Feature | Prokaryotic cell | Eukaryotic cell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nucleus | Absent; DNA free in cytoplasm | Membrane-bound nucleus |
| Chromosomes | One circular, plus plasmids | Linear, wrapped around histones |
| Organelles | No membrane-bound organelles | Mitochondria, ER, Golgi, lysosomes |
| Ribosomes | 70S, smaller | 80S, larger |
| Size | 0.5–5 µm | 10–100 µm |
| Division | Binary fission | Mitosis or meiosis |
| Examples | Bacteria, archaea, cyanobacteria | Animals, plants, fungi, protists |

Archaea are prokaryotic but differ from bacteria in their cell wall, membrane lipids and ribosomes, which is why the specification places them in a separate domain. **Prokaryotic and eukaryotic** is a classification based on cell type; **archaea, bacteria and eukaryota** is a domain classification. Questions swap these deliberately.

#### Complexity in multicellular organisms

In a multicellular organism, differentiation means cells of the same organism have different structures because different genes are switched on or off in each cell — a neurone and a muscle cell contain the same genome but express different subsets of it. Cells lose the ability to live independently and gain intercellular signalling, specialised tissues and organs. Because no single cell carries out all the functions, an injury to one tissue can affect the whole organism — and because differentiation is produced by switching genes rather than changing them, the underlying DNA is still identical in every cell of the body.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Reading a primary micrograph critically

The Biology in Practice Investigation requires primary data, and unit 1.3 is the unit where a light microscope appears most frequently. The evaluation marks are earned by saying something specific about the imagery, not by writing "human error".

- **Systematic vs random error in imaging.** A graticule inserted into the eyepiece lets you calibrate once and then measure many fields without recalibrating. Not doing this is a *systematic* error, because it biases every reading in the same direction.
- **Sampling.** Ten randomly selected fields is a poor sample of a whole tissue; the field positions should be systematic and the count large enough that the count of cells stabilises as fields are added.
- **Why use more than one stain.** Iodine shows starch but not nuclei; methylene blue shows nuclei but not starch. No single stain reveals everything, so a comparative approach gets more out of the same slide.
- **Comparing LM primary data with SEM secondary data.** The two are not in competition: an SEM image shows true surface topography in three dimensions, whereas a stained light micrograph shows stained components in two dimensions but allows you to see the whole cell at once. The specification asks you to relate both to function, so the stronger answer states what *each* technique contributes.

#### Worked Higher-level questions with answers

**Q1.** A student claims a structure visible on a light micrograph at ×400 is a mitochondrion. Identify the error and correct it.
*A.* The error is that resolution, not magnification, limits a light microscope to about 200 nm. Mitochondria are roughly 0.5–2 µm wide — they *are* above the limit and can be seen in some cells with suitable stains, but the claim is unsupported because no stain is named and no organelle-specific evidence is given. The correction is to state the resolution limit, name the stain used, and identify structures by their actual dimensions. If the structure measured 30 nm, no light microscope could resolve it and the correct answer would be "it cannot be a mitochondrion".

**Q2.** Explain why a plant cell placed in pure water does not burst.
*A.* Water enters by osmosis down a water potential gradient, increasing turgor pressure inside the cell. The rigid cellulose cell wall is placed under increasing mechanical pressure and, because plant cells cannot contract, the combination of turgor pressure pushing out from the cytoplasm and the wall pushing in from outside balances. Without the wall the cell would lyse — this is the osmotic basis of food preservation in salt and sugar solutions, and of plasmolysis, where the protoplast pulls away from the wall.

**Q3.** Two cells from the same animal have identical DNA but different structures. Explain.
*A.* The cells have differentiated. Different genes have been switched on or off in each cell during development, so the two cells synthesise different proteins and therefore have different organelles and different shapes, even though the genome — and hence the information for making every protein — is identical. This is why most of a differentiated cell's DNA is not expressed, and why a skin cell can be used to produce a cloned animal with an identical genome.

#### How this unit connects forward

Unit 2.2 requires you to relate the **internal structures of mitochondria and chloroplasts** to photosynthesis and respiration — the cristae and the thylakoid membranes both carry the electron transport chains and ATP synthase named in the specification. Unit 2.6 covers how substances actually cross that membrane. Unit 3.2 examines the bacterial cell in detail. A firm grip on organelle function here is what makes those three units cost an hour rather than three.

#### Mistakes students actually make

1. **Listing organelles without functions.** The mark scheme reads the function, not the label. Pair every structure with a verb.
2. **Saying the nucleus controls everything.** More precise: the nucleus stores the genetic material and controls which proteins are made, and through those proteins the cell's activities.
3. **Forgetting ribosomes sit on the rough ER as well as free in the cytoplasm.** Both make protein; only the membrane-bound ones make it for export.
4. **Omitting the resolution argument.** When a question asks why an organelle was or was not visible, quote the 200 nm limit and the organelle's actual size.
5. **Ignoring the scale bar on electron micrographs.** Magnification labels on published images assume a print size that may not be yours.
6. **Treating "image too blurry" as an explanation for everything.** Blurriness is a focusing fault; faintness may be a stain concentration problem; a partly visible field is a sampling problem. Name the specific fault.
