# Progressions

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **progression** is an ordered sequence generated by a fixed rule. JEE Advanced restricts the scope to four families:

- **Arithmetic Progression (AP):** constant *common difference* $d$; $T_n = a + (n-1)d$, $S_n = \frac{n}{2}[2a+(n-1)d]$.
- **Geometric Progression (GP):** constant *common ratio* $r$; $T_n = a r^{n-1}$, $S_n = \dfrac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}$ for $r \neq 1$.
- **Harmonic Progression (HP):** reciprocals form an AP; harmonic mean $H = \dfrac{2ab}{a+b}$.
- **Arithmetic-Geometric Progression (AGP):** termwise product of an AP and a GP, summed via the **method of differences**.

For positive $a, b$: $A \geq G \geq H$, with $A \cdot H = G^2 = ab$. Standard sums to memorise: $\sum k = \dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$, $\sum k^2 = \dfrac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}$, $\sum k^3 = \left[\dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}\right]^2$.

**High-yield exam pointers:** AGP telescoping, $|r|<1$ convergence for infinite GP, AM-GM-HM chains, and the method of differences for higher-order polynomial/GP-type sequences.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Definitions and Identifying a Progression
A sequence $\{T_n\}$ is an **progression** when a single parameter (the common difference or ratio) controls its growth. To classify an unknown sequence, compute first differences $\Delta T_n = T_{n+1} - T_n$; constant $\Delta T_n$ confirms an AP. For a GP, check the ratio $T_{n+1}/T_n$ is independent of $n$. A constant second difference $\Delta^2 T_n = 0$ identifies a linear AP-style term.

#### Means of Two Positive Numbers
For $a, b > 0$:

| Mean | Formula | Relation |
|------|---------|----------|
| Arithmetic | $A = \dfrac{a+b}{2}$ | $A \geq G$ |
| Geometric | $G = \sqrt{ab}$ | $G^2 = ab$ |
| Harmonic | $H = \dfrac{2ab}{a+b}$ | $A \cdot H = G^2$ |

Inserting $n$ means between $a$ and $b$ in an AP gives the $k$-th inserted mean as $a + \dfrac{k(b-a)}{n+1}$. For GMs, the inserted term multiplies $a$ by $r^k$ where $r = \left(\dfrac{b}{a}\right)^{1/(n+1)}$.

#### Sum Formulas and Convergence
The finite GP sum $S_n = a\dfrac{1-r^n}{1-r}$ requires $r \neq 1$. The infinite sum $S_\infty = \dfrac{a}{1-r}$ is valid **only** when $|r|<1$; otherwise the geometric series diverges. Students often plug $r=2$ into $a/(1-r)$ and obtain a misleading finite value — the formula is undefined for $|r|\geq 1$.

#### Method of Differences
For a sequence whose $k$-th finite difference is a known progression (AP, GP, or constant), sum by writing $S_n$, applying the same recurrence to $S_n$, then eliminating. This handles $\sum n^2$, $\sum n^3$, and any polynomial-in-$n$ sum in one pass.

#### Typical JEE Patterns
- Direct MCQ on $T_n$ or $S_n$ with parameter traps ($r=1$, $|r|\geq 1$).
- NAT answers for the sum of an AGP evaluated at a specific $n$.
- Linked questions using $1^2 + 2^2 + \cdots + n^2$ inside a binomial expansion.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Arithmetic-Geometric Progression Summation
For $S_n = a + (a+d)r + (a+2d)r^2 + \cdots + [a+(n-1)d]\,r^{n-1}$, compute both $S_n$ and $r S_n$:

$$S_n - r S_n = a + d\,r\,\frac{1-r^{n-1}}{1-r} - [a+(n-1)d]\,r^{n}.$$

The geometric-tail collapses after subtraction, leaving a fresh AP in powers of $r$ that you re-sum with the GP formula. This is the workhorse behind sums such as $\sum n \cdot 2^{-n}$ and $\sum (2n-1) \cdot 3^n$ — both recurring JEE numerical-answer-type (NAT) items.

#### Edge Cases and Recurrences
A recursion $T_{n+1} = p\,T_n + q$ is *not* a GP. Substitute $U_n = T_n + \dfrac{q}{p-1}$ (for $p \neq 1$) to expose $U_{n+1} = p\,U_n$, a true GP. For $p=1$, the sequence is linear (AP). Missing this conversion step is the most common trap in MCQs framed around recursive definitions.

#### Method of Differences in Depth
Given $\{T_n\}$, form successive differences $\Delta T_n, \Delta^2 T_n, \ldots$ until you reach a constant or a familiar progression. If $\Delta^k T_n = c$ (constant), then $T_n$ is a polynomial of degree $k$ in $n$ and $\sum T_n$ is a polynomial of degree $k+1$. If $\Delta^k T_n$ is geometric in $n$, apply the AGP technique to the highest surviving level, then integrate back via telescoping sums.

#### Common Mistakes
- Using $S_n = \dfrac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}$ with $r=1$; reduce to $S_n = na$ first.
- Forgetting to reciprocate when finding $n$-th term of an HP from its AP form.
- Swapping $T_n$ and $S_n$ in statement reading; NAT items penalise this harshly.
- Treating $\sum n r^n$ directly as a GP; it is an AGP with $a=1, d=1, r=r$.

#### Worked Micro-Example
Let $S_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (2k+1)\cdot 3^{k-1}$. Then $a=3, d=2, r=3$ (re-indexed). Apply $S_n - 3S_n$: $ -2S_n = 3 + 2\cdot 3 \cdot \dfrac{1-3^{n-1}}{1-3} - (2n+1)\cdot 3^n$, giving $S_n = \dfrac{(2n-1)\cdot 3^n + 3}{4}$. At $n=4$: $S_4 = \dfrac{7\cdot 81 + 3}{4} = \dfrac{570}{4} = 142.5$.

#### Exam Strategy (5% weightage)
Expect 1–2 standalone MCQs and 1–2 linked NATs per paper. Allocate 4–5 minutes per direct question; linked items inside Binomial or Probability fall to the parent topic's timer.

#### Practice Prompts
1. If $S_n = 1\cdot 2 + 2\cdot 2^2 + \cdots + n\cdot 2^n$, find $S_5$ in closed form using the AGP trick.
2. Three numbers are in GP. If 8 is added to the middle term, the new triple is in AP with sum 30. Find the original numbers.
