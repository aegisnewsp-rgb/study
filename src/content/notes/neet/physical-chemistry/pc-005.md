# States of Matter

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

- **Three classical states** of matter — **solid, liquid, gas** — are distinguished by the balance between **intermolecular forces** (cohesive) and **thermal kinetic energy** (dispersive). When forces dominate, the substance is condensed; when thermal energy wins, it is gaseous.
- **Ideal gas equation** is the single most-tested formula: **PV = nRT**, where P in atm, V in L, n in mol, R = 0.0821 L·atm·K⁻¹·mol⁻¹, T in K. Memorise the value of R in SI as **8.314 J·K⁻¹·mol⁻¹** for numerical problems in energy units.
- **Gas laws**: Boyle's (P∝1/V, T const), **Charles's** (V∝T, P const), **Gay-Lussac's** (P∝T, V const), and **Avogadro's** (V∝n). NEET regularly asks the combined form **P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂**.
- **Real gases** deviate from ideality; the **van der Waals equation** **(P + a/V²)(V − b) = RT** (per mole) introduces constants **a** (attraction) and **b** (excluded volume).
- **Dalton's law** of partial pressures: **P_total = Σ Pᵢ**; **Graham's law**: rate ratio = √(M₂/M₁).
- **Compressibility factor Z = PV/nRT**: Z = 1 ideal, Z < 1 at low T (attractions), Z > 1 at high P (repulsions).
- **High-yield traps**: forgetting to convert °C → K; mixing up Graham's law (heavier gas diffuses *slower*); confusing **boiling point** with the temperature where vapour pressure = external pressure.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Arrangement and Properties of the Three States
In a **solid**, particles occupy fixed lattice positions with only vibrational motion, giving a definite **shape and volume**, very low **compressibility**, and the highest **density**. A **liquid** retains a definite volume but conforms to the container's shape; particles can translate past one another, producing **fluidity** and measurable **viscosity** and **surface tension**. A **gas** has neither fixed shape nor volume, fills the entire container, is highly **compressible**, and has the lowest density. The macroscopic differences trace directly to the **average intermolecular separation** (≈ solid < liquid < gas) and to the relative magnitudes of cohesive forces vs. **kT** (thermal energy per degree of freedom).

#### Gas Laws — Derivation and Use
Each empirical law is a special case of **PV = nRT**:
- **Boyle's law** (T, n constant): P₁V₁ = P₂V₂. A plot of P vs. 1/V is linear through the origin.
- **Charles's law** (P, n constant): V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂. Volume extrapolates to zero at T = 0 K (absolute zero).
- **Gay-Lussac's law** (V, n constant): P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂.
- **Avogadro's law** (P, T constant): V/n = constant = 22.4 L mol⁻¹ at STP (273.15 K, 1 bar).

The **combined gas equation** P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ and the **ideal gas density form** P = (dRT)/M (where d = mass/V, M = molar mass) appear in NEET numericals asking for molecular mass from gas density.

#### Mixture Laws
**Dalton's law of partial pressures**: for non-reacting gases, **P_total = P_A + P_B + …**, with **Pᵢ = xᵢP_total** (mole fraction form). **Graham's law of diffusion/effusion**: **r₁/r₂ = √(M₂/M₁)**. Applied problems usually give two gases and ask for the rate ratio or time ratio (t ∝ √M).

#### Real Gases and the Compressibility Factor
No real gas obeys PV = nRT exactly. **van der Waals equation** for n moles:
**(P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT**, where **a** corrects for intermolecular attractions (lowers effective pressure) and **b** corrects for the finite volume of molecules (reduces free volume). The **compressibility factor Z = PV/nRT** is the diagnostic for ideality: Z = 1 ideal, Z < 1 dominates at low T (N₂, CO₂, CH₄ just above their **Boyle temperature**), Z > 1 at very high P. NEET typically asks to identify which gas is most/least ideal at a given condition, or to compute Z from given P, V, T, n.

#### Key Numerical Form
A useful rearrangement is **M = dRT/P**. Given d in g L⁻¹, P in atm, R = 0.0821 L·atm·K⁻¹·mol⁻¹, T in K, M comes out in g mol⁻¹. Always convert Celsius to **kelvin** first.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### The Intermolecular Force–Kinetic Energy Balance
Each state is a thermodynamic phase, defined by a region of (P, T) space bounded by **phase-transition curves**. On a P–T diagram the **triple point** is the unique condition where solid, liquid, and gas coexist; the **critical point** marks the end of the liquid–vapour coexistence curve, above which only a supercritical fluid exists. The slope of the solid–liquid line (Clausius–Clapeyron) is negative for water (ice is less dense than liquid water due to open H-bonded tetrahedral structure) and positive for most other substances.

#### Why the van der Waals Constants Vary
The constant **a** scales with the strength of pairwise attractive forces; it is largest for easily polarisable, large-electron-cloud gases (Cl₂, CO₂) and for hydrogen-bonded vapours (H₂O, NH₃). The constant **b** scales with molecular size; it correlates well with the **van der Waals volume** derived from crystallographic radii. NEET occasionally asks: "Arrange CO₂, H₂, N₂ in order of increasing a." The answer hinges on **polarisability** and H-bonding, not on molecular mass.

#### Boiling Point, Vapour Pressure, and External Pressure
A liquid boils when its **saturated vapour pressure equals the external pressure**. Hence boiling point drops at higher altitudes (lower atmospheric pressure). **Raoult's law** for an ideal solution: **P_A = x_A P°_A** — the partial pressure of a component equals its mole fraction times the pure-component vapour pressure. This is the conceptual bridge to the **Solutions** chapter.

#### Common Mistakes and Exam Strategy
- **Sign of work in isothermal expansion**: w = −nRT ln(V₂/V₁) is **negative** for expansion; the magnitude is what gases "do" on the surroundings.
- **STP confusion**: NEET has shifted between 0 °C/1 atm (older) and 0 °C/1 bar (IUPAC). The molar volume is **22.4 L** at 1 atm and **22.7 L** at 1 bar; check which standard the question uses.
- **Graham's law direction**: heavier gas → slower rate → **longer** time. A common trap asks "gas A takes 4× the time of gas B" and expects √(M_A/M_B) = 4.
- **Compressibility factor interpretation**: at the **Boyle temperature** T_B = a/(Rb), Z ≈ 1 over a wide pressure range — Boyle's law is obeyed even by real gases at this special temperature.

#### Practice Prompts
1. A 2.0 L flask at 300 K contains 0.50 mol of a gas with van der Waals constants a = 3.6 L²·atm·mol⁻² and b = 0.04 L·mol⁻¹. Estimate the pressure using the van der Waals equation and compare it with the ideal-gas value. (Answer: V − nb = 1.98 L; (P + 0.45 atm)(1.98) ≈ 6.15 → P ≈ 2.66 atm ideal vs ≈ 2.65 atm vdW; the correction is small because conditions are mild.)
2. A H₂–O₂ mixture has P_H₂ = 0.40 atm, P_O₂ = 0.60 atm. A pinhole allows effusion; what is the ratio of H₂ to O₂ escaping per second? (Answer: r_H₂/r_O₂ = √(32/2) = 4; hydrogen escapes 4× faster.)
