# Main Point & Primary Purpose Questions

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **Main Point** question asks you to identify the single claim the entire passage is built to support. A **Primary Purpose** question asks *why* the author wrote the passage — the motivation behind the writing. Both reward **passage-wide** thinking; any choice that is true of only one paragraph or one example is automatically suspect.

The correct answer is the option whose truth **requires and is supported by every section** of the passage, framed at moderate scope. Strip extreme words like *always*, *only*, *never*, and *completely* before you commit — passages rarely justify absolute claims.

- The **main point** is the **what**: the central thesis the author defends.
- The **primary purpose** is the **why**: explain, argue, criticize, evaluate, describe, compare, refute, or propose.
- **Distractors** fall into four families: too narrow (one paragraph only), topic-not-claim, opposite/inverted stance, or structural description mistaken for purpose.

| Question stem | What it really asks |
| --- | --- |
| "The main point of the passage is…" | Central thesis of the whole passage |
| "The author's primary purpose is to…" | Why the author wrote this passage |
| "Which of the following best expresses the passage…" | Passage-spanning claim at moderate scope |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Distinguishing the Two Stems

Although **Main Point** and **Primary Purpose** questions look similar, they test different cognitive moves. The *main point* identifies the **substantive claim** — what the passage asserts. The *primary purpose* identifies the **author's intent** — what the author is using the passage to accomplish. A passage can be primarily intended to *critique a theory* even when its main point is a moderate claim about that theory's limits.

#### Anatomy of the Correct Answer

A correct answer must satisfy three tests simultaneously:

1. **Passage-spanning** — every paragraph, chart, or example must relate to that claim.
2. **Moderately scoped** — specific enough to be falsifiable, broad enough to encompass every section.
3. **Supported** — the passage contains evidence (not necessarily the words) that backs the claim.

#### Distractor Families

| Distractor type | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Partial truth | A claim true of only paragraph 2 or only one example |
| Topic confusion | Names the subject matter, not the author's thesis |
| Opposite | Inverts or contradicts the central argument |
| Structure-as-purpose | Says the passage "contrasts X and Y" rather than explaining *why* |
| Overgeneralization | Uses "always," "only," "every," "completely" beyond what the passage shows |

#### Common Traps

- Picking the **most memorable or vivid** sentence instead of the most central one.
- Treating a **structural description** ("contrasts," "chronicles") as a primary purpose — structure is *how*; purpose is *why*.
- Choosing an answer that names the **topic** (e.g., "the passage is about judicial review") instead of the **claim** (e.g., "the passage argues that judicial review has expanded beyond its original limits").

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### The Scope Test in Practice

For every candidate answer, mentally strip the passage down to a single paragraph. If the answer still reads true with only that paragraph present, it is **too narrow**. If it reads true with the *opposite* of the passage's evidence, it is an **inversion**. The correct answer should feel slightly **understated** — qualified, hedged, and aligned with the author's exact degree of certainty.

#### Connection to Adjacent RC Question Types

Main-point and primary-purpose questions are the **anchors** of every other RC question. Tone, inference, function, and strengthen/weaken items all depend on correctly identifying the passage's spine. If you misread the central claim, every downstream answer is suspect. Treat these questions as **orientation devices** — when you meet them, slow down and resolve the passage's thesis before attempting the remaining items.

#### Common Mistakes Specific to LSAT India Timing

- **Speed bias:** Main-point questions often sit first or last in a passage set; students rush them, mistaking ease for triviality. A wrong main-point answer corrupts the entire passage set.
- **Extreme-language blindness:** LSAT writers deliberately inject "only," "every," "always," and "completely" into wrong answers to bait students who skip a careful read.
- **Structure vs. purpose slippage:** Passages that *compare* two theorists are not primarily intended "to compare" — they are usually intended "to evaluate," "to argue that one is superior," or "to explain a divergence."

#### Worked Micro-Example

Suppose a passage has three paragraphs: paragraph 1 describes a regulatory gap, paragraph 2 critiques prior scholarly responses, paragraph 3 proposes a new framework. The main point is **not** "there is a regulatory gap" (only paragraph 1) nor "prior scholarship is flawed" (only paragraph 2) — it is the integration: *the passage argues that prior responses to the regulatory gap are inadequate and proposes a new framework.* That claim is supported by all three paragraphs.

#### Practice Prompts

1. After reading any RC passage, write the main point in one sentence **without** using extreme words ("always," "only"). Compare against the author's concluding paragraph.
2. Take a passage whose author *criticizes* a theory. Confirm that the correct primary-purpose verb is *criticize* or *evaluate*, never *describe* or *explain* — and explain why each alternative fails.
