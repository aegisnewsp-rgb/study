# Fill in Blanks

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Fill in the Blanks in CLAT English is a cloze-test item where one or more words are deleted from a sentence or short passage and four options are given. The correct choice must satisfy three checks at once: **vocabulary in context**, **grammar**, and **collocation**. Each question carries 1 mark with a negative marking of 0.25, so blind guessing is costly.

- **Vocabulary in context** — pick the word whose meaning fits the surrounding sentence, not the one that sounds familiar in isolation.
- **Grammar-fit** — the blank must agree with the subject (number, person, tense) and sit correctly between its neighbours.
- **Collocation** — common pairings like *commit a crime*, *heavy rain*, *keen on* must be matched, not paraphrased.

> **Tip:** Always read the *complete* sentence after mentally inserting each option before locking your answer.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### What the question actually tests

CLAT fillers are not vocabulary-recall MCQs. They test whether you can read a sentence as a whole unit and judge which of four candidates (a) fits the grammar slot, (b) carries the right register and meaning, and (c) collocates naturally with the surrounding words. Roughly 4–6 such items appear in the English section, which itself contributes about 20% of the 150-mark paper.

#### Categories of blanks you will see

| Category | What is deleted | Example clue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vocabulary | A content word (noun/verb/adjective) | "The verdict was ________ by the bench." (pronounced / pronounced / enunciated / voiced) |
| Articles & determiners | a, an, the, zero | "She is ________ honest officer." (a / an / the / no word) |
| Prepositions | in, on, at, with, of, for, to | "He is eligible ________ the post." (to / for / of / with) |
| Tense / modal | Verb form or auxiliary | "If she had studied, she ________ passed." (will have / would have / has / had) |
| Conjunction | and, but, although, because | "He was tired ________ he kept working." (and / yet / so / for) |
| Idiom / phrase | A fixed multi-word unit | "He brought the matter ________ light." (to / in / into / under) |

#### Step-by-step elimination method

1. Read the full sentence; identify the **part of speech** the blank needs.
2. Mentally slot each option; cross out any that break grammar (wrong plural, wrong tense, wrong article sound).
3. Among the survivors, choose the one whose meaning and collocation fit the context.
4. Re-read the completed sentence aloud — awkward phrasing usually flags the wrong pick.

> **Trap:** Polysemous words (e.g., *conduct*) tempt candidates to pick the rarer meaning. CLAT almost always tests the contextual, not the dictionary-first, sense.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge cases that decide the top 1%

Three categories separate a 90%iler from a 99%iler in Fill in the Blanks:

- **Phonetic article rules:** *a university* (consonant /juː/ sound) but *an hour* (silent *h*), *an MBA* (vowel letter sequence pronounced /e/). Always sound the word, don't just look at the first letter.
- **Confusables:** *affect* (verb, usually) vs *effect* (noun, usually); *fewer* (countable) vs *less* (uncountable); *principal* (chief / head of school) vs *principle* (rule). CLAT frequently places these in pairs.
- **Preposition after adjectives/verbs:** *angry with* a person, *angry at* a thing; *married to*, *capable of*, *interested in*, *good at*. These are not interchangeable even when both feel natural.

#### Worked micro-example

Sentence: *"The committee, after much deliberation, decided to ________ the proposal until the next session."*
Options: (a) lay off (b) lay down (c) table (d) put up

- "Lay off" means dismiss — wrong meaning.
- "Lay down" means establish a rule — wrong meaning.
- "Put up" means tolerate or erect — wrong meaning.
- "Table the proposal" is a fixed collocation meaning to postpone formal discussion — correct.

#### Common mistakes and how to avoid them

1. Choosing by standalone meaning rather than sentence fit.
2. Ignoring subject–verb agreement when the blank sits next to the verb.
3. Misapplying prepositions after common adjectives and verbs.
4. Picking an option that is grammatically valid but breaks collocation.
5. Skimming the sentence and missing negative cues (*not*, *rarely*, *hardly*) that flip the meaning.

> **Strategy:** Spend 45–60 seconds per item. Read the entire sentence before looking at the options, because option-order bias is a real source of error.

1. **Practice prompt A:** Identify the part of speech, then choose the best article for *"She is ________ European engineer."* (Answer logic: *a*, because *European* begins with /j/.)
2. **Practice prompt B:** Fill *depend ________* with the correct preposition and explain why each wrong option fails. (Answer: *on*, because *depend on* is the fixed collocation; *of*, *from*, *to* are all common distractor traps.)
