# Classification

Classification is not a memorisation chapter — it is a **decision-procedure** chapter. Every question here reduces to one of four looks: *which block is this element in, which functional group is this molecule, which acid–base theory does this reaction fit, and which of these two named substances is which.* Get the four lookups right and the chapter is finished.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

| If the question gives you | Look at | Decide by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An element or its configuration | The subshell receiving the last electron | s, p, (n−1)d, (n−2)f → the block |
| An organic compound | The functional group present | –OH, –CHO, –CO–, –COOH, –NH₂, –X, C–O–C |
| An acid or base | What is being transferred | H⁺ (Arrhenius), proton (Brønsted), electron pair (Lewis) |
| Two properties of an element | Z_eff, shielding, and electron configuration | explains every periodic trend |

**The four trends, in one table**

| Property | Across a period (left → right) | Down a group |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Atomic radius | Decreases | Increases |
| Ionisation enthalpy | Increases, with two dips | Decreases |
| Electronegativity | Increases | Decreases |
| Metallic / non-metallic character | Metallic falls, non-metallic rises | Metallic rises |

**The two ionisation-enthalpy dips** in period 2 and 3: at Group 2 the valence configuration is ns², a filled s subshell, so Be and Mg sit below the trend. At Group 15 it is ns²np³, a half-filled p subshell, so N, P and As sit below the trend. Both dips are extra stability from a filled or half-filled subshell, and the same fact explains why Group 18 has the highest ionisation enthalpy in the period.

**Three facts that decide whole topics**
- **Zn, Cd and Hg are d-block but not transition metals**, because their d subshell is filled in the atom and in every common ion. A transition element needs a partly filled d subshell in at least one common oxidation state.
- **Phenol versus alcohol** turns on one test: is the –OH attached directly to an aromatic ring? Yes → phenol. No → alcohol.
- **Ethers never contain –OH.** C₂H₅–O–C₂H₅ is diethyl ether, named as an **alkoxyalkane** (methoxyalkane family), not with a suffix.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Classifying matter, and why the distinction earns its keep

Matter is **pure** or a **mixture**. Pure substances have fixed composition and sharp melting and boiling points; they are elements or compounds. Mixtures are not chemically combined, so each component keeps its own properties, and they separate by physical means — which is the test. Distillation, fractional distillation, chromatography, sublimation and crystallization are all mixture-separation techniques; none of them breaks a chemical bond, and none of them will ever separate a compound into its elements.

**Homogeneous** mixtures are uniform at every scale (air, salt solution, glucose in water). **Heterogeneous** mixtures show distinguishable phases (sand and water, oil and water, chalk and water). Colloids sit in between: particles are 1–1000 nm, they scatter light (Tyndall effect) and they do not settle on standing, which is why a colloid looks milky and a true solution does not.

#### Periodic block classification

The block of an element is fixed by **which subshell receives the differentiating electron** — the last electron that distinguishes it from the previous element.

| Block | Groups | Differentiating electron | Character | Oxidation states |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| s | 1, 2 | ns¹, ns² | Soft, reactive metals, low density, low melting point | One fixed state: +1, or +2 |
| p | 13–18 | ns²np¹–⁶ | Widest diversity: metals, non-metals, metalloids | Varies, usually differing by 2 |
| d | 3–12 | (n−1)d¹–¹⁰ | High density, high melting point, coloured ions, complex formation | Variable, differing by 1 |
| f | Lanthanoids, actinoids | (n−2)f¹–¹⁴ | Inner electrons fill, so members are almost identical chemically | +3 dominant, wider for actinoids |

The d-block earns its place in this syllabus for two reasons: variable oxidation states (Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺, Cu⁺/Cu²⁺, Cr²⁺ through Cr⁶⁺) and coloured ions from **d–d transitions**. Zn²⁺ is colourless precisely because d¹⁰ has no available empty d orbital to absorb into.

**f-block:** the 4f and 5f subshells fill inside the lanthanoid and actinoid series. Because the f electrons are shielded, the chemical properties of successive lanthanoids are nearly identical, which is precisely why separation of lanthanoids is difficult. Actinoids differ more because the 5f electrons are not as effectively shielded, so radioactivity and a wider range of oxidation states both increase.

#### Periodic trends, explained rather than listed

**Across a period,** protons are added to the nucleus while shielding by inner electrons stays almost constant, so effective nuclear charge Z_eff rises. Stronger pull on the same shell → smaller radius, harder to remove an electron → higher ionisation enthalpy, more electronegative, more non-metallic.

**Down a group,** a new shell is added at each step and inner-electron shielding increases, so the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded. Radius increases, ionisation enthalpy falls, electronegativity falls, metallic character rises.

The exceptions are where a partly-filled subshell is more stable than the trend predicts: Be (1s²2s²) > B (1s²2s²2p¹) in the first ionisation enthalpy, and the same pattern at N > O, Mg > Al, P > S.

#### Organic functional-group classification

| Group | Structure | Class | IUPAC form | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| –OH on sp³ carbon | R–OH | Alcohol | alkan**-ol** | CH₃CH₂OH, ethanol |
| –OH on aromatic ring | Ar–OH | Phenol | phenol / hydroxybenzene | C₆H₅OH, phenol |
| –CHO | R–CHO | Aldehyde | alkan**-al** | CH₃CHO, ethanal |
| >C=O between two carbons | R–CO–R | Ketone | alkan**-one** | CH₃COCH₃, propanone |
| –COOH | R–COOH | Carboxylic acid | alkan**-oic acid** | CH₃COOH, ethanoic acid |
| –NH₂ | R–NH₂ | Amine | alkan**-amine** | CH₃NH₂, methanamine |
| –X (F, Cl, Br, I) | R–X | Haloalkane / haloarene | halo prefix | CH₃Cl, chloromethane |
| C–O–C | R–O–R | Ether | alkoxy + alkane | CH₃OCH₃, methoxymethane |
| >C=C< | Alkene | | alkan**-e** / ene | CH₂=CH₂, ethene |
| –C≡C– | Alkyne | | alkan**-yne** | CH≡CH, ethyne |

**Primary, secondary, tertiary alcohols** are counted by the number of **carbon atoms attached to the carbon bearing the –OH**, not by the number of –OH groups. Butan-1-ol (–CH₂OH at the end) is primary. Butan-2-ol (–CH(OH)–, attached to two carbons) is secondary. 2-methylpropan-2-ol (–C(OH)(CH₃)₂, attached to three carbons) is tertiary. A molecule with three –OH groups is a **triol** and nothing is said about primary/secondary/tertiary.

#### Acids, bases and salts — three theories, nested not competing

**Arrhenius** is the narrowest: in aqueous solution, an acid produces H₃O⁺ and a base produces OH⁻. It works for HCl, NaOH and H₂SO₄, and fails for NH₃ (no OH⁻ in the formula, yet it is a base) and for any non-aqueous system.

**Brønsted–Lowry** widens the frame to any solvent: an acid **donates a proton**, a base **accepts one**. Each proton transfer produces a **conjugate pair** — HCl/Cl⁻, H₂O/OH⁻, NH₃/NH₄⁺. This immediately explains why the reaction works both ways: HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻ (HCl donates), and the reverse (H₂O donates to Cl⁻, which is why chloride is a negligibly weak base). Buffer solutions are built from a weak acid and its conjugate base, or a weak base and its conjugate acid, in comparable amounts.

**Lewis** widens it again to an electron-pair donor and acceptor. A Lewis base is a Brønsted base, and more besides; a Lewis acid need not donate a proton at all. BF₃, AlCl₃, FeCl₃, SiF₄ and SO₂ are Lewis acids with no proton to give, and they accept a pair from NH₃ to form adducts such as BF₃·NH₃. SiF₄ + 2NH₃ → SiF₄·2NH₃ is a pure Lewis acid–base reaction with no proton anywhere in it.

**Salts** are the survivors of the neutralisation. They dissociate completely in water into a cation and an anion, and their behaviour is set by whichever ion came from the stronger acid or base. Sodium carbonate is basic because the carbonate ion hydrolyses to produce OH⁻; ammonium chloride is acidic because the ammonium ion hydrolyses to produce H₃O⁺. Is this Arrhenius, Brønsted or Lewis? All three — the same reaction has a description at each level, and the strongest applicable one is usually the expected answer.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Edge cases in block placement

Four elements break the naive pattern, and each has a reason.

- **Hydrogen** is 1s¹, so it is s-block, but it behaves as a non-metal and is studied on its own.
- **Helium** is 1s², so it is s-block by configuration, yet it sits in Group 18 because its closed shell and very high ionisation enthalpy make it a noble gas. Block is about the subshell being filled; group is about chemical behaviour. This is the cleanest illustration that the two are different classifications.
- **Lanthanum (Z = 57)** is [Xe] 5d¹ 6s² with no f-electron in the ground state, so it is d-block even though it heads the lanthanoid series. **Actinium (Z = 89)** is [Rn] 6d¹ 7s², likewise d-block. The f-electrons appear from Ce onwards. This convention is the direct cause of the old "f-block has 14 elements" versus "15 elements" disagreement, and of the separate periodic table layout.
- **Lu and Lr** are conventionally placed with the d-block because their differentiating electron is a d electron.

#### Solid classification, reusing the same ideas

| Type | Examples | Made of | Conducts? | Melting point |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ionic | NaCl, MgO, KBr | Cations and anions in a lattice | Molten or in solution only | High |
| Covalent network | Diamond, SiO₂, SiC | Covalent bonds through the whole lattice | No (except graphite) | Very high |
| Molecular | I₂, CO₂, NH₃, sucrose | Discrete molecules held by intermolecular forces | No | Low |
| Metallic | Fe, Cu, alloys | Cation lattice in a delocalised electron sea | Yes | Varies, mostly high |

**Crystalline** solids have long-range order, sharp melting points and definite lattice geometry, and give sharp X-ray diffraction spots. **Amorphous** solids (glass, plastic, rubber) have short-range order only, soften over a range, and give diffuse rings. A liquid crystal is a substance that is liquid in flow but keeps an ordered arrangement of its molecules, so it is intermediate.

#### Isotopes, isobars and other near-misses

**Isotopes** have the same Z and different A, so same element, different mass number: ¹H, ²H, ³H; ³⁵Cl and ³⁷Cl. **Isobars** have the same mass number and different Z, so different elements entirely: ⁴⁰Ca (Z = 20) and ⁴⁰Ar (Z = 18). **Isotones** have the same number of neutrons. These three prefixes are the fastest way to lose a mark in a one-line question, so decide which property the stem says is shared and match the prefix to it — do not try to remember which is which.

#### Worked Example 1 — Palladium and the transition-metal test

Palladium, Z = 46. Its configuration is [Kr] 4d¹⁰ 5s⁰.

1. The differentiating electron goes into the 4d subshell → **d-block**.
2. In the atom the 4d subshell is **completely filled**.
3. Its common ion Pd²⁺ is [Kr] 4d⁸ — partly filled — so the trap is to call it a transition metal because Pd²⁺ is coloured and forms complexes.
4. **It is not a transition element.** The standard requirement is that the d subshell is partly filled in the atom or in at least one common oxidation state; Pd is d¹⁰ in the atom and forms Pd²⁺, so no partly filled d state is available in the common chemistry.

Checklist for this question type: write the ground-state configuration, write the ion configuration, ask whether d is partly filled in either. Zn, Cd, Hg, Pd, Ag and Au all need this check.

#### Worked Example 2 — Naming and classing a C₃H₆O compound

The formula fits both propanal (CH₃CH₂CHO) and propanone (CH₃COCH₃), which are functional-group isomers. A positive Tollens' test identifies an **aldehyde**, so the compound is **propanal**.

- A secondary alcohol is ruled out because an alcohol would be C₃H₈O, not C₃H₆O — the alcohol's formula demands two more hydrogens.
- The primary/secondary/tertiary label is not applicable, because that classification describes alcohols only. Calling propanal "a primary aldehyde" is meaningless; aldehydes are never classed this way.
- Trap to expect: propanone gives a **negative** Tollens' test, so if your answer names a ketone the compound identification is wrong, not the test.

#### Worked Example 3 — Is the salt acidic, basic or neutral?

Predict from the parent acid and base, using the stronger partner.

| Salt | From | Character | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| NaCl | Strong acid HCl, strong base NaOH | Neutral | Neither ion hydrolyses |
| CH₃COONa | Weak acid, strong base | Basic | CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O → CH₃COOH + OH⁻ |
| NH₄Cl | Strong acid, weak base NH₃ | Acidic | NH₄⁺ + H₂O → NH₃ + H₃O⁺ |
| CH₃COONH₄ | Weak acid, weak base | Roughly neutral | The two hydrolyses nearly cancel; not exactly equal |
| Na₂CO₃ | Strong base, weak acid H₂CO₃ | Basic | CO₃²⁻ hydrolyses appreciably |

Mnemonic that gets you through without a table: **the ion from the strong partner is the spectator, and the ion from the weak partner decides.** Strong partners are always neutral ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻).

### 🧠 Memory Anchors

```mermaid
flowchart TD
    A[Classification question] --> B{Element or compound?}
    B -->|element| C[Block = subshell taking the last electron]
    C --> D[ns = s-block 1,2]
    C --> E[nsp = p-block 13 to 18]
    C --> F[n-1 d = d-block 3 to 12]
    C --> G[n-2 f = f-block, lanthanoids and actinoids]
    B -->|compound| H{Organic or inorganic?}
    H -->|organic| I[Find the functional group, then the suffix]
    I --> J{OH on sp3 C = alkan-ol]
    I --> K{OH on ring = phenol]
    I --> L[CHO = alkan-al]
    I --> M[CO between carbons = alkan-one]
    I --> N[COOH = alkan-oic acid]
    I --> O[COC, no OH = ether, named alkoxyalkane]
    H -->|inorganic| P[Acid, base or salt reaction?]
    P --> Q[Proton moved = Bronsted]
    P --> R[No proton, pair donated to a metal or B = Lewis]
    P --> S[H3O+ or OH- in water = Arrhenius]
    P --> T[Salt? Strong partner is the spectator, weak partner decides the pH]
```

- **"Filled 2s and half-filled 2p dip."** The only two ionisation-enthalpy dips in a period, and both are the same idea — a stable subshell.
- **"Block is the subshell, group is the behaviour."** Helium proves the two are different questions.
- **"Alkan-ol, alkan-al, alkan-one, alkan-oic acid."** Four suffixes, one table, and the structure tells you which.
- **"1°, 2°, 3° counts carbons next door."** The –OH carbon's neighbours, not the number of –OH groups.
- **"The weak partner decides the pH of a salt."** Strong acid, strong base → neutral salt, no hydrolysis.
- **"Isotope is same protons, isobar is same mass."** Decide from the stem which property is shared, then match the word.
- **"No partly-filled d, no transition metal."** Pd, Ag, Au, Zn, Cd, Hg — write both configurations and check.
- **Flashcard Q&A:**
  - *Block of element 46?* → d-block, [Kr] 4d¹⁰ 5s⁰, not a transition metal.
  - *Is CH₃OCH₃ an alcohol?* → no, it is an ether; no –OH at all.
  - *Lewis acid with no proton?* → BF₃, AlCl₃, FeCl₃, SiF₄, SO₂.
  - *Conjugate base of H₂SO₄?* → HSO₄⁻.
  - *CH₃COONa acidic or basic?* → basic, acetate hydrolyses to make OH⁻.
  - *Group 18 element that is s-block?* → helium.
  - *Is ⁴⁰Ar an isotope or isobar of ⁴⁰Ca?* → isobar; same A, different Z.

### 🧪 Self-Test — 8 Questions with Worked Answers

1. **An element has the configuration [Xe] 5d¹ 4f¹ 6s². Which block, and which series does it head?**
   The differentiating electron is in 5d, so it is **d-block**. It is lanthanum (Z = 57), which heads the lanthanoid series despite being d-block, because its ground state has no f-electron. The f-electrons start with cerium. This is the standard example of the convention conflict.
2. **Rank the first ionisation enthalpies of B, Be, C, N and O.**
   Be > B < C < N > O. Be is above B because B's 2p¹ electron is easier to remove than a filled 2s electron. N is above O because O has a 2p⁴ arrangement containing a paired set, and removing one electron from that pair gives the more stable half-filled 2p³. The two anomalies are the same anomaly — subshell stability — in two places.
3. **Give the Lewis structure of SO₂, state whether it is a Lewis acid, and say which Brønsted behaviour applies.**
   Two S=O or S–O resonance forms with a lone pair on S, giving a bent shape. It is a **Lewis acid** because the sulphur accepts an electron pair into an empty antibonding orbital. It has no ionisable proton, so the Brønsted description does not apply to it. Arrhenius classification also fails.
4. **Classify NH₃ and BF₃ under each of the three acid–base theories, and say which category each belongs to.**
   NH₃ is a Brønsted base (accepts H⁺ to form NH₄⁺) and a Lewis base (lone pair on N). It is not an Arrhenius base in the literal sense because it produces no OH⁻ in water, though its solution is alkaline. BF₃ is a **Lewis acid only**: it has no lone pair to donate, and it accepts a pair from NH₃ to form the adduct F₃B←NH₃. A substance can belong to only the Lewis category and still be a fully valid exam answer.
5. **Is CH₃–O–CH₃ an alcohol, an ether or an ester? Give its IUPAC name.**
   An **ether** — the linkage is C–O–C with no –OH and no C=O adjacent to the oxygen. IUPAC name: **methoxymethane**, named by taking the smaller chain as the parent and the –OCH₃ group as a methoxy prefix. Ethers are the one class in the table with no suffix of their own.
6. **A salt is made from HCN and KOH. Is it acidic, basic or neutral?**
   **Basic.** K⁺ is the spectator from the strong base, and CN⁻ from the weak acid HCN hydrolyses: CN⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HCN + OH⁻. The weak partner decides, and the weak partner is the acid, so the salt is basic. The reverse case, a weak base with a strong acid, gives an acidic salt.
7. **Which has more unpaired electrons, Fe²⁺ or Fe³⁺?**
   Fe²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶, which by Hund's rule puts one electron in each of the five d orbitals and pairs the sixth — so **4 unpaired**. Fe³⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁵, exactly one in each orbital — **5 unpaired**. This is why Fe²⁺ solutions are pale green while Fe³⁺ solutions are yellow-brown, and it is the cleanest illustration that colour in d-block ions comes from unpaired electrons.
8. **Is ³⁵Cl an isotope or isobar of ³⁷Cl? Can the two be separated by ordinary chemical means?**
   Isotopes — same Z (both chlorine, Z = 17), different A. They have the same electron configuration and therefore almost identical chemistry, so **ordinary chemical methods cannot separate them**; separation needs physical methods such as mass spectrometry or fractional diffusion. The practical consequence is that chlorine's atomic mass comes out as the abundance-weighted 35.45, not as either isotope's whole number.

### 🎯 Exam Traps & Error Log

1. **Deciding block from the element's symbol or position in a list of "d-block metals."** The block is defined by the differentiating electron, so copper is d-block but tin is p-block, even though both are metals found in the middle of the table. Check the ground-state configuration every time.
2. **Naming an ether as a suffix.** Ethers are named as alkoxyalkanes (methoxymethane), not with any suffix. There is no "-oxy-" suffix.
3. **Calling propanal a primary aldehyde.** The 1°/2°/3° labels belong to alcohols and amines only.
4. **Calling a triol "a tertiary alcohol".** Three –OH groups is a triol, not a 3° alcohol.
5. **Accepting Pd or Ag as transition metals** without writing the d-count in the atom.
6. **Placing helium by configuration and ignoring its behaviour**, or placing La in the f-block without noting the convention.
7. **Swapping isotope and isobar.**
8. **Assuming all p-block compounds are covalent or all d-block ions are coloured.** d¹⁰ and d⁰ ions are colourless.
9. **Judging salt pH from the formula alone** and forgetting hydrolysis.
10. **Using the alcohol suffix for a phenol,** or calling phenol "benzenol".
11. **Forgetting that bond angles and steric-number questions from Chemical Bonding reappear here** in disguised form whenever an –OH carbon is involved in naming.

### 💡 Pro Tips

1. **Write the classification route on the question, not in your head** — a four-word chain: element → block; organic → group → suffix; acid/base → what is transferred. It takes five seconds and prevents category errors.
2. **Build a single-page functional-group table** and use it for every naming question for the rest of the syllabus. It is reused in Aldehydes, Ketones, Acids, Amines and Alcohols.
3. **Memorise the exceptions as a short list:** Zn, Cd, Hg, Pd, Ag, Au (not transition), He (s-block, Group 18), La and Ac (d-block, head the f series). Everything else follows the rule.
4. **Say the reason out loud for every "why" question.** Almost every question in this chapter asks why, and the reasons are short — filled subshell, shielding, resonance, hydrolysis.
5. **Practise the Lewis category actively,** by listing substances that are Lewis acids with no proton. It is the theory with the least practice and the clearest pass/fail.
6. **For salt pH questions, apply the weak-partner rule in one step** rather than writing both hydrolysis equations and solving.
7. **Keep Tollens', Fehling's and the iodoform test on your functional-group card.** They are the fastest way to resolve C₃H₆O-style isomers.
8. **Revisit this chapter whenever organic nomenclature feels slow.** Most "why can't I name this" problems in later chapters are classification problems in disguise.
