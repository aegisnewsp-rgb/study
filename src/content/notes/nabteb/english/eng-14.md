# Comprehension: Inference

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Inference is the act of reading between the lines — pulling out a meaning the writer hints at but never says outright. NABTEB tests this in Paper 1 (multiple-choice comprehension) where stem phrases like *"It can be inferred that…"*, *"The writer implies…"*, and *"From the passage, one can deduce…"* demand answers grounded in clues rather than copied lines.

- **Explicit** = written in black and white; **Implicit** = suggested through tone, mood, or situation.
- Always combine a textual clue with your own knowledge of how people behave.
- The right answer is *suggested*, never *quoted verbatim* and never *extreme*.

| Signal word in the stem | What you must do |
| --- | --- |
| It can be inferred that | Pick the choice the passage hints at |
| The writer implies / suggests | Match the implied attitude or cause |
| It is likely that / One can deduce | Choose the most probable, moderate option |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### What inference really tests
Inference questions measure whether a reader can move past surface wording. A passage rarely hands you its conclusion; instead, the writer's word choice, sentence rhythm, and situational detail carry a hidden message. NABTEB examiners reward candidates who fuse those textual signals with ordinary life knowledge — never those who project personal bias onto the text.

#### How to attack an inference item in NABTEB Paper 1
Read the stem first, then scan the paragraph that anchors the clue. Look for:

- Tone words (e.g., *"scornfully," "lamented," "boasted"*) that betray attitude.
- Cause-effect links (action followed by consequence) that reveal motivation.
- Contrast markers (*however, but, yet*) that pivot meaning.

Eliminate any option that is directly stated in the passage — that is an *explicit* detail, not an inference. Also drop choices with absolute words like *always, never, completely, none*, because genuine inferences are usually moderate and qualified.

#### Question stems you will meet
- *"From the passage, one can infer that the speaker…"*
- *"Which of the following best describes the writer's attitude?"*
- *"It can be deduced that the character felt…"*

| Common trap | Why it fails | Correct habit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Choosing the explicitly stated line | It tests recall, not inference | Ask: is this *implied* or *quoted*? |
| Picking an extreme option | Absolute words overreach the text | Prefer moderate, qualified language |
| Inserting personal opinion | Inference must be text-supported | Tie every claim to a clue in the passage |

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge cases and subtle traps
The hardest inference items hide behind **double negatives**, **irony**, and **unreliable narrators**. A line like *"He smiled and wished her well"* can imply contempt if earlier sentences reveal his cruelty — NABTEB setters love this kind of tonal contrast. Another classic edge case: an inference about *future* events. Because the passage ends before the action, the correct answer must stay probabilistic (*"is likely to"*, *"may soon"*), never definitive.

#### Links to adjacent topics
Inference overlaps with **deduction** (logic), **pragmatics** (what is meant vs. said), and **tone/atmosphere** questions. Mastery here boosts scores in **vocabulary-from-context** items too, since both rely on surrounding clues rather than dictionary recall.

#### Common mistakes — and the fix

1. Confusing assumption with inference — an *assumption* lacks textual backing; an *inference* must be defensible from the passage.
2. Ignoring narrator reliability — first-person bias can mislead; weigh who is speaking.
3. Over-relying on background knowledge — useful only when fused with a textual clue.

#### Micro-practice prompts
1. A passage states: *"After three failed harvests, Aisha quietly packed her bags."* What can be inferred about her emotional state? *(Answer check: resignation, disappointment, or determination — supported by the cumulative failure.)*
2. A writer notes: *"The governor laughed at the petition and adjourned the meeting."* What is implied about his regard for the petitioners? *(Answer check: dismissal or contempt, supported by the dismissive laughter.)*
