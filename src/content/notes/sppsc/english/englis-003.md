# Grammar Fundamentals: the English component of an SPSC written test

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Five categories, and why they are the five.

A note on scope before anything else. SPSC does not run one paper. It advertises post by post, and the Commission publishes a **dated syllabus document for each advertisement** under *Scope & Syllabus* on spsc.gos.pk — recent entries include *Assistant Director (Technical), BPS-17, Mines & Mineral Development Department* (14 July 2026), *Assistant Collector Part-I & Part-II for Officers of PMS, BPS-17* (28 March 2026), *Assistant Engineer, BPS-17, Culture Tourism Antiquities Archives Department* (4 December 2025) and *Accounts Officer, BPS-17, Finance Department* (14 March 2025).

So the **English component is post-specific**, and the dated syllabus PDF attached to your own advertisement is the authoritative description of it. This note does not guess that structure for you. What it does is give you the grammar that carries marks in every English paper written for a Pakistani competitive service, because the underlying categories are the same regardless of the post.

Five of them, in the order they pay:

| Category | What breaks | The one-line fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subject–verb agreement | Singular subject, plural verb (and the reverse) | Find the real subject, then match the verb to it |
| Tense consistency | One tense switching mid-sentence | Decide the tense in the first clause, hold it |
| Articles (*a / an / the*) | *a hour*, *the Pakistan* | Fix by sound for *a/an*; *the* for specific things |
| Prepositions | *discuss about*, *according me* | Learn the verb-plus-preposition as one unit |
| Voice and narration | Active/passive slippage, tense back-shift errors | Do the transformation mechanically, clause by clause |

That is the whole syllabus in practice. Everything else is vocabulary, comprehension or précis, and those are skill sections rather than correction sections.

**One rule with an outsized price.** The Commission states its standard process as a pre-written screening test, the written examination, and then the interview. A written paper is where grammar is scored directly, and marks lost to *discuss about* are marks no amount of reading practice recovers. Grammar is the cheapest marks-per-hour section in the whole preparation, and candidates skip it because it feels like schoolwork.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> How to fix each category on real sentences rather than exercises.

#### Subject–verb agreement

The rule: the verb agrees with the subject, not with a nearby noun. Almost every error in this category comes from a noun phrase sitting between the two.

> *The list of approved candidates **is** now on the website.* (not "are" — subject is "list")
> *Each of the departmental examinations **has** its own syllabus.* (not "have")

Two constructions cause most of the trouble:

- **Quantified nouns** — *a number of* takes a plural verb, *the number of* takes a singular one. *A number of posts have lapsed. The number of lapses is small.*
- **Intervening clauses** — long prepositional phrases hide the subject. Underline the subject before you check the verb.

#### Tense consistency

Most tense errors are not knowledge errors; they are drift. The writer picks a tense, loses it in a long subordinate clause, and comes back in a different one.

> *The advertisement was issued, and the last date for submission **remains** unchanged.* (the first verb is past; "remains" is not drift, it is a deliberate shift — but the candidate must be able to justify it)

Rule of practice: decide the tense from the time frame in the first clause and hold it through every clause that shares that frame. Shift only where the time frame genuinely shifts, and make the shift visible.

#### Articles

Two separate problems, frequently confused.

- **a / an** is a sound test, not a spelling test: *an hour* (h is silent), *a university* (*y* sound), *an MBA*, *an FPSC*.
- **the** marks something already identified. Test: can you say *which one*? If not, it is probably not *the*.

> *The Board of Revenue Department published the syllabus.* — *the* Board because it is the identified body; *the* syllabus because it is the specific document.

#### Prepositions

The efficient method is to stop treating prepositions as decoration and learn them as fixed units with their verb. *Depend on*, *comply with*, *constitute*, *aware of*, *capable of*, *responsible for*, *in accordance with*.

> Correct: *The application must comply with the instructions in the advertisement.*
> Incorrect: *comply the instructions*; *according the advertisement* (should be *according to*).

Preposition errors are the least forgivable category in written answers because they are visible on the page and they are never a matter of interpretation.

#### Voice and narration

Passive voice is not wrong. It becomes wrong when the transformation is done by ear instead of mechanically. Do it in a fixed order: identify tense → identify subject and object → apply the structure → carry the agent across only if the actor matters.

> Active: *The Commission published the syllabus.*
> Passive: *The syllabus **was published by the Commission**.*

Narration works the same way — transform, then back-shift the tense and shift the pronoun, person and time reference together. Students who attempt this in prose rather than as a mechanical operation lose marks on questions that are otherwise free.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Building an error log that actually reduces your error rate.

#### Collect your own sentences

Grammar improves fastest when the practice material is text you wrote yourself, because then the error and its cause are both visible. A weekly loop that works:

1. Write three short paragraphs on any subject — current affairs from the past week is ideal.
2. Next day, reread them and mark every error you find, without fixing it yet.
3. Name the category from the five above for each one.
4. Fix them and record the count by category.

Three months of this produces a table that tells you where your marks actually go. In almost every case it collapses to two or three categories carrying most of the errors — often articles and tense drift — and once that is visible, targeted study becomes obvious instead of theoretical.

#### The rewriting drill

Take a published paragraph from an editorial or a government circular and rewrite it twice: once in the active voice and plain register, once in a more formal register with correct subordination. The second rewrite is where sentence-combining skill is built, and sentence-combining is what written answers are actually marked on.

If your written English leans on "very", "important", "good" and "thing", the rewrite is too easy and you should pick harder source text. Formal written answers are marked on structure and precision, and both come from rewrites, not from reading rules.

#### Register control in a written paper

Professional-service written answers are read quickly by an examiner who has marked hundreds. Three habits make an answer readable:

- **Front-load.** Put the claim in the first sentence of the paragraph.
- **One idea per sentence.** If a sentence has two "ands", split it.
- **Vary length deliberately.** A sequence of similar-length sentences reads as flat and scores as flat.

None of this is style for its own sake. Under time pressure it is the difference between an answer whose argument is legible and one the examiner has to decode.

#### The four marks most candidates lose

1. **Subject–verb agreement across an intervening phrase.** Underline the subject; it takes four seconds and catches most of these.
2. **Drift in tense inside a subordinate clause.** Re-read the last clause of every long sentence against the first.
3. ***a* / *an* by spelling rather than by sound.** The silent-*h* cases (*hour*, *honourable*, *honest*) and the *-u* / *-eu* words (*university*, *uniform*, *one*) are where this breaks.
4. **Verbs that do not take a preposition.** *Explain*, *discuss*, *mention*, *emphasise*, *request* — candidates add "about" or "on" to all four. None of them take one.

#### Common mistakes and exam strategy

1. **Studying grammar rules without collecting your own errors.** Rules read once change nothing; a logged error changes the next answer.
2. **Fixing by ear.** Passive and narration transformations are mechanical. Do them as steps, not by judgement.
3. **Confusing *a/an* as a spelling rule.** It is a sound rule.
4. **Revising from an undated circulating syllabus PDF.** Match the document to the date on your own advertisement.
5. **Assuming the Commission's process is one exam.** The Commission's stated standard process is a screening test, then the written examination, then the interview — three stages, three different preparation profiles.

> **Exam strategy:** in the last fortnight before the written test, stop learning new grammar. Reread your own error log. Every mark in that log is a mark you personally lost, and rereading your own mistakes is the highest-yield grammar revision that exists — it is targeted by construction, because your log is a measurement of your own weak categories rather than a general list.

#### Practice prompts

1. *"Correct the following and name the category of each error: 'The list of departments have published it's own syllabus, and the number of advertisement are large.'"* — Five errors across agreement, possessive, verb choice and plurality.
2. *"Rewrite this sentence in the passive voice, then back: 'The Commission published the revised syllabus for the Assistant Collector post on 28 March 2026.'"* — Check the tense handling and whether the agent needs to survive.
3. *"Why does an SPSC candidate need to read the dated syllabus PDF rather than a general English preparation guide?"* — Answer using the Commission's per-post publication practice and the range of departments and grades that advertise through it.
