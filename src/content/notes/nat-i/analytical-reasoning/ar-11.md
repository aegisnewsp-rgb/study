# Statement and Arguments

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Statement and Arguments** questions present a brief statement of fact, opinion, or policy, followed by two or more numbered arguments (I, II, III…) that either support or oppose it. The task is to judge each argument independently as **Strong** or **Weak**.

A **Strong argument** is one that is (a) **logically relevant** to the statement, (b) **practically feasible**, and (c) built on a **true or highly plausible assumption**. A **Weak argument** is vague, irrelevant, far-fetched, emotionally driven, or rests on a doubtful premise.

High-yield pointers for NAT-I:
- Evaluate each argument **separately** — one strong argument never boosts a weak one.
- An argument that merely **restates** the premise adds nothing and is weak.
- Moral, emotional, or statistical appeals without direct linkage to the statement are weak.
- For- and against-arguments can **both be strong** when each is logically valid.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Definition and Format
A Statement and Arguments item consists of a short passage (the *statement*) and a set of numbered *arguments*. Each argument is a reason offered either **for** or **against** the proposition, policy, or claim made in the statement. In NAT-I Analytical Reasoning, the statement is usually a single sentence describing a social practice, government policy, business rule, or general observation.

#### Criteria for a Strong Argument
An argument qualifies as strong only when **all three** tests pass:

1. **Logical Relevance** — the argument must address the *exact* issue raised by the statement, not a side-issue or a different problem.
2. **Practicality** — the proposed point must be realistic and implementable in the real world, not a theoretical or utopian ideal.
3. **Valid Assumption** — the underlying premise the argument rests on must be reasonable and verifiable, not invented or controversial.

#### Criteria for a Weak Argument
An argument is weak if it fails any of the three tests above, or if it:
- Restates the obvious content of the statement.
- Appeals to emotion, morality, or vague ideals without evidence.
- Introduces a **new, unrelated** problem.
- Proposes an **extreme or unrealistic** solution.
- Depends on a **false or unverified** statistic, survey, or fact.

#### Common NAT-I Question Stems

| Stem | Task |
|------|------|
| "Which of the following is a strong argument?" | Pick the single strongest among given options. |
| "Which of the following is a weak argument?" | Identify the option that fails relevance, practicality, or assumption. |
| "Which of the following pairs of arguments are strong?" | Both arguments in the chosen pair must independently pass the three tests. |
| "Statement followed by a course of action" | Decide if the course of action should be followed — only if it directly solves the problem stated. |

#### Worked Mini-Example
*Statement:* "Foreign direct investment should be allowed in the retail sector."
- *Argument I:* "FDI will bring capital and modern technology, benefiting consumers." → **Strong** — relevant, practical, rests on established economic effect.
- *Argument II:* "It is morally wrong for outsiders to control our markets." → **Weak** — moral appeal with no logical linkage to retail efficiency.
- *Argument III:* "FDI may harm small domestic retailers." → **Strong** — relevant counter-point grounded in a verifiable competitive effect.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Traps
The hardest Statement and Arguments items hide the weakness inside plausible-sounding phrasing. Watch for these specific traps:

- **The “True but Irrelevant” Trap.** A factually correct claim (e.g., “India is a democracy”) may still be weak if it does not logically support or oppose the statement on the table.
- **The “Plausible Assumption” Trap.** An argument may *appear* to rest on common sense, but the assumption is actually contestable. Example: “Online classes should replace classrooms because students save travel time” assumes travel time is a barrier to learning — it is not.
- **The “Restated Premise” Trap.** Phrasing the statement in different words and offering it as an argument is a recurring weak-argument pattern in NTS papers.
- **The “Both Strong” Possibility.** Students often assume a for-argument and an against-argument must include one weak entry. In NAT-I, both can be strong when the statement itself has two valid competing considerations.

#### Connection to Adjacent Topics
Statement and Arguments overlaps with **Assumption-Based Reasoning**, **Course of Action**, and **Inference Drawing**. Practising assumptions strengthens your ability to spot the hidden premise inside any argument. Course of Action items are a direct subtype — the “argument” is replaced by a proposed step, and the same three tests (relevance, practicality, valid assumption) apply.

#### Common Mistakes
- Confusing **strong-sounding rhetoric** with logical strength. Tone is irrelevant; structure is everything.
- Rejecting an argument because you **disagree personally** with its conclusion. Judge the reasoning, not the opinion.
- Treating **statistical claims** as automatically strong without verifying whether the statistic supports the specific claim in the statement.

#### Exam Strategy for NAT-I
The Analytical Reasoning section contributes roughly 20 questions to NAT-I, with Statement and Arguments typically supplying **1–3 questions**. Budget about **45–60 seconds** per item. Read the statement twice, underline the **key issue**, then for each argument ask: *Is it on-topic? Is it realistic? Does its premise hold?* Eliminate weak arguments first; the survivor is your answer. For “pair” questions, verify each member of the pair independently — partial credit does not exist.

#### Practice Prompts
1. *Statement:* “Examinations should be replaced by continuous assessment.” Identify which of the following is a strong argument for the statement: (a) Students work harder when monitored daily; (b) It is the global trend; (c) Exams cause stress, which is unhealthy; (d) Teachers find grading easier. *(Answer key insight: (a) is the only one meeting relevance + practicality + valid assumption.)*
2. *Statement:* “Smoking should be banned in public places.” Decide whether the course of action “Levy a heavy fine on violators” is to be followed, and justify using the three-test framework.
