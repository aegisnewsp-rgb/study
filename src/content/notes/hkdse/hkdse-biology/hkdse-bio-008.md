# VIII. Biotechnology (Elective) — HKDSE Biology Notes

This topic covers Elective Topic VIII of the HKDSE Biology Elective Part. Candidates sit any two of the four electives on Paper 2. The topic addresses techniques in modern biotechnology (recombinant DNA technology, PCR, gel electrophoresis, DNA sequencing, cloning, monoclonal antibodies); applications in biotechnology (medical, agricultural, industrial); and bioethics (genetic testing, GM organisms, cloning, ownership of genetic material).

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Techniques**: recombinant DNA technology (cutting DNA with restriction enzymes, ligating with DNA ligase into plasmids, transforming bacteria); PCR (polymerase chain reaction — denaturation, annealing, extension); gel electrophoresis (separates DNA fragments by size); DNA sequencing (Sanger sequencing, next-generation sequencing); cloning (reproductive, therapeutic); monoclonal antibodies (hybridoma technology).
- **Applications**: medical (insulin production, gene therapy, monoclonal antibody therapy, recombinant vaccines); agricultural (GM crops — Bt cotton, herbicide-tolerant soy, golden rice); industrial (enzymes for detergents, biofuels, bioplastics).
- **Bioethics**: genetic testing (privacy, discrimination); GM organisms (environmental impact, food safety); cloning (human reproductive cloning is widely banned; therapeutic cloning is more accepted); ownership of genetic material (patents on genes and sequences); consent in genetic research.

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing reproductive cloning (producing a genetically identical organism) with therapeutic cloning (producing stem cells for treatment).
- Treating GM crops as inherently harmful or inherently beneficial — the risk assessment is case-by-case.
- Forgetting that PCR amplifies specific DNA sequences using primers.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Techniques in modern biotechnology

**Recombinant DNA technology**:

1. Cut the target DNA and the plasmid with the same restriction enzyme. The restriction enzyme recognises a specific palindromic sequence (e.g. EcoRI: GAATTC) and cuts at a specific site, producing "sticky ends".
2. Mix the cut target DNA and plasmid. The complementary sticky ends anneal.
3. Add DNA ligase, which seals the nicks to form a recombinant plasmid.
4. Transform the recombinant plasmid into a bacterium (e.g. E. coli), which replicates and expresses the inserted gene.

**PCR (polymerase chain reaction)**: amplifies a specific DNA sequence in vitro. Steps (repeated for 25-35 cycles):

1. **Denaturation** (~95°C): the double-stranded DNA separates into single strands.
2. **Annealing** (~55-65°C): primers bind to the complementary sequences flanking the target region.
3. **Extension** (~72°C): DNA polymerase (typically Taq polymerase, from Thermus aquaticus) synthesises the new strand from the primers.

After n cycles, the number of copies is 2ⁿ. PCR is used in forensics (DNA fingerprinting), medical diagnostics (pathogen detection), and genetic testing.

**Gel electrophoresis**: separates DNA fragments by size. DNA is loaded into wells at one end of an agarose gel; an electric current is applied; DNA (negatively charged) migrates toward the positive electrode. Smaller fragments migrate faster than larger ones. Bands are visualised with a DNA stain (e.g. ethidium bromide, SYBR Green).

**DNA sequencing**:

- **Sanger sequencing**: chain-termination method; uses dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs) to terminate DNA synthesis; fragments are separated by capillary electrophoresis.
- **Next-generation sequencing (NGS)**: massively parallel sequencing of millions of fragments; faster and cheaper than Sanger for large genomes.

**Cloning**:

- **Reproductive cloning**: produces a genetically identical organism (e.g. Dolly the sheep, 1996). Human reproductive cloning is widely banned.
- **Therapeutic cloning**: produces embryonic stem cells for medical treatment; less controversial than reproductive cloning.

**Monoclonal antibodies**: produced by hybridoma cells (B cell fused with myeloma cell). Hybridoma cells produce a single antibody specificity; can be cultured indefinitely. Used in diagnosis (e.g. pregnancy tests, pathogen identification) and treatment (e.g. trastuzumab for HER2-positive breast cancer).

#### Applications in biotechnology

**Medical**:

- **Insulin production**: human insulin gene in E. coli; the bacteria produce insulin, which is harvested and purified. Replaced animal insulin for most diabetics.
- **Gene therapy**: introduces a functional gene into a patient's cells to treat genetic disease. Examples: SCID (severe combined immunodeficiency), Leber's congenital amaurosis.
- **Monoclonal antibody therapy**: rituximab (lymphoma), trastuzumab (HER2+ breast cancer), bevacizumab (colorectal cancer), adalimumab (autoimmune diseases).
- **Recombinant vaccines**: hepatitis B vaccine in yeast, HPV vaccine in yeast or insect cells, COVID-19 mRNA vaccines.

**Agricultural**:

- **Bt crops**: Bacillus thuringiensis genes inserted into crops produce Bt toxin, which is insecticidal. Examples: Bt cotton, Bt corn.
- **Herbicide-tolerant crops**: e.g. Roundup Ready soy (tolerant to glyphosate). Reduces need for tilling.
- **Golden rice**: enriched in beta-carotene (vitamin A precursor); aimed at reducing vitamin A deficiency in low-income countries.

**Industrial**:

- **Enzymes**: proteases and lipases in detergents; amylases in starch processing; cellulases in textile finishing.
- **Biofuels**: ethanol from corn or sugarcane fermentation; biodiesel from vegetable oils.
- **Bioplastics**: PHA (polyhydroxyalkanoate) produced by bacteria; biodegradable.

#### Bioethics

**Genetic testing**: pre-implantation genetic diagnosis (PGD), prenatal screening, predictive testing for adult-onset diseases (e.g. BRCA1/BRCA2 for breast cancer). Issues: privacy, discrimination by insurers or employers, psychological impact.

**GM organisms**: environmental concerns (gene flow to wild relatives, impact on non-target species); food safety (allergenicity, toxicity); corporate control of seed supply.

**Cloning**: reproductive cloning of humans is widely banned. Therapeutic cloning raises concerns about the moral status of embryos.

**Ownership of genetic material**: patents on isolated genes (the US Supreme Court ruled in 2013 that naturally occurring DNA sequences cannot be patented, but cDNA can be). Issues: access to genetic data, commercialisation of human biology.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Cross-topic links

- **II. Genetics and Evolution**: recombinant DNA technology is applied molecular genetics.
- **VII. Microorganisms and Humans**: many biotechnology tools were first developed in microbiology.
- **VI. Applied Ecology**: GM crops have environmental impacts; bioremediation uses biotechnology.

#### Exam technique

- **For technique questions**: name the technique, the principle, the inputs, the outputs, and a specific application.
- **For ethics questions**: present both sides of the argument; cite specific cases (e.g. the 2018 He Jiankui CRISPR babies case).
- **For "discuss the implications" questions**: distinguish short-term and long-term implications; cite specific examples.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Cloned animals are exact copies." Cloned animals are genetic copies, but environmental factors (epigenetics, maternal effects, postnatal environment) can produce differences in behaviour and appearance.
- "GM crops are unsafe to eat." Major scientific bodies (WHO, NAS, EFSA) have concluded that approved GM crops are safe for human consumption. The risks are environmental, not food safety per se.
- "Gene therapy is a cure." Gene therapy is still experimental and has had limited success outside a few specific diseases. Many trials have had serious adverse events.
