# Argument Structure and the Argument's Conclusion — LSAT Logical Reasoning

Identifying the conclusion of an argument is the foundation for every other Logical Reasoning skill. The MCAT-style LSAT Logical Reasoning items test whether the test-taker can identify the argument's conclusion, the premises that support it, and the overall structure (deductive, inductive, or analogical). Without a clear read on the conclusion, every other item type is harder.

---

### Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

#### What argument structure tests

- **Conclusion:** the main claim the argument tries to establish.
- **Premises:** the reasons offered to support the conclusion.
- **Structure:** deductive (conclusion follows necessarily), inductive (conclusion follows probably), analogical (conclusion by analogy).

#### One-pass drill

1. Read the stimulus once. Mark the claim that the rest of the passage supports.
2. Identify the premises. Each premise is a reason given for the conclusion.
3. Identify the structure. Deductive items use absolute language; inductive uses probabilistic; analogical uses "like" or "similar to".

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing a premise with the conclusion. The conclusion is the claim the premises support, not the other way around.
- Treating background information as premises. Background information is context, not a reason for the conclusion.
- Missing a hidden conclusion. Some arguments have a stated premise that leads to an unstated conclusion.

### Standard — Regular Study (2d-2mo)

#### Identifying the conclusion

**Indicator words for conclusions:** "therefore," "thus," "so," "hence," "consequently," "accordingly," "it follows that," "the conclusion is," "we may infer."

**Indicator words for premises:** "because," "since," "for," "given that," "as," "after all," "the reason is."

**Common patterns:**
- Premise + Conclusion: "X. Therefore Y."
- Premise + Premise + Conclusion: "X. Y. Therefore Z."
- Background + Premise + Conclusion: "X is true. Y follows. Therefore Z."

#### Argument structures

**Deductive:** the conclusion follows necessarily from the premises. If the premises are true, the conclusion must be true. Items use absolute language ("all," "every," "no," "never").

**Inductive:** the conclusion follows probably from the premises. The premises provide evidence but do not guarantee the conclusion. Items use probabilistic language ("likely," "probably," "most").

**Analogical:** the conclusion is drawn by comparing two cases. The strength of the analogy depends on the relevance of the similarities.

**Causal:** the conclusion is drawn about cause and effect. Strength depends on correlation, mechanism, and elimination of alternatives.

#### Practice routine

- Take 10 LR stimuli. For each, identify the conclusion and the premises.
- Classify each argument as deductive, inductive, analogical, or causal.
- Time yourself: under 1 minute per stimulus for the structure pass.

### Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo-6mo)

#### How argument structure connects to the other LR skills

- **Assumptions** depend on structure: a necessary assumption is the link between premises and conclusion.
- **Strengthen/weaken** items target the premises or the assumption.
- **Flaw identification** items ask what is wrong with the structure.
- **Inference** items ask what must follow from the premises.

#### Common errors and the correction

- **"The conclusion is at the end."** Conclusions can appear at the beginning, middle, or end of the stimulus.
- **"Deductive arguments are always valid."** Validity depends on the premises; an argument can be deductive in form but unsound if premises are false.
- **"Inductive arguments are weak."** Inductive arguments can be strong or weak; the question is how well the evidence supports the conclusion.

#### Exam technique

- For structure items, identify the conclusion first, then the premises, then the type.
- For conclusion items, look for indicator words; if absent, find the claim that the rest of the passage supports.
- For deductive items, use absolute reasoning ("if all X are Y, and Z is X, then Z is Y").
- For inductive items, evaluate the strength of the evidence ("how representative is the sample?").
