# Molecular Biology and Genetics

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review

The Biological and Physical Sciences section is 75 multiple-choice questions in 150 minutes, and ACER describes it as assessing "the ability to identify knowledge in new contexts, and analyse and interpret data". Read that as a promise about the shape of the questions. Molecular biology in this section arrives as a pedigree to trace, a sequence to read, a compound to place in a pathway, or an energy budget to check. It very rarely arrives as "state the function of the ribosome".

Every one of those stems is solvable the same way: run the chain from what the stem gives you to what the stem implies. The answer sits at the end of the chain, not in a fact you happened to memorise.

**Five chains cover most of the topic.**

| Stem gives you | Chain to run | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| A sequence, a strand, a base pair | Structure to function | Treating the template strand as the one that gets built |
| A pedigree | Pattern to mode of inheritance | Reaching for population frequency instead of the family in front of you |
| A change in DNA | Consequence for protein, cell and organism | Calling every deletion a frameshift |
| A compound or a mutated gene | Target to pathway | Skipping a step between the target and the phenotype |
| A rate, a yield, an oxygen reading | Energy accounting | Adding up ATP without checking which stage is blocked |

**The facts that do the heavy lifting.**

- DNA strands run antiparallel. One runs 5' to 3', the other 3' to 5'.
- DNA polymerase adds nucleotides only to a 3' end, so every new strand is synthesised 5' to 3'. That single rule explains the leading and lagging strands.
- Replication is semiconservative: each daughter molecule keeps one parental strand and gains one new strand.
- The leading strand is made continuously. The lagging strand is made as Okazaki fragments, each starting from an RNA primer, and ligase seals the gaps.
- RNA polymerase reads the template strand 3' to 5' and builds RNA 5' to 3', and it does not need a primer.
- The code is read in triplets: 64 codons, of which three are stop codons, and the rest specify amino acids.
- Translation reads the mRNA 5' to 3'. The codon sits on the mRNA, the anticodon on the tRNA.
- Crossing over occurs between homologous chromosomes in prophase I, which is why linked genes fail to assort independently.

**The figures worth having cold.**

| Quantity | Figure |
|---|---|
| Bases per codon | 3 |
| Codons in the genetic code | 64, of which 3 are stop |
| Amino acids specified | 20 |
| Net yield of glycolysis per glucose | 2 ATP and 2 NADH |
| Pyruvate to acetyl-CoA, per glucose | 2 NADH and 2 CO2 |
| Krebs cycle per acetyl-CoA | 2 ATP (or GTP), 6 NADH, 2 FADH2, 2 CO2 |
| Direction of DNA and RNA synthesis | 5' to 3' only |

If an option gives a single total ATP yield per glucose, work out which assumption the question has fixed before you accept it. The total shifts with the route cytosolic NADH takes into the mitochondrion, so an option that ignores the assumption is the one to strike out.

**Traps that cost marks.**

- Leading and lagging name the new strands, not the parental ones. Ask which strand is being synthesised.
- The coding strand has the same sequence as the mRNA, with T where the mRNA has U. It is not the strand that is transcribed.
- Dominant and recessive describe what a heterozygote looks like. They say nothing about how common a trait is.
- Independent assortment needs two different chromosomes. Two genes on one chromosome stay linked unless a crossover separates them.
- Mitochondrial traits arrive from the mother. X-linked recessive traits go from carrier mothers to sons, never from father to son.
- More transcription does not mean more protein. Regulation runs at several points at once, and an unstable message or a blocked translation step cancels the extra transcripts.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study

#### Reading a pedigree: the order of the questions

Work these in order and stop when one of them settles the pattern.

1. Does the trait appear in every generation? That is the continuity of a dominant trait.
2. Do two unaffected parents have an affected child? That points to recessive inheritance.
3. Are only males affected? If so, check the affected boy's father. An unaffected father with an affected son points to X-linked recessive.
4. Does an affected mother pass the trait to all of her children? That is mitochondrial.
5. Are affected females commoner than affected males, and does an affected father pass it to every daughter? That is X-linked dominant.

| Feature | Autosomal dominant | Autosomal recessive | X-linked recessive | X-linked dominant | Mitochondrial |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Skips generations | No | Often | Often | No | No |
| Two unaffected parents, affected child | No | Yes | Yes | No | No |
| Father to son transmission | Possible | Possible | Never | Never | Never |
| Affected father passes it to | Half his children | Half his children | All daughters, no sons | All daughters, no sons | None of his children |
| Affected mother passes it to | Half her children | Half her children | All sons affected if she is affected, daughters carriers | All children if homozygous, half if heterozygous | All of them |
| Who is affected more often | Both sexes | Both sexes | Mostly males | Mostly females | Both sexes |

Two checks on that table. An affected daughter with an unaffected father cannot carry an X-linked recessive trait, because she would need a copy from each parent. And a trait that appears in a family with no history of it is not automatically dominant: recessive conditions turn up in families where carriers marry in.

#### Worked example: the pedigree that decides itself

Two unaffected parents have four children: an unaffected daughter, an affected son, an unaffected son, and an affected daughter. The affected daughter has an unaffected husband, and their two children are both unaffected sons.

Unaffected parents producing affected children of both sexes makes this recessive. If it were X-linked recessive, the affected daughter would need an affected father, and he is unaffected. So the pattern is autosomal recessive with two carrier parents, and a carrier by carrier cross gives an expected three affected children for every one unaffected. Two affected out of four sits comfortably inside that expectation.

Now change one fact and the answer changes. Make the father affected and the mother still unaffected. For the affected daughter to be affected, the mother must carry a hidden recessive allele, so the pedigree now shows two different recessive conditions running at once. That is the kind of coincidence the section rarely sets, so read every generation before committing.

#### Replication, transcription and translation under a question stem

The stems tend to describe a change and ask for its consequence. Work the chain out loud: which step is affected, what that step normally does, and what changes when it is blocked or altered.

- A compound that inhibits the proofreading activity of DNA polymerase raises the error rate, so point mutations accumulate faster. It does not change how fast replication runs and it does not affect transcription, because proofreading belongs to DNA synthesis alone.
- A mutation in a promoter region reduces the binding of RNA polymerase, so fewer transcripts are made. The ribosome is untouched and translation is normal for each transcript that is made.
- A mutation in a splice site changes which exons are joined into the mature mRNA, so the protein is missing a stretch or carries extra sequence even though the base sequence changed in one place.
- A mutation in a tRNA anticodon can make the tRNA read the wrong codon or carry the wrong amino acid, which is why a tRNA gene can cause disease while the protein-coding sequence stays untouched.

#### Regulation: two logics, and the stem usually tells you which one

Bacterial regulation runs on operons. Several genes sit behind one promoter and are transcribed together, a repressor protein can bind the operator and switch transcription off, and the lac operon adds a second layer in which CAP, activated by cyclic AMP, recruits RNA polymerase when glucose is scarce.

Eukaryotic regulation runs on individual genes. Transcription factors bind enhancers and silencers that can sit far from the gene, chromatin accessibility decides what can be transcribed at all, and control continues after transcription through splicing, message stability, microRNAs and protein modification.

The exam craft is in noticing which cell you are in. Words such as operator, repressor, polycistronic, attenuation, or the absence of a nucleus point to a bacterium. Words such as intron, splicing, methylation, enhancer or imprinting point to a eukaryote. Where the stem is deliberately vague, the correct answer is usually the one that holds in both, which usually means regulation was a distraction rather than the point.

#### Worked example: the lac operon in words rather than a table

Lactose present and glucose present: allolactose inactivates the repressor so the operator is free, but cyclic AMP is low so CAP cannot bind and RNA polymerase is poorly recruited. Transcription is low rather than zero.

Lactose present and glucose absent: cyclic AMP is high, the CAP-cyclic AMP complex binds upstream, and transcription is high.

Lactose absent: the repressor sits on the operator and transcription stops, whatever the glucose is doing.

Answer with the logic rather than a memorised table, because the stem will move. The tryptophan operon runs the other way: tryptophan acts as a corepressor, and when tryptophan is scarce the operon is expressed so that more tryptophan can be made.

#### Respiration: follow the electrons

Glycolysis runs in the cytoplasm and nets 2 ATP and 2 NADH per glucose. Pyruvate is then converted to acetyl-CoA, which produces more NADH and releases carbon dioxide. The Krebs cycle yields ATP or GTP, NADH and FADH2 and more carbon dioxide per turn, and it runs twice per glucose because there are two acetyl-CoA. Those reduced carriers hand electrons to the electron transport chain in the inner mitochondrial membrane. The energy released pumps protons into the intermembrane space, and the resulting gradient drives ATP synthase, with oxygen as the final electron acceptor reduced to water.

The blockers are worth knowing as a set, because the question usually asks what rises and what falls.

| What is blocked | What rises | What falls |
|---|---|---|
| ATP synthase | Proton gradient | ATP synthesis, electron transport |
| Cytochrome c oxidase | NADH and FADH2 | Proton pumping, oxygen consumption |
| An uncoupler carrying protons across the inner membrane | Oxygen consumption | ATP synthesis, and the energy leaves as heat |
| Oxygen supply | NADH | Everything downstream of the chain |

An uncoupler is the classic item. The gradient dissipates, so electron transport speeds up and oxygen consumption rises while ATP production falls. Thermogenesis in brown fat works the same way, which is how a body generates heat without doing mechanical work.

#### Common mistakes

Confusing the coding and template strands is the most frequent error, and it flips the direction of everything else in the question. The second is reading "dominant" as "common" and rejecting a recessive trait because it is uncommon in the population. The third is quoting an ATP total without checking which stage the question has blocked. The fourth is answering an operon question from a memorised table when the stem has changed one condition.

### 🔴 Extended — Mastery

#### The same biology in two cell types

In a bacterium, transcription and translation are coupled: ribosomes begin translating a message while RNA polymerase is still making it. In a eukaryote the nucleus separates the two, and the transcript must be capped, polyadenylated and spliced before it is exported for translation. Three consequences follow, and each is a question stem in its own right. Introns exist in eukaryotes and mostly do not in bacteria, so a mutation inside an intron is harmless in one cell and can be fatal in the other. Antibiotics that hit the bacterial ribosome leave the eukaryotic one alone because the two ribosomes differ. And a eukaryotic gene can be regulated long after transcription begins, which has no bacterial equivalent.

#### Pedigree cases that break the simple rules

Four situations defeat a mechanical reading of the inheritance table. A mother whose mitochondria are a mix of normal and faulty copies can have an unaffected child, because mitochondria are inherited in mixed proportions and disease appears only once the faulty fraction crosses a threshold. Repeat-expansion disorders appear earlier and more severely in each generation, and that anticipation is the single pedigree clue that points at the mutation type. Recessive conditions turn up in families with no history of the trait whenever a carrier marries in, so rarity does not rule recessivity out. And an affected daughter with an unaffected father rules out X-linked recessive inheritance outright, which is worth checking before anything else.

#### Classifying a change by what it does to the product

| The change | The class | What happens to the protein |
|---|---|---|
| Base substitution, same amino acid | Silent | Protein is unchanged, although splicing or message stability can still be disturbed |
| Base substitution, different amino acid | Missense | Tolerated or destabilising, depending on how conserved the position is |
| Base substitution creating a stop codon | Nonsense | Truncated protein, usually destroyed by nonsense-mediated decay |
| Insertion or deletion not in multiples of three | Frameshift | Everything downstream is wrong and a premature stop usually follows |
| Insertion or deletion of three bases | In-frame | One amino acid added or lost, sometimes harmless |
| Gain or loss of whole chromosome sets | Aneuploidy | Whole-syndrome effects, as in trisomy 21 or monosomy X |
| A segment that moves or swaps | Rearrangement | Can fuse two genes into one, as in the Philadelphia chromosome, or break a gene |
| Expansion of a repeated sequence | Repeat expansion | Later onset and greater severity in later generations |

The silent case is the one worth double-checking, because students read "silent" as "no effect" and move on. The amino acid is unchanged, but a base substitution can create or destroy a splice site, and a message that is mis-spliced is degraded instead of translated.

#### Control points in a eukaryotic gene

Expression can be stopped at several separate places, and a question will usually point at one of them. Chromatin decides access: histone modification, DNA methylation and imprinting all change whether a promoter can be used at all. Transcription factors and enhancers decide how often transcription starts, and an enhancer can sit far away from the gene it controls. RNA processing decides which version of the message leaves the nucleus, which is how one gene can make several proteins. RNA interference and microRNAs reduce message stability and slow translation. Phosphorylation and the ubiquitin-proteasome system decide how long a finished protein lasts.

The exam point is that the same genetic change can behave completely differently depending on which layer it lands in. Methylating a promoter silences a gene without changing a single base of its sequence. Imprinting shows the same principle from the other end: the same deletion in the same region produces a different syndrome depending on which parent passed it on.

#### Control points in respiration

Respiration is not one reaction but four linked ones, and the interesting biology is in the control. Substrate availability sets the ceiling, since no glycolysis happens without glucose. Enzyme control sets the rate, and the enzyme that commits pyruvate to acetyl-CoA is inhibited when the cell already has plenty of energy. The proton gradient sets the yield, and it is the only part that can be uncoupled from the electron transport chain feeding it. When oxygen runs out the chain stops, NADH accumulates, and glycolysis stalls as well unless lactate dehydrogenase regenerates the NAD+ that glycolysis depends on.

#### Write the chain before you open the options

For every item in this topic, write one line: given X, this changes Y, so the observable result is Z. If the line cannot be finished, the item is not understood yet, and reading the options will not help. Once the line exists, test each option against it rather than against your memory of the topic.

#### Where marks are actually lost

| Symptom | Where it comes from | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Direction errors | Coding and template strands mixed up, or leading and lagging named from the parental strand | Ask which strand is being built, and in which direction |
| Ratio errors | A 3:1 ratio applied to a testcross, or to genes on the same chromosome | Work out the cross before you write the ratio |
| Total errors | An ATP total quoted without checking what the question has blocked | Name the stage first, then the yield |
