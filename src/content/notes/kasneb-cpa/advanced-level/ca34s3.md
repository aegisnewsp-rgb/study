# CA34S3: Advanced Management Accounting

Read the paper code before you read anything else. KASNEB's own qualification page lists Advanced Management Accounting as **CA34S3**, and the three specialisation papers as **CA34S1** Advanced Taxation, **CA34S2** Advanced Auditing and Assurance and **CA34S4** Advanced Public Financial Management. A number of third-party sources and older syllabi list Advanced Management Accounting as CA34 and the specialisations as CA35S1, CA35S2 and CA35S3.

This page follows KASNEB. If you are building a study timetable from a source that uses the older codes, check it against KASNEB first — a candidate who registers for the wrong paper has lost a sitting and a fee.

Beyond the papers, three further requirements sit alongside them: CA35P Business Data Analytics, a practical paper; CA36WE, a Workshop on Ethics; and CA37WP, a Workshop on Work Simulation for candidates who do not yet have one year of relevant practical experience.

## What "advanced" changes

The Intermediate Level teaches you the technique. The Advanced Level examines judgement about when the technique applies and what it costs.

Relevant costing is the clearest example. At Intermediate you learn to exclude fixed costs from a decision because they will not change. At Advanced you are asked what happens when the constraint is capacity rather than demand, when a qualitative factor outweighs a small quantitative gain, when the fixed cost is avoidable after all, or when the information is simply too unreliable to decide. Each of those is a different answer and none of them is found by plugging into the same formula.

Same pattern with performance measurement. Throughput accounting, balanced scorecards, responsibility accounting and transfer pricing each have a case where they are the right tool and a case where they mislead. Advanced questions are almost always about the second case.

## Transfer pricing is the Advanced signature

A transfer price is the price at which one division supplies another. The methods are cost-based, market-based, negotiated and dual, and the choice determines the motivation of both divisions.

A transfer price set at market puts no incentive on the supplying division to reduce its own cost, because the benefit goes to the buying division. A transfer price at full cost transfers profit between divisions without changing the total, which makes divisional performance measures misleading. A negotiated price sets both incentives but needs negotiation every period.

An item that gives you the same cost and market transfer prices and asks why a division is resisting one of them is testing this directly. Reason from the incentives, not from the arithmetic.

## Modern approaches and their limits

Throughput accounting treats all costs except the truly variable ones as a single period cost, which is useful when a constraint limits throughput and misleading when the bottleneck is elsewhere.

Balanced scorecards add non-financial measures to a purely financial assessment and reduce the distortion caused by single-metric evaluation, but their weakness is that a measure selected for its strategic relevance still has to be measured, and the selection is a judgement that can be wrong.

Levers of value and absorbers, driver-based costing, activity-based costing, target costing and kaizen costing are all examinable and all share a weakness the exam tests: they require data that an organisation may not have.

## Common mistakes

Planning a revision timetable from outdated paper codes. Confirm against KASNEB.

Applying a relevant-costing conclusion without asking whether the constraint is demand or capacity. The answer changes completely.

Treating a transfer price as an accounting fact rather than an incentive. The number is a decision, and the decision changes behaviour.

Forgetting that divisional performance measures can mislead even when every individual calculation is correct. The distortion is in the measure, not the arithmetic.

Assuming activity-based costing is always better than traditional absorption costing. It is more accurate where the overhead drivers are genuinely diverse and expensive to allocate, and less accurate — and much more costly to operate — where they are not.

Leaving CA35P, CA36WE and CA37WP off the plan because they are not written papers. They are assessed separately.
