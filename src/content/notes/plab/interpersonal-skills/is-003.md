# Explaining Bad News and Difficult Issues in PLAB 2

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review

The GMC assesses interpersonal skills as one of three domains in every PLAB 2 station, alongside data gathering and clinical management. That means it is scored in every station you sit, not in a separate "communication" station — 16 of 16. A candidate who is diagnostically excellent and says nothing back to the patient loses the interpersonal score in each one of them.

The skills that mark well here are unglamorous and learnable: open properly, listen visibly, explain in plain language, check understanding, and respond to emotion rather than ignoring it.

**The interpersonal marks live in the reaction, not the explanation.**

- Most of the clock spent on clinical detail leaves nothing for the part actually being scored
- Budget the opening minute to yourself — silence, seating, permission, privacy
- Explanation belongs in short chunks with a pause between, so a reaction has somewhere to arrive
- Reserve the closing minute for checking understanding and agreeing the next step

**The first part of any structure is the part candidates skip.**

- Establish whether you can talk privately and whether the patient wants anyone present
- Ask what they already think is going on before telling them
- Ask how much detail they want; most people want more than you expect and fewer than they receive
- Naming what you can see out loud earns marks that clinical accuracy does not

**Plain language is a skill, not a courtesy.**

- Saying the cells have stopped responding is honest where marker language is not
- Jargon used as protection transmits your distress without reaching the patient
- Do not withhold the difficult word until after a long buildup
- Do not use a euphemism as a way of not saying it at all

**Two sentences decide how the station is remembered.**

- Naming the emotion and pausing usually scores more than any clinical sentence
- Reassurance you cannot support is the most damaging thing available to you in that room
- Answering an emotional question with a fact leaves the emotion untouched
- Teach-back is marked, and asking is not the same as asking

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study

#### The eight minutes are not evenly spent

The commonest failure in a bad-news station is spending the first six minutes on clinical explanation and having two minutes left when the patient reacts. The reaction is where the interpersonal marks are.

Budget roughly the first minute for yourself — silence, sit down, check the room, ask permission. The next three for finding out what the patient already understands and what they most want to know. The next three for the explanation in small chunks with pauses. The last minute for checking understanding and agreeing what happens next.

Delivering the news in one unbroken paragraph is the mistake that costs the most marks, because it makes it impossible to respond to anything.

#### SPIKES, in plain language

A standard stepwise structure exists and is worth knowing, but the value is in its shape rather than its name: setting, perception, invitation, knowledge, emotion, strategy, summary.

The parts most often skipped are the first three. Setting means checking you can talk privately and whether the patient wants company. Perception means asking what they already think is going on before you tell them — which surfaces the gap between what you know and what they have imagined, and it lets you respond to their actual fear rather than the one you assumed. Invitation means asking how much detail they want.

Knowledge means the explanation itself, delivered in short chunks with pauses. Plain language is a skill, not politeness: "the cells have stopped responding to the treatment" is honest in a way "your markers are not responding" is not. Avoid jargon, avoid euphemism delivered as avoidance, and do not use the word "cancer" only after three minutes of buildup.

Emotion means stopping and naming what you see. "This is clearly frightening news, and I'd like to pause for a moment." That sentence routinely earns more marks than any clinical sentence.

#### Reacting when the patient breaks

Silence is correct. Filling it with more clinical information is the classic error. Let the pause last, let them speak, and do not move on to the plan until they have.

Avoid false reassurance. "It\'s probably nothing" after you have told someone it might be cancer is the most damaging thing you can say, and examiners watch for it. Equally, avoid catastrophising: state what you know, what you do not, and what the next step is.

Avoid asking what they think they can do, or offering to come back later. Both transfer the work to the patient at the worst moment.

#### Common mistakes

Starting the explanation before you have checked what the patient already knows. You will then explain at the wrong level and talk past their actual concerns.

Delivering all the information at once. Chunk it, pause, and let the reaction arrive where you have time to handle it.

Using jargon to protect yourself from the emotion. It communicates distress without communicating to the patient.

Forgetting to check understanding at the end. Teach-back is a scored behaviour: "so I know I have explained that clearly — can you tell me in your own words what we are doing next?" Asking is not the same as asking.

Confusing respecting silence with avoiding the conversation. Silence after a pause is communication; silence because you are uncomfortable is not.

Answering an emotional question with a fact. "Am I going to die?" needs an acknowledgement before it needs information.

### 🔴 Extended — Mastery

#### What the opening minute is actually for

The first minute is the only part of the station with no clinical task attached to it, which is exactly why candidates spend it on one. Silence, checking the room, sitting down if the patient is standing, and asking whether it is all right to talk — none of that advances the medicine, and all of it sets the conditions under which the rest is received.

There is a marking logic underneath. An examiner watching a candidate open badly will discount the explanation that follows, because the candidate has demonstrated that the explanation is the priority rather than the person. Opening properly is therefore not politeness that happens to be scored; it is the first piece of evidence that you understood what the station measures.

Doing it under time pressure is harder than it sounds, because the impulse to move on is strongest exactly when the scenario feels urgent. Rehearse the minute as a fixed sequence so that under pressure you perform it rather than decide on it.

#### Why asking what they already think is worth the time it costs

Finding out what the patient believes before correcting it is the step most often dropped, and it is the step that makes everything after it land. What they have already constructed is usually the real source of the distress, and it is rarely the diagnosis itself. Someone who has searched their symptoms at one in the morning has a specific and usually inaccurate model of their illness, and an explanation delivered without hearing that model talks straight past it.

It also saves time. Knowing what they think tells you where to begin and what to expand, rather than starting from a fixed script regardless of who is in front of you. In a bad-news station that is the difference between three minutes well spent and three minutes repeated back at you.

The habit to practise is a question, asked early, followed by a pause long enough that the answer can be incomplete. Candidates fill that pause. The pause is the scored part.

#### Silence, and the two ways it can be wrong

Silence after an explanation is doing work: it gives the patient somewhere to put what they have just heard. Silence because you do not know what to say is a different silence, reads as avoidance, and the difference is visible even to an examiner who cannot hear you.

The practical rule is that you do not move to the plan until the reaction has been handled, whatever length that takes. Running ahead of the emotion is the error that costs marks across the whole station, because the patient stops hearing anything clinical once they have felt unheard.

This is also where unsupported reassurance does its damage. Reassurance offered to relieve your own discomfort rather than theirs is the one response that makes the next conversation harder. Separate what is established from what is still uncertain, name the next step, and stop there — that sequence is honest, and it does not require you to predict an outcome you cannot.

#### Understanding is not demonstrated by nodding

The closing minute scores on something specific: whether the patient can restate the plan in their own words. Asking whether they understand produces a yes from almost everyone, including people who understood nothing, so the behaviour being marked has to be the one that actually tests it.

Teach-back is not a test of the patient either, and it is worth saying so aloud when you use it. Framed as checking your own explanation, it is a natural thing to do and it removes the sense of being examined. It also surfaces the specific part that failed to land, which lets you re-explain that part rather than repeat the whole thing.

Candidates who skip this usually skip it because they are running late — the same time pressure that caused the opening to be rushed. Both errors come from treating the interpersonal domain as something that happens in the gaps between the clinical work, rather than as the medium through which the clinical work is delivered.
