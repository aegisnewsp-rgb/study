# Quantitative Reasoning — UCAT ANZ Notes

Quantitative Reasoning is the third subtest of the UCAT ANZ. It assesses the candidate's ability to critically evaluate information presented in a numerical form. The subtest includes numerical problem solving, percentages, ratios, rates, data interpretation from tables and charts, and basic arithmetic and estimation. The on-screen calculator is available.

> Re-check the live test format and any updates on https://www.ucat.edu.au/about-ucat-anz/test-format/ before planning revision around these figures.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Subtest structure.** Tables, charts and short numerical problems followed by multiple-choice items.
- **On-screen calculator.** Available for every question. But typing in calculations is slow — use mental arithmetic and estimation first.
- **Time pressure.** Approximately 40 seconds per item.

#### Examiner traps

- Reading tables incorrectly — note the units carefully.
- Using the calculator for simple arithmetic — slows you down.

---

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Percentages

"X% of Y" = X/100 × Y. "Increase by r%" means multiply by (1 + r/100). "Decrease by r%" means multiply by (1 − r/100).

#### Ratios

a:b is equivalent to a×k : b×k for any positive k. To find "one part": divide total by sum of ratio parts.

#### Rates

Speed = distance/time. Density = mass/volume. Concentration = amount/total.

#### Data interpretation

Read the table header. Note the units. Cross-reference rows and columns. Estimate before calculating.

#### Why Quantitative Reasoning rewards arithmetic fluency

- Mental arithmetic is faster than calculator for simple calculations.

#### Worked example — percentage change and reverse percentage

A price rose from $80 to $92.

```
  Increase = 92 − 80 = $12
  % increase = 12 / 80 = 0.15 = 15%
```

Reverse: a sale price is $92 after a 20% discount. Find the original.

```
  sale = original × (1 − 0.20) = original × 0.80
  92   = original × 0.80
  original = 92 / 0.80 = $115
```

Common mistake: adding 20% of $92 ($18.40) to $92, giving $110.40. The discount was applied to the original, so the recovery operation is division by 0.80.

#### Worked example — ratio partition

Divide 240 in the ratio 3:5.

```
  Total parts = 3 + 5 = 8
  One part = 240 / 8 = 30
  First share = 3 × 30 = 90
  Second share = 5 × 30 = 150
```

Check: 90 + 150 = 240 ✓. For three-way ratios, the same method applies: total parts is the sum of all three.

#### Worked example — rate problem with unit conversion

A car travels 180 km in 2 hours 30 minutes.

```
  Time = 2.5 h
  Speed = 180 / 2.5 = 72 km/h
```

In m/s:

```
  72 km/h × (1000 m / 1 km) × (1 h / 3600 s)
  = 72 × 1000 / 3600
  = 20 m/s
```

Memorising the conversion factor 1 m/s = 3.6 km/h is faster than re-deriving it.

#### Worked example — reading a table

A table shows annual sales (in $ thousands) for a small retailer:

```
  Year   Q1   Q2   Q3   Q4
  2022  120  150  100  180
  2023  140  160  110  200
  2024  130  175  125  210
```

What is the percentage change in Q4 sales from 2022 to 2024?

```
  Change = 210 − 180 = 30
  % change = 30 / 180 = 0.1667 = 16.67%
```

Estimate first: 210 is roughly 17% above 180. The exact answer matches. If your estimate and exact answer disagree, redo the calculation.

#### Worked example — percentage as a fraction of a quantity

A medication order is 250 mg. The stock concentration is 50 mg/mL. What volume should be drawn up?

```
  Volume = dose / concentration
         = 250 mg / 50 mg/mL
         = 5 mL
```

UCAT ANZ tests drug-dose and dilution arithmetic heavily in the health-professions cohort. Always carry units and check that the final unit matches the requested one.

#### Common marking-scheme (or scoring) mistakes

- Reading a table row from the wrong year or quarter when the question asks for a specific period.
- Treating percentages as additive. A 20% rise and then a 20% fall does not return to the start; it lands at 96% of the original (multiply by 1.20, then by 0.80).
- Using the wrong denominator for a reverse-percentage question (multiplying by 1.20 instead of dividing by 0.80).
- Converting km/h to m/s without the factor 1000/3600 = 5/18, or 1 m/s = 3.6 km/h.
- Reaching for the on-screen calculator for trivial arithmetic. Mental math is faster for single-digit operations.

#### 20-minute recap before you walk in

- Estimate first, calculate second. The estimate catches unit errors.
- For reverse percentages, divide by (1 − rate), do not multiply by (1 + rate).
- For ratios, divide the total by the sum of the parts to find one part.
- For rates, carry the unit through the calculation.
- Tables: read the row and column headers, then read the cell. Check the units.
