# Reading Comprehension Fundamentals

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your MAT exam.

Reading Comprehension (RC) tests how actively you build meaning from a passage by combining decoded words, context, and prior knowledge. On MAT, RC contributes roughly 20–24 questions from 4–5 passages of 300–600 words each, drawn from editorials, business journals, scientific essays, and literary criticism. Strong RC performance disproportionately lifts your sectional percentile because each passage multiplies into 4–5 marks.

| Key Term | Working Definition |
|---|---|
| **Central Idea** | The single thought the entire passage argues or explains |
| **Inference** | A conclusion the author implies but never states outright |
| **Tone** | The author's attitude (sceptical, laudatory, ironic, neutral) |
| **Paraphrase** | Restating a sentence in different words without changing meaning |
| **Skimming** | Reading quickly to capture structure and main argument |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **S-E-A-R** — **S**kim passage for structure, **E**ye question stems for keywords, **A**nnotate transitions and opinion markers, **R**eturn to text for evidence before locking each option. Combine with the rule: *"Qualifiers save you — extreme words like always/never usually kill an MAT inference choice."*

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months before MAT.

#### How Meaning Is Built
Reading comprehension is the interaction of three strands per the Simple View of Reading: **decoding** (recognising words), **language comprehension** (grasping syntax and vocabulary), and **prior knowledge** (schemata). If any strand is weak, comprehension collapses — which is why MAT questions often hinge on vocabulary-in-context even when the passage looks easy.

#### Micro vs Macro Skills
MAT rewards two skill clusters. Micro-skills ask you to decode literal meaning, infer word sense from surrounding clauses, detect fact/opinion splits, and identify tone. Macro-skills ask you to extract the central idea, map passage structure (cause-effect, compare-contrast, problem-solution, chronological), and weigh primary versus supporting arguments.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Distractor Pair | What Examiners Test | How to Tell Apart |
|---|---|---|
| Central Idea vs Paragraph Main Idea | Whether you zoom out to the whole passage or stay inside one paragraph | Drop the paragraph test: if the thought holds across all paragraphs, it is central; otherwise it is local |
| Inference vs Assumption | Whether the author states the idea by implication or leaves it unstated as a premise | Assumption = what must be true for the claim to hold; Inference = what follows logically from stated facts |
| Fact vs Opinion | Whether a statement can be verified externally or reflects judgement | Look for evaluative adjectives (egregious, brilliant) and modal verbs (should, must) — those flag opinion |
| Tone vs Purpose | Whether you read the author's attitude or his reason for writing | Tone = how it feels (sceptical, mournful); Purpose = why it exists (to persuade, to inform, to critique) |
| Skimming vs Scanning | Speed versus precision of reading | Skim for gist; scan with a fixed target (date, name, number) |

#### Strategy Cycle for MAT Passages

1. Read the question stems first and underline the operative verb — *infer, imply, author would most likely agree, tone, title*.
2. Skim the passage in 60–90 seconds, bracketing each paragraph's job and circling transition words (however, therefore, by contrast).
3. Mark opinion markers — *must, ought, regrettably, clearly* — they telegraph the author's stance.
4. Return to each question and locate the sentence or paragraph that anchors the answer.
5. Eliminate options using the **word-trap test**: if an option copies passage phrasing but flips polarity, drop it.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** *A passage argues that traditional classroom assessment measures memorisation rather than reasoning, and concludes that schools should redesign tests to include open-ended problem-solving tasks. Which of the following is the most reasonable inference from the passage?*

(A) Open-ended assessments are easier to design than multiple-choice tests.
(B) Schools that continue using rote-based tests will fall behind in global rankings.
(C) Measuring reasoning ability requires assessment formats different from those currently dominant.
(D) The author believes all multiple-choice testing should be abolished immediately.

#### Solution:

1. Locate the anchor sentence — *"traditional classroom assessment measures memorisation rather than reasoning."*
2. Identify the operative verb in the stem: *inference* = something the author implies but does not state.
3. Test each option against the text:
   - (A) "easier to design" — never mentioned; pure invention. Eliminate.
   - (B) "fall behind in global rankings" — the passage says nothing about rankings. Eliminate.
   - (C) "Measuring reasoning ability requires assessment formats different from those currently dominant" — this is a direct logical restatement of the contrast drawn (memorisation-focused tests ≠ reasoning-focused tests). Keep.
   - (D) "all multiple-choice testing should be abolished immediately" — the author proposes *redesign*, not abolition; "immediately" is unsupported. Eliminate.
4. **Answer: (C).**

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Option (D) uses strong language (*all, abolished, immediately*) that the passage never endorses. Option (C) is correct because it preserves the author's actual claim without adding unstated extremes — a classic MAT inference pattern.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer MAT preparation timeline.

#### Advanced Question Typology

| Question Type | Trigger Phrases | Resolution Move |
|---|---|---|
| **Author's Attitude** | "The author's tone is best described as…" | Scan for opinion markers and emotive adjectives across the full passage |
| **Title Selection** | "Which title best suits the passage?" | Match scope: reject any title narrower than the central idea or wider than the text supports |
| **True/False/Can't Say** | "Which statement is true/false/cannot be determined?" | Apply the **evidence rule**: only mark True if a passage sentence directly states it; only mark Can't Say if the text is silent and no reasonable inference bridges the gap |
| **Logical Structure** | "The passage proceeds by…" | Identify the dominant pattern — cause-effect, compare-contrast, problem-solution, definition-example |
| **Vocabulary in Context** | "As used in line X, 'X' most nearly means…" | Substitute each choice into the original sentence and test collocational fit |

#### Edge Cases and Boundary Conditions

- **Mixed tone**: MAT passages occasionally shift from laudatory to critical mid-paragraph. When two options both feel plausible, choose the one that dominates across the *full* passage, not the most recent paragraph.
- **Can't Say vs Inference**: Use the bridge test. If you can write one logical step linking the premise to the conclusion without adding new facts, it is an inference. If the bridge requires an external fact the passage never supplies, it is Can't Say.
- **Paraphrase traps**: MAT often presents a technically correct paraphrase of a passage sentence as a wrong option because it inverts causality ("X caused Y" vs "Y caused X"). Always compare the direction of the relationship, not just the entities.
- **Speed-accuracy frontier**: Target 150–200 words per minute for first read, then re-read targeted sentences at native pace. If you finish a passage in under 3 minutes and your accuracy falls below 75%, slow the first pass by 30 seconds.

#### Common Mistakes Specific to MAT RC

1. Choosing an option that contains passage words but reverses the original meaning.
2. Confusing the central idea with a vivid supporting detail.
3. Treating a hypothesis as a fact because the author describes it plausibly.
4. Ignoring pronoun referents and misattributing a claim to the wrong actor.
5. Picking "can't say" defensively when a defensible inference exists from two passage sentences combined.
6. Spending 8+ minutes on one passage and starving later, easier ones of time.

#### Two Advanced Practice Prompts

1. Take any 500-word editorial from *The Hindu Business Line* or *The Economist*. Skim in 75 seconds, write a 25-word central idea, then answer five questions: one central idea, one inference, one tone, one vocabulary-in-context, one logical structure. Time yourself strictly.
2. Convert one MAT-style passage into a question set of your own, deliberately inserting one word-trap distractor and one extreme-language distractor. Solving your own traps trains you to recognise them on the actual exam.

> 📌 **Formula Check:** MAT RC has no fixed numerical formula, but the practical throughput equation is *Score = Passages Attempted × (Accuracy per Passage)*.
