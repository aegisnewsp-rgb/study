# Real Numbers, Ratio, Percentage, Indices and Surds — Junior Cycle Mathematics Notes

The Number strand at Junior Cycle asks you to move between representations of the same quantity without losing your place. A value can be a fraction, a decimal, a percentage or a ratio; an expression can be rewritten with negative, zero or fractional indices; a surd can be simplified or rationalised. The learning outcomes are not about doing these operations once — they are about choosing the representation that suits the question and justifying the change you made.

## Placing a value in the number system

The sets nest: ℕ ⊂ ℤ ⊂ ℚ ⊂ ℝ.

- **Natural numbers** count: 1, 2, 3 …
- **Integers** include zero and negatives.
- **Rationals** are fractions *p*/*q* with *q* ≠ 0, including all terminating and all recurring decimals.
- **Irrational** numbers are real but not expressible as a fraction: √2, π, e.

Two consequences worth being able to state. Every integer is rational, because *n* = *n*/1. And a decimal is rational if and only if it is terminating or recurring — a non-recurring non-terminating decimal is irrational. When you are asked whether a decimal is rational, do not rely on how it looks; establish whether it repeats.

## Ratio and proportion

**Ratio** compares two quantities of the same kind. Write it as *a* : *b*, or as the fraction *a*/*b* when you are going to do something with it.

**Proportion** states that two ratios are equal, *a*/*b* = *c*/*d*. Three ways to solve:

1. **Cross-multiply**: *ad* = *bc*, then solve. Fastest and always valid.
2. **The fraction of an amount method**: identify what *b*/*d* represents and apply it to *a*. Often clearer when the numbers are awkward.
3. **Unitary method**: work out the value of one, then multiply up. Slower but reliable when you are not sure which proportion is intended.

**Ratio in a shared quantity.** "In the ratio 3 : 5, what fraction of the total is the 3?" — 3/8. Getting this wrong (using 3/5) is the single most common ratio error, and the question usually disambiguates by saying "what fraction of the total".

**Sharing in a ratio** is the same calculation going forwards: total the parts, then divide.

## Percentage

**Percentage change.** New value = original × (1 + *r*), where *r* is written as a decimal. A rise means adding the rate; a fall means subtracting it. "Increased by 40%" is × 1.4; "increased *to* 140%" is × 1.4 of the original too, but "increased *by* 140%" is × 2.4. The prepositions carry the meaning and the exam tests it.

**Percentage point versus percentage.** A rate rising from 5% to 8% is a rise of **3 percentage points** but a rise of **60%**. Writing "it increased by 3%" is wrong. This distinction is assessed directly.

**Percentage of a percentage.** 15% of 40% of €200 is 0.15 × 0.40 × 200. Multiply the rates — do not add them.

**Reverse percentage.** To find the original when you are given a percentage of it, divide. Finding the original when a figure is "20% more than the original" means dividing by 1.2; the increase and the original are different quantities and this is the error the question is built around.

**Interest** is compound when the calculation repeats on the new balance, and simple when it repeats on the original principal. Irish Junior Cycle financial work sits close to this boundary, so read the wording: "simple interest" and "compound interest" are named.

## Indices

**Negative indices** put the term in the denominator. **Zero index** gives 1. **Fractional indices** are roots: *a*^(1/3) = ∛*a*.

Multiplying adds indices, dividing subtracts them, a power of a power multiplies them. The inverse-reading skill matters: given an expression with *x* in the numerator and a negative index, rewrite it with positive indices and solve.

**Order of precedence** — brackets, then indices, then multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction. When in doubt, substitute brackets. The Junior Cycle exam rewards a candidate who inserts brackets to show what a formula means, and the marks for reasoning are available in algebra questions.

## Surds

A surd is an irrational root that cannot be simplified: √2, √3, √5.

**Simplify** by taking out perfect squares: √72 = 6√2, √50 = 5√2, √75 = 5√3, √12 = 2√3. Reducing is not optional — √72 is not an acceptable final answer.

**Combine like surds only.** √8 + √18 = 2√2 + 3√2 = 5√2, because both reduce to the same surd. √2 + √3 stays as it is; unlike surds never cancel to a rational number.

**Multiply and divide** as fractions: √*a* × √*b* = √(*ab*), √*a* ÷ √*b* = √(*a*/*b*), then reduce.

**Rationalise the denominator** by multiplying by the conjugate:

√2 / (3 − √2) × (3 + √2)/(3 + √2) = (3√2 + 2)/(9 − 2) = (3√2 + 2)/7

Write the conjugate multiplication as a step. It is a mark.

**Estimating surds.** Knowing √2 ≈ 1.41, √3 ≈ 1.73 and √5 ≈ 2.24 lets you sanity-check an answer. A result that should be about 7 and comes out at 0.7 has a decimal error, not an arithmetic one — and checking takes two seconds.

## Self-check

1. A value increases from 25% to 30%. Describe the change two ways. *(An increase of 5 percentage points; an increase of 20% relative to the original, because 5 ÷ 25 = 0.2. Calling it "a 5% increase" is the error.)*
2. Share €96 between two people in the ratio 5 : 3. *(Five parts plus three is eight parts, so each part is 96 ÷ 8 = 12. The shares are 60 and 36.)*
3. Simplify √108. *(√(36 × 3) = 6√3. Leaving √108 forfeits the simplification mark.)*
4. Why is 3/8 the right answer to "in the ratio 3 : 5, what fraction is the first part?" *(Because the two parts together make the whole, so the first part is 3 out of 3 + 5 = 8 parts. Using 3/5 answers a different question — the ratio of the first part to the second, not its share of the total.)*
5. A price is reduced by 25% and then increased by 25%. Is the original price recovered? *(No. The multiplier is 0.75 × 1.25 = 0.9375, so the price is 6.25% below the original. Percentage changes do not cancel unless one of them is measured against the new base, which this one is not.)*
