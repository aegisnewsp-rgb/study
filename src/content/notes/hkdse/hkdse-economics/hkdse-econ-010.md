# J. International Trade and Finance — HKDSE Economics Notes

This topic covers Topic J of the Compulsory Part of HKDSE Economics. It addresses comparative advantage and the gains from trade; trade protection (tariffs, quotas, subsidies); the World Trade Organization and regional trade agreements; the balance of payments (current account, capital and financial account); exchange rates (floating, fixed, managed); and the Linked Exchange Rate system in Hong Kong.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Comparative advantage**: a country has a comparative advantage in producing a good if its opportunity cost is lower than that of another country.
- **Absolute advantage**: a country has an absolute advantage if it can produce a good using fewer resources.
- **Gains from trade**: total output rises when countries specialise according to comparative advantage and trade.
- **Terms of trade**: the ratio of export prices to import prices. A rise in terms of trade is beneficial for the exporting country.
- **Trade protection**:
  - **Tariff**: a tax on imports. Raises the domestic price, reduces imports, generates government revenue.
  - **Quota**: a quantitative limit on imports. Domestic price rises, imports fall, quota rent goes to foreign producers (under an import quota) or domestic importers (under a tariff-quota).
  - **Subsidy**: a payment to domestic producers. Lowers their cost, raises domestic output, reduces imports.
  - **Administrative barriers**: red tape, regulations, licensing, etc.
- **World Trade Organization (WTO)**: the multilateral trade body that promotes trade liberalisation and settles trade disputes.
- **Regional trade agreements**: ASEAN, the EU, the USMCA, the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP), the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP).
- **Balance of payments**: a record of all economic transactions between residents of a country and the rest of the world.
  - **Current account**: trade in goods and services, primary income, secondary income.
  - **Capital and financial account**: cross-border investment, changes in reserve assets.
- **Exchange rate regimes**:
  - **Floating**: determined by supply and demand in the foreign exchange market.
  - **Fixed**: pegged by the central bank.
  - **Managed (dirty float)**: mostly floating, but with central bank intervention.
  - **Currency union**: shared currency (e.g. the euro).
- **Hong Kong's Linked Exchange Rate**: HKD pegged to USD at HK$7.80 = USD 1 since 1983.

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing comparative advantage with absolute advantage — comparative advantage is about opportunity costs.
- Treating tariffs and quotas as equivalent — they differ in who captures the quota rent.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Comparative advantage and gains from trade

**Comparative advantage**: the ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another producer.

**Example**: Country A and Country B both produce wheat and cloth. Country A's opportunity cost of 1 unit of cloth is 2 units of wheat; Country B's opportunity cost of 1 unit of cloth is 1 unit of wheat. Country B has comparative advantage in cloth; Country A has comparative advantage in wheat.

If both countries specialise according to comparative advantage and trade, total world output rises. Both countries gain.

**Assumptions**: the analysis assumes constant opportunity costs, no trade barriers, no transport costs, perfect competition, and full employment. In reality, the gains from trade are reduced by transport costs, trade barriers, and adjustment costs.

#### Trade protection

**Tariff**: a tax on imports.

- Effects: domestic price rises by less than the tariff; quantity imported falls; government revenue rises (= tariff × imports); CS falls; PS rises; deadweight loss = triangle.
- Types: specific (fixed amount per unit), ad valorem (percentage of value).

**Quota**: a quantitative limit on imports.

- Effects: domestic price rises; quantity imported is fixed; CS falls; PS rises; quota rent (= price difference × quantity) goes to the foreign producers (under import quota) or to the importers or government (under tariff quota).
- Compared to a tariff: a quota caps the quantity directly, while a tariff raises the price.

**Subsidy to domestic producers**:

- Effects: domestic output rises; imports fall; PS rises; CS rises (lower price); government cost = subsidy × domestic output; deadweight loss = triangle.

**Other protection**: administrative barriers, regulations, licensing, voluntary export restraints (VERs).

**Arguments for protection**:

- **Infant industry**: new industries need protection to develop.
- **National security**: protect industries critical for defence.
- **Strategic trade**: help domestic firms gain market share in oligopolistic markets.
- **Anti-dumping**: protect domestic firms from unfair foreign competition.
- **Terms of trade**: a large country can improve its terms of trade with an optimal tariff.

**Arguments against protection**: higher prices for consumers, reduced competition, retaliation, inefficiency.

#### WTO and regional trade agreements

**WTO**:

- Replaced the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) in 1995.
- Promotes trade liberalisation through negotiations (trade rounds).
- Settles trade disputes between members.
- 164 members (as of 2024).

**Regional trade agreements**: ASEAN, EU, USMCA, RCEP, CPTPP. Reduce or eliminate tariffs between members.

#### Balance of payments

**Current account**:

- **Trade in goods (visible trade)**: exports - imports of physical goods.
- **Trade in services (invisible trade)**: exports - imports of services.
- **Primary income**: investment income (interest, dividends), compensation of employees.
- **Secondary income**: transfers (foreign aid, remittances).

**Capital and financial account**:

- **Capital account**: capital transfers, transactions in intangible assets (patents, copyrights).
- **Financial account**: foreign direct investment, portfolio investment, other investment, reserve assets.

**Accounting identity**: current account + capital and financial account = 0 (in principle; statistical discrepancies exist).

#### Exchange rate regimes

**Floating**: the exchange rate is determined by supply and demand in the foreign exchange market. The central bank may intervene occasionally.

**Fixed**: the central bank pegs the exchange rate by buying and selling foreign currency.

**Managed float**: the exchange rate is mostly floating, but the central bank intervenes to smooth volatility or maintain a target.

**Currency board**: a strict form of fixed exchange rate; the central bank holds foreign reserves equal to the monetary base.

**Hong Kong's Linked Exchange Rate**: a currency board system since 1983. The HKD is pegged to the USD at HK$7.80 = USD 1. The HKMA operates through Convertibility Undertakings (weak-side CU at HK$7.85, strong-side CU at HK$7.75). All HKD banknotes are fully backed by USD reserves held by the issuing banks.

#### Effects of exchange rate changes

**Currency depreciation** (HKD weakens):

- Exports become cheaper for foreign buyers → X rises.
- Imports become more expensive for domestic buyers → M falls.
- Net exports (X - M) rise → AD shifts right → output rises (in the short run, under certain conditions).
- Foreign-denominated debt becomes more expensive to service.

**Currency appreciation** (HKD strengthens): opposite effects.

#### Exam technique

- **For comparative advantage questions**: compute opportunity costs; identify the country with the lower opportunity cost for each good; explain the gains from trade.
- **For tariff questions**: identify who bears the burden; compute the deadweight loss.
- **For balance of payments questions**: identify the components; explain the accounting identity.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Tariffs protect domestic jobs." Tariffs may protect some jobs but raise prices for consumers and reduce overall welfare.
- "A current account deficit is always bad." A current account deficit is not necessarily bad if it reflects productive foreign investment.
- "The Linked Exchange Rate means Hong Kong has no monetary policy." Hong Kong has monetary policy (e.g. exchange fund bills), but interest rates follow the US because of the peg.
