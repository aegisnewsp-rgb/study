# Section V: Electricity and Magnetism — O-Level Pure Physics (6091) Notes

Section V (Electricity and Magnetism) is the second-largest single section in the O-Level Pure Physics 6091 syllabus by marks, after Section II (Newtonian Mechanics). It covers seven sub-topics: Static Electricity, Current Electricity, D.C. Circuits, Practical Electricity, Magnetism, Electromagnetism, and Electromagnetic Induction. The section spans roughly half of Paper 2's structured questions, with circuit-analysis questions and electromagnetic-induction questions being the heaviest individual items.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### The seven facts every Sec 4 student needs

1. **Charge** is a property of matter; like charges repel, unlike charges attract.
2. **Current** (I) is the rate of flow of charge. Unit: ampere (A). I = Q / t.
3. **Voltage** (V) is the energy transferred per unit charge. Unit: volt (V). V = E / Q.
4. **Resistance** (R) is the opposition to the flow of current. Unit: ohm (Ω). V = IR.
5. **Series circuit**: the same current flows through every component; total resistance = sum of individual resistances; total voltage = sum of individual voltages.
6. **Parallel circuit**: each component has its own path; total current = sum of branch currents; total voltage = voltage across each branch.
7. **Electromagnetic induction**: a changing magnetic field through a coil induces an EMF across the ends of the coil. The direction of the induced EMF opposes the change (Lenz's law).

#### Key equations

- V = IR (Ohm's law)
- P = VI (power in an electrical component)
- E = Pt (energy transferred)
- Q = It (charge transferred)
- R_total (series) = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + …
- 1 / R_total (parallel) = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + …
- EMF = −N ΔΦ / Δt (electromagnetic induction; the minus sign is Lenz's law)

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Static electricity

A material becomes positively charged when it loses electrons and negatively charged when it gains electrons. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. A charged object can attract small neutral objects (like pieces of paper) by inducing a charge on the near side of the neutral object.

**Charging by friction**: rubbing two insulating materials together transfers electrons from one to the other. A polythene rod rubbed with a duster becomes negatively charged; a cellulose acetate rod rubbed with a duster becomes positively charged.

**Electric field**: the region around a charged object where another charged object experiences a force. Field lines run from positive to negative; the closer the lines, the stronger the field.

#### Current electricity

**Current** (I) is the rate of flow of charge, measured in amperes (A). I = Q / t, where Q is the charge in coulombs and t is the time in seconds. Conventional current flows from positive to negative (the direction in which positive charge would move); electron flow is in the opposite direction.

**Voltage** (V) is the energy transferred per unit charge, measured in volts (V). V = E / Q, where E is the energy in joules and Q is the charge in coulombs. A 1.5 V battery supplies 1.5 J of energy per coulomb of charge that flows through it.

**Resistance** (R) is the opposition to the flow of current, measured in ohms (Ω). For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, V = IR (Ohm's law). The resistance of a metal wire increases with length and decreases with cross-sectional area: R = ρL / A, where ρ is the resistivity of the material.

The I-V characteristic of an ohmic conductor (e.g. a metal wire at constant temperature) is a straight line through the origin. The I-V characteristic of a filament lamp is a curve that flattens at high voltages (because the filament heats up and its resistance increases). The I-V characteristic of a diode is asymmetric: very low resistance in the forward direction, very high resistance in the reverse direction.

#### D.C. circuits

A **series circuit** has all components connected end-to-end in a single loop. The current is the same at every point; the sum of the voltages across the components equals the battery voltage; the total resistance is the sum of the individual resistances.

A **parallel circuit** has each component connected across the same two points of the circuit. The voltage across each branch is the same; the sum of the branch currents equals the total current from the battery; the reciprocal of the total resistance is the sum of the reciprocals of the individual resistances.

For two resistors in parallel: R_total = R₁R₂ / (R₁ + R₂).

#### Practical electricity

**Power** in an electrical component: P = VI = I²R = V² / R.

**Energy** transferred by an electrical component: E = Pt = VIt. Units: joules (J); in practical contexts, often kilowatt-hours (kWh).

**Household electricity** is supplied as alternating current (a.c.) at 230 V and 50 Hz in Singapore (and most of Asia). The three wires in a three-pin plug are the live wire (brown in newer wiring, red in older), the neutral wire (blue, black) and the earth wire (green-yellow). The fuse (in the live wire) breaks the circuit if the current exceeds a safe limit. The earth wire provides a low-resistance path for fault currents.

**Electrical safety**: insulation around wires prevents electric shock; fuses and circuit breakers cut the current in the event of a fault; the earth wire directs fault currents away from the user.

#### Magnetism

A magnet has two poles: north and south. Like poles repel; unlike poles attract. A magnetic field is the region around a magnet where a magnetic material experiences a force.

**Magnetic field lines** run from the north pole to the south pole outside the magnet; they form closed loops through and around the magnet. The closer the field lines, the stronger the field.

A current-carrying wire produces a magnetic field around it (the right-hand grip rule). A solenoid (coil of wire) produces a magnetic field similar to that of a bar magnet; placing an iron core inside the solenoid makes an electromagnet.

The Earth's magnetic field is roughly like that of a bar magnet, with a magnetic south pole near the geographic north pole and a magnetic north pole near the geographic south pole. A compass needle aligns with the Earth's magnetic field.

#### Electromagnetism

A current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force (the motor effect). The direction of the force is given by Fleming's left-hand rule: first finger = field (N to S), second finger = current (conventional, + to −), thumb = motion (force).

The size of the force is F = BIL, where B is the magnetic flux density (T), I is the current (A), and L is the length of the conductor in the field (m). F is in newtons.

This effect is used in electric motors: a coil carrying current in a magnetic field experiences a force on each side of the coil, producing a torque that rotates the coil.

#### Electromagnetic induction

A changing magnetic field through a coil induces an EMF across the ends of the coil (Faraday's law). The induced EMF is proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux through the coil: EMF = −N ΔΦ / Δt (the negative sign is Lenz's law: the direction of the induced EMF opposes the change that produced it).

The induced EMF can be increased by:

- Using a stronger magnet.
- Moving the magnet (or the coil) faster.
- Using more turns of wire in the coil.
- Using a coil with a larger cross-sectional area.

A transformer uses electromagnetic induction to step a.c. voltages up or down. A step-up transformer has more turns on the secondary coil than on the primary; a step-down transformer has fewer. For an ideal transformer: V_p / V_s = N_p / N_s (and power in = power out for an ideal transformer, so I_p V_p = I_s V_s).

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Where Electricity and Magnetism appears across the papers

- **O-Level Pure Physics 6091 Paper 1**: short structured items on current, voltage, resistance, and basic magnetism.
- **O-Level Pure Physics 6091 Paper 2**: long structured items on circuit analysis (series / parallel combinations), electromagnetic induction, and the transformer. Often the second-highest-mark structured item on the paper.
- **Across both papers**: drawing a clear labelled circuit diagram with all components, currents and voltages marked is the highest-leverage exam technique.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Current is consumed by a component." Current is not consumed; the same current flows in and out of a component (in a series circuit). What is consumed is energy, transferred from the source to the component.
- "Voltage is the same in every part of a circuit." Voltage is the same in a parallel branch but is split between components in a series circuit.
- "Higher resistance means more current." For a fixed voltage, higher resistance means LESS current. For a fixed current, higher resistance means a larger voltage drop across the component.
- "Conventional current is the direction electrons flow." Conventional current is opposite to electron flow; conventional current goes from + to −, electron flow goes from − to +.
- "Lenz's law is a separate effect from Faraday's law." Lenz's law is the direction part of Faraday's law; the minus sign in the EMF equation encodes it.

#### Exam technique

- For circuit-analysis questions, redraw the circuit with the values of every component labelled. The labelled diagram is the method mark.
- For power questions, state the formula before substituting. P = VI, P = I²R, P = V²/R — choose the one that uses the values you know.
- For electromagnetic-induction questions, identify what is changing (the magnetic field, the area of the coil, the angle between them) and write the equation with that change. The change is the key.
- For transformer questions, set up the ratio V_p / V_s = N_p / N_s before substituting. The ratio is the method mark.
