# Basic Chemistry

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review

Science is **split** in 2026: Paper II runs **08:30–11:40** and Paper I runs **13:00–14:00**. The long paper comes first in the day and rewards coverage; the one-hour paper rewards precision on a narrow set of facts.

The classification test that sorts most questions: **a change of state is physical, a new substance is chemical.**

| Concept | The one thing to hold |
|---|---|
| Element | One type of atom |
| Compound | Two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio, properties entirely different from its parts |
| Mixture | Properties of the parts survive; separated physically |

**Balance equations by coefficients only, never subscripts.**

- higher metal displaces a metal below it from its salt solution

- a metal above carbon needs electrolysis to extract from its oxide

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study

The Department of Examinations schedules Science in a split form in the 2026 timetable: Paper II from 08:30 to 11:40 and Paper I from 13:00 to 14:00. The long paper comes first in the day and the short paper in the afternoon, which is worth knowing in advance because the two papers behave differently — the 3 hour 10 minute paper rewards coverage, the one-hour paper rewards precision on a narrow set of facts.

Basic chemistry is the highest-density block in that syllabus. Almost every other topic in O/L science depends on being able to read a formula, write a balanced equation and reason about a particle.

#### Matter, and the particle model

Solids, liquids and gases are distinguished by particle arrangement and by the strength of the forces between particles, not by particle size. Melting breaks some forces, boiling breaks all of them, and dissolving separates particles without breaking them at all — which is why a solution is not a new substance.

A change of state is physical; a new substance is chemical. That test alone sorts most of the classification questions.

#### Elements, compounds and mixtures

An element contains one type of atom. A compound contains atoms of two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio, and it has entirely different properties from its elements — that is the classic demonstration of which students misremember. A mixture keeps the properties of its parts and can be separated physically.

Writing formulas: the number in a formula is a ratio, not an amount of substance. $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ are different compounds with different properties, and the difference is one oxygen atom per molecule.

#### Equations and the reactivity series

Balance by changing coefficients only, never subscripts — putting a coefficient inside a polyatomic ion that cancels is the shortcut that saves time until it does not.

The reactivity series orders metals by how readily they lose electrons. Higher means more reactive, and the consequences run consistently: a metal higher in the series displaces a metal below it from its salt solution; a metal above hydrogen reacts with dilute acid to give hydrogen; a metal above carbon needs electrolysis to extract from its oxide.

The opposite error is the commonest: saying a metal reacts with water when it needs steam, or saying iron reacts with dilute sulfuric acid when it displaces copper. Read the specific conditions.

#### Acids, alkalis and salts

Neutralisation is acid plus base to give salt and water, and it is double displacement in every case: swap the partners. The pH scale runs 0 to 14 with 7 neutral at ordinary temperature, and the strength of an acid or alkali is not the same as its pH — a dilute strong acid can have a higher pH than a concentrated weak one.

Titration gives you the concentration of one solution from another. Read the question carefully about which is in the flask and which is in the burette, because the roles reverse the arithmetic direction, and average the titres from the concordant runs rather than using the first.

#### Common mistakes

Confusing a mixture with a compound. If the properties of the parts survive, it is a mixture.

Treating the formula number as a mass. $\text{CuSO}_4\cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$ is one formula unit, not five.

Using the reactivity series without the conditions — steam versus water, and the position of hydrogen in the series, are what decide whether a reaction happens at all.

Forgetting the sign and unit on a titration answer.

Calling neutralisation a combination reaction. It is double displacement; combination is the one where one product forms.

Skipping the indicator or using the wrong one at the endpoint in a titration question.

### 🔴 Extended — Mastery

#### Why the split papers behave differently

Paper II is 3 hours 10 minutes and Paper I is 1 hour. That is not a cosmetic difference in the timetable — it changes what each paper can test. The long paper has room for descriptive and multi-step questions that need working shown. The one-hour paper cannot, so it compresses into short factual recall and one or two calculations. Revision that ignores this splits your effort wrongly.

#### The three states, read as forces rather than sizes

The distinction is particle arrangement and the strength of forces between particles, **not particle size** — which is the mistake candidates make when asked why a solid holds its shape.

- **Melting** breaks *some* forces.
- **Boiling** breaks *all* of them.
- **Dissolving** separates particles without breaking them at all.

That last one is why a solution is not a new substance. No new substance forms, so it is physical.

#### The classic demonstration, and why students misremember it

A compound has **entirely different properties from its elements**. The commonest failure is to answer a mixture question here, because the familiar demonstration (iron and sulphur) is remembered as "the parts are still there, so it is a mixture". The property change is the point: the compound is new.

#### Formula numbers are ratios — the test question

$\text{CuSO}_4 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$ is **one formula unit**, not five. Asking students to treat the 5 as an amount of substance is the standard trap. Similarly, the difference between $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ is one oxygen atom per molecule, and they have different properties because the ratio changed.

#### The coefficient shortcut, and where it stops working

Putting a coefficient outside a polyatomic ion that cancels is the shortcut that saves time in equations. It stops working the moment the coefficient needs to go *inside* the ion, and candidates who use it reflexively produce unbalanced equations exactly there. Balance by changing coefficients only — never subscripts, because a subscript changes the substance.

#### Reading the reactivity series with conditions

The opposite error is the commonest: saying a metal reacts with water when it needs **steam**, or saying iron reacts with dilute sulfuric acid when it displaces copper. The series alone does not decide the question; the **specific conditions** do.

#### Neutralisation is not a combination reaction

Neutralisation is acid plus base giving salt and water, and it is **double displacement** in every case — you swap the partners. Calling it combination is a named fault, because combination is the classification where **one** product forms.

| Reaction type | Products | Neutralisation? |
|---|---|---|
| Combination | One | No |
| Neutralisation | Two (salt + water) | Yes — and it is double displacement |

#### Titration: the two errors that cost the unit mark

First, the **roles reverse the arithmetic direction** — read carefully which solution is in the flask and which is in the burette before computing. Second, average the titres from the **concordant** runs rather than using the first, which is the outlier by construction. And at the endpoint, do not skip the indicator or use the wrong one: both are listed faults.

#### Safe answer versus strong answer

A safe candidate states that a mixture keeps the properties of its parts. A strong one gives the test as a procedure — *check whether the properties of the parts survive; if they do, it is a mixture* — because that phrasing answers any mixture-or-compound question without needing to name the substances.
