# Current Electricity

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Current electricity studies the steady flow of **electric charge** through conductors, quantified by **current** $I = q/t$ in ampere (A), where $q$ is charge in coulomb (C) and $t$ is time in second (s). A cell's **electromotive force** $\varepsilon$ drives this flow, while the conductor's **resistance** $R$ (in ohm, $\Omega$) opposes it according to **Ohm's law**: $V = IR$, with $V$ in volts. Energy dissipated per second is **electrical power** $P = VI = I^2R = V^2/R$, released as heat via Joule's law $H = I^2Rt$.

| Quantity | Symbol | SI Unit | Formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current | $I$ | ampere (A) | $I = q/t$ |
| Potential difference | $V$ | volt (V) | $V = IR$ |
| Resistance | $R$ | ohm ($\Omega$) | $R = \rho L / A$ |
| Conductivity | $\sigma$ | S·m⁻¹ | $\sigma = 1/\rho$ |
| Power | $P$ | watt (W) | $P = I^2R$ |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **VIR-PAC** — *Volts, In amperes, Resistance in ohms, then Power = VI, And Current = V/R*. For series resistors just **add**, for parallel use **reciprocal add** $1/R_{eq}=\sum 1/R_i$.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Microscopic Picture of Current and Ohm's Law
Inside a metal, free electrons drift opposite to the conventional current with **drift velocity** $v_d = I/(nAe)$, where $n$ is the carrier number density (m⁻³), $A$ is the cross-sectional area (m²), and $e = 1.6\times 10^{-19}$ C is the elementary charge. Their **mobility** is $\mu = v_d/E$ in m²·V⁻¹·s⁻¹, and conductivity obeys $\sigma = ne\mu$. For an ohmic (linear) conductor, $V \propto I$ at constant temperature, with $R = V/I$ constant.

#### Cells, Internal Resistance, and Terminal Voltage
A real cell of emf $\varepsilon$ has internal resistance $r$, so when delivering current $I$ the **terminal voltage** is $V = \varepsilon - Ir$. The emf $\varepsilon$ represents energy supplied per unit charge, while $V$ is the energy actually delivered to the external circuit.

#### Kirchhoff's Rules for Multi-Loop Networks
- **KCL (junction rule):** $\sum I_{\text{in}} = \sum I_{\text{out}}$ at every node — charge is conserved.
- **KVL (loop rule):** $\sum (\text{emf}) = \sum (IR)$ around any closed loop — energy is conserved.

Apply a consistent sign convention while traversing loops to avoid algebraic mistakes.

#### Wheatstone Bridge and Meter Bridge
A bridge with arms $P, Q, R, S$ is **balanced** when the galvanometer shows zero current. The classic balance condition is:

$$\frac{P}{Q} = \frac{R}{S}$$

A meter bridge uses this with a uniform wire, where $R/S = l_1/l_2$ (lengths of the two wire segments).

#### Potentiometer Principle
A potentiometer compares emfs without drawing current from the source. If balancing length for cell 1 is $l_1$ and cell 2 is $l_2$:

$$\frac{\varepsilon_1}{\varepsilon_2} = \frac{l_1}{l_2}$$

The unknown emf is measured at the **null deflection** point, so no internal-resistance error creeps in.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Quantity / Tool | Definition | Formula | Used For |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\varepsilon$ (emf) | Energy per unit charge supplied by cell | measured when $I = 0$ | Source rating |
| $V$ (terminal p.d.) | Energy per unit charge delivered to load | $V = \varepsilon - Ir$ | Real working voltage |
| Resistance $R$ | Opposition to current at fixed $T$ | $R = \rho L / A$ | Resistor design |
| Resistivity $\rho$ | Material property | intrinsic, units $\Omega \cdot$m | Comparing materials |
| Wheatstone bridge | Null-deflection resistor comparison | $P/Q = R/S$ | Unknown resistance |
| Potentiometer | Null-deflection voltage comparison | $\varepsilon_1/\varepsilon_2 = l_1/l_2$ | Comparing emfs |

#### Standard Problem-Solving Checklist
1. Sketch the circuit and label every branch with a current direction.
2. Reduce series/parallel resistor groups to a single $R_{eq}$.
3. Apply KVL for each independent loop; KCL at each junction.
4. Verify balance condition for bridges before solving emf equations.
5. Convert all quantities to SI ($\Omega\cdot$m for $\rho$, m for $L$, m² for $A$).

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** A $12\,\text{V}$ battery with internal resistance $r = 0.5\,\Omega$ drives a parallel combination of a $6\,\Omega$ resistor and a $12\,\Omega$ resistor. Find (a) the current supplied by the battery, and (b) the power dissipated in the $6\,\Omega$ branch.

#### Solution:

Step 1 — Equivalent resistance of parallel branches:
$$\frac{1}{R_p} = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{12} = \frac{2 + 1}{12} = \frac{3}{12} \implies R_p = 4\,\Omega.$$

Step 2 — Total resistance seen by the battery:
$$R_{eq} = r + R_p = 0.5 + 4 = 4.5\,\Omega.$$

Step 3 — Battery current from Ohm's law:
$$I = \frac{\varepsilon}{R_{eq}} = \frac{12}{4.5} = \frac{40}{15} = 2.67\,\text{A}.$$

Step 4 — Terminal voltage across the parallel combination:
$$V = \varepsilon - Ir = 12 - (2.67)(0.5) = 12 - 1.33 = 10.67\,\text{V}.$$

Step 5 — Current through the $6\,\Omega$ branch:
$$I_6 = \frac{V}{R_6} = \frac{10.67}{6} = 1.78\,\text{A}.$$

Step 6 — Power in the $6\,\Omega$ branch:
$$P_6 = I_6^{\,2} R_6 = (1.78)^2 \times 6 = 3.16 \times 6 \approx 18.98\,\text{W}.$$

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students frequently forget to include the cell's internal resistance $r$ in the total circuit, doubling the load current. Others compute current in the $6\,\Omega$ branch as $12/6 = 2\,\text{A}$ (using emf instead of terminal voltage $V$), then notice the answer doesn't sum correctly with the $12\,\Omega$ branch. Always reduce to $R_{eq}$ first, then split the **terminal voltage** — never the emf — across parallel branches.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Temperature Dependence and Joule Heating Coupling
Resistance varies with temperature as $R_T = R_0[1 + \alpha(T - T_0)]$, where $\alpha$ is the temperature coefficient in K⁻¹. For metallic conductors $\alpha > 0$; for semiconductors and insulators $\alpha < 0$. When current passes through such a resistor, Joule heating $H = I^2Rt$ raises $T$, which raises $R$, which lowers $I$ — a coupled nonlinear feedback that explains why a filament bulb's cold resistance is roughly **one-tenth** of its hot operating resistance.

#### Advanced Edge Cases and Traps

| Edge Case / Trap | Statement | Correct Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Drift vs. thermal speed | $v_d \approx 10^{-4}$ m·s⁻¹; thermal speed $\sim 10^5$ m·s⁻¹ | Never equate them — net drift is tiny |
| Series-parallel miscounting | Two $R$ in series give $2R$; two in parallel give $R/2$ | Remember reciprocal addition for parallel |
| Sign convention in KVL | emf traversed − to + is +ε; resistor drop follows $I$ direction | Track both consistently per loop |
| Maximum power transfer | Load receives maximum power when $R_{\text{load}} = r$ | $P_{\text{max}} = \varepsilon^2/(4r)$ |
| Internal resistance of parallel cells | $r_{eq} = r/n$ for $n$ identical cells in parallel | Emf stays at $\varepsilon$ of one cell |
| Potentiometer with finite cell current | Balance length changes if driver cell has internal $r$ | Use a buffer or zero-current galvanometer |

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics
- **Electrostatics:** $\varepsilon$ derived from work done per unit charge; $V$ links to work-energy theorem.
- **Magnetism:** Current in a magnetic field experiences $F = BIl\sin\theta$ (a direct bridge to *Moving Charges and Magnetism*).
- **Capacitance:** RC circuits combine Current Electricity with charging/discharging transients — a favourite two-topic hybrid in BUET problems.
- **Modern Physics:** Conductivity band theory replaces classical $\sigma = ne\mu$ for semiconductors.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts
1. A battery of emf $\varepsilon$ and internal resistance $r$ is connected to an external load $R$. Prove $P_{\text{load}}$ is maximum when $R = r$, and compute that maximum in terms of $\varepsilon$ and $r$.
2. In a potentiometer experiment, the driver cell's emf is $5\,\text{V}$ and a standard cell shows balance at $60\,\text{cm}$. An unknown cell gives balance at $45\,\text{cm}$. Find the unknown emf. Then discuss qualitatively how the answer would change if the driver cell had a noticeable internal resistance.
