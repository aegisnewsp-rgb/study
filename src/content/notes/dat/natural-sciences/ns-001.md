# General Chemistry Foundations

General chemistry is 30 of the 100 items in the Survey of the Natural Sciences. The ADA's DAT Candidate Guide splits those 100 items into Biology (40 items), General Chemistry (30 items) and Organic Chemistry (30 items), and the section runs for 90 minutes inside a five-hour fifteen-minute administration of 280 items in total. The ADA publishes no per-topic weightage, so how many of those 30 items are stoichiometry rather than electrochemistry is unknown. Treat the seven pillars below as a coverage checklist, not a forecast of the paper.

What makes this topic tractable is that general chemistry items are mechanical once you know what kind of item you are looking at. Almost every one of them is a move, and the move tells you what to build before you touch a calculator. The chemistry itself is first-year chemistry, not third-year, and first-year chemistry is learnable in weeks — but only if you practise the moves rather than re-reading chapters and hoping the formulas stay.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

**Every general chemistry item is one of four moves.** Identify the move before you read the options, because the same fact is the answer in one move and a distractor in another.

| The move | The question it produces | What you build before the numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Picture | What is this species shaped like, and is it polar? | Lewis structure → VSEPR shape → dipole sum |
| Inventory | How many grams, moles or litres does this make? | balanced equation → mole ratio → conversion |
| Compare | Which is more stable, more acidic, more reactive? | the trend, or the reference table |
| Know the number | What is the pH, the K, the cell potential? | the equation, then one substitution |

**Memory hook — P-I-C-K.** **P**icture the structure, **I**nventory the moles, **C**ompare against a trend, **K**now the number you are asked for. "PICK the move, then do only that move." Most lost marks come from doing the right calculation for the wrong move — grinding through mole ratios when the item wanted a geometry sketch.

**The seven pillars, and the one fact that carries most of the items.**

| Pillar | The fact that earns the marks |
|---|---|
| Atomic structure and periodicity | Electrons fill the lowest-energy orbitals first, and every trend follows from effective nuclear charge |
| Bonding and molecular geometry | Electron domains repel each other; four domains give a tetrahedron |
| Stoichiometry and the mole | The mole is the only unit that bridges grams, litres and particles |
| Solutions and concentration | Molarity changes when the solvent evaporates; molality does not |
| Thermodynamics and equilibrium | ΔG = ΔH − TΔS decides spontaneity; Q against K decides direction |
| Acid–base chemistry | pH is a logarithm, and a buffer resists change near its pKa |
| Electrochemistry | Electrons flow from anode to cathode; AN OX, RED CAT |

**Ten facts worth a mark each.**

- One mole is 6.022 × 10²³ particles, and an element's molar mass in grams per mole equals its atomic mass.
- Avogadro's law: equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules.
- Strong acids: HCl, HNO₃ and the first proton of H₂SO₄. Strong bases: LiOH, NaOH, KOH, Ba(OH)₂ and Ca(OH)₂.
- pH + pOH = 14 at 25 °C, and one unit of pH is a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration.
- Bond angles: linear 180°, trigonal planar 120°, tetrahedral 109.5°, trigonal bipyramidal 90° and 120°, octahedral 90°.
- Hybridisation matches geometry: sp linear, sp² trigonal planar, sp³ tetrahedral.
- Formal charge = valence electrons − nonbonding electrons − half the bonding electrons.
- ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, and at equilibrium ΔG = 0.
- Oxidation is loss of electrons; reduction is gain; the anode is where oxidation happens.
- pKa = −log Ka, and a buffer's pH = pKa + log([base]/[acid]).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Atomic structure and periodicity

Electron configurations fill by the aufbau principle, with one electron per orbital before pairing (Hund's rule) and no two electrons in the same orbital (Pauli exclusion). Periodicity then follows from effective nuclear charge: across a period, shielding barely changes while nuclear charge rises, so the pull on outer electrons grows. Down a group, shielding grows faster than nuclear charge, so the pull weakens.

| Trend across a period | Why |
|---|---|
| Atomic radius decreases | more protons pulling the same shell inward |
| First ionisation energy increases | harder to remove an electron held more tightly |
| Electronegativity increases | a stronger pull on bonding electrons |

The exceptions are worth knowing because items use them. Beryllium has a higher first ionisation energy than boron because boron begins a new subshell with a higher-energy electron, and nitrogen has a higher first ionisation energy than oxygen because oxygen's electrons must pair in one p orbital and repel.

For isoelectronic species — ions with the same number of electrons, such as O²⁻, F⁻ and Na⁺ — nuclear charge decides everything, and the ranking runs from fewest protons to most.

#### Bonding and molecular geometry

Ionic bonding is electron transfer with electrostatic attraction in every direction; covalent bonding is sharing, with the shared pair pulled toward the more electronegative atom. A polar covalent bond sits between the two, and the polarity of the whole molecule depends on whether the bond dipoles cancel.

Lewis structures are the entry ticket. Count valence electrons, connect the atoms with single bonds, complete the octets, then check the formal charge on each atom. The best structure carries the fewest formal charges, and any negative charge sits on the more electronegative atom.

**VSEPR shapes, angles and polarity.**

| Electron domains | Shape | Bond angle | Polar? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | linear | 180° | Only if the two ends differ — CO₂ no, HCN yes |
| 3 | trigonal planar, or bent with one lone pair | 120° | Only if the three positions do not all carry identical groups — BF₃ no, SO₂ yes |
| 4 | tetrahedral | 109.5° | Only if not all four substituents are the same (CCl₄ no, CH₃Cl yes) |
| 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | 90° and 120° | Depends on which positions the lone pairs take |
| 6 | octahedral | 90° | Depends on the lone pairs |

Intermolecular forces decide the bulk properties: dispersion forces act between all molecules and grow with size, dipole–dipole forces act between polar molecules, and hydrogen bonding requires hydrogen bonded to nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine. A substance with strong hydrogen bonding boils at a higher temperature than an otherwise similar one, and "like dissolves like" is the rule that follows.

#### Stoichiometry and the mole

The mole is the bridge. Convert from grams to moles with the molar mass, ride the coefficients of the balanced equation, then convert into whatever the item wants — grams, litres, particles.

Limiting reactant is the single most common item in this pillar, and it always goes the same way: convert both reactants to moles, compare the mole ratio each would need, take the smaller, and then convert that product to the unit asked for. Percent yield is actual divided by theoretical, times 100.

For gases, the ideal gas law PV = nRT with R = 0.0821 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹ does most of the work, and at 0 °C and 1 atm one mole of any ideal gas occupies 22.4 L. Combined gas law problems track P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ with temperature in kelvin.

#### Solutions

| Quantity | Symbol and definition | What changes with temperature |
|---|---|---|
| Molarity | M = moles of solute per litre of solution | changes if the solvent evaporates |
| Molality | m = moles of solute per kilogram of solvent | does not change with temperature |
| Mass percent | (mass of solute ÷ mass of solution) × 100 | does not change with temperature |
| Mole fraction | moles of component ÷ total moles | does not change with temperature |

Dilution uses M₁V₁ = M₂V₂, and it is the one calculation in this pillar where students lose marks by adding instead of multiplying.

The solubility rules sort ionic compounds: salts of group 1 metals and ammonium are always soluble, as are nitrates, acetates, chlorides, bromides, iodides and most sulfates; carbonates, phosphates, sulfides and hydroxides are insoluble except for group 1, calcium, strontium and barium. Colloids scatter light (the Tyndall effect), solutions do not, and suspensions settle.

#### Thermodynamics and equilibrium

Endothermic reactions absorb heat, exothermic reactions release it, and ΔH measures the difference. A bond-energy estimate runs ΔH ≈ Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed), which works because breaking a bond always costs energy and forming one always returns some.

Spontaneity comes from ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. If ΔH is negative and ΔS positive, the reaction runs forward at every temperature. If ΔH is positive and ΔS negative, it never runs forward on its own. In the two mixed cases the temperature decides, and an item that gives you a temperature is telling you the signs matter.

For equilibrium, the equilibrium constant K is the ratio of products to reactants at equilibrium. Compare the reaction quotient Q with K: Q below K means the reaction still has forward work to do, Q above K means it runs back. Le Chatelier's principle handles the disturbances — add a reactant, raise the pressure, cool an exothermic reaction, and the equilibrium shifts to oppose the change. A catalyst changes the rate of both directions and shifts nothing.

#### Acid–base chemistry

Strong acids ionise completely; weak acids only partly. That single difference produces the pH of a weak acid, because for a weak acid Ka = x²/(C − x) with x = [H⁺], and when x is small relative to C this simplifies to x ≈ √(Ka·C).

Buffers resist pH change by consuming what you add. A buffer of a weak acid and its conjugate base satisfies pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]), and the half-equivalence point is where pH = pKa exactly.

| Titration | Equivalence-point pH | Shape of the curve |
|---|---|---|
| Strong acid with strong base | 7 | Sharp, symmetric |
| Weak acid with strong base | Above 7 | Sharp, with a flatter start |
| Strong acid with weak base | Below 7 | Sharp, with a flatter finish |

#### Electrochemistry

Oxidation is loss of electrons and an increase in oxidation number; reduction is gain and a decrease. The anode is where oxidation happens, the cathode is where reduction happens, and electrons travel from anode to cathode through the external wire. In a galvanic cell the anode is negative and the reaction is spontaneous; in an electrolytic cell the anode is positive and the reaction is driven by an external source.

The standard reduction potential table ranks reactions. E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode, and a positive E°cell predicts a spontaneous galvanic cell. The table also predicts displacement: any metal above another in the series will displace it from solution.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Worked item 1 — limiting reagent, done in the right order

**Question.** A student mixes 10.0 g of magnesium with 25.0 g of hydrochloric acid. What mass of hydrogen can be produced, and which reactant is left over?

**Step 1 — balance.** Mg + 2 HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂.

**Step 2 — convert both reactants to moles.**

- n(Mg) = 10.0 g ÷ 24.3 g/mol = 0.411 mol
- n(HCl) = 25.0 g ÷ 36.5 g/mol = 0.685 mol

**Step 3 — ask which one runs out first.** Reacting all 0.411 mol of Mg would need 0.822 mol of HCl, and only 0.685 mol is available. So HCl is limiting and magnesium is in excess. The two-step check matters: magnesium has fewer moles, but it needs two HCl each.

**Step 4 — convert the limiting reactant.** n(H₂) = 0.685 mol HCl × (1 mol H₂ ÷ 2 mol HCl) = 0.3425 mol. Mass = 0.3425 mol × 2.016 g/mol = 0.69 g of hydrogen. At 0 °C and 1 atm that is 0.3425 mol × 22.4 L/mol = 7.7 L.

**Step 5 — check what is left.** Magnesium consumed = 0.3425 mol, so 0.411 − 0.3425 = 0.069 mol of magnesium remains.

> ⚡ **Examiner Trap.** Choosing the reactant with the smaller mass, or the one with fewer moles, as the limiting reactant. The comparison is between moles *after* the coefficients are applied. The second trap is converting grams of hydrogen straight from grams of HCl and skipping the 2:1 ratio, which is where most of the lost marks go. Third trap: reporting a percent yield when the item asked for the mass actually produced — mass of product is the theoretical yield here.

#### Worked item 2 — weak acid, then buffer

**Question.** What is the pH of 0.10 M acetic acid, and what is the pH of a buffer made from 0.10 M acetic acid and 0.20 M sodium acetate? Take Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵.

**Step 1 — the weak acid.** For a weak acid, [H⁺] ≠ molarity. Set up Ka = x²/(C − x) with x = [H⁺]. Since x is small compared with C, take x ≈ √(Ka·C) = √(1.8 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.10) = √(1.8 × 10⁻⁶) = 1.34 × 10⁻³ M. The pH is therefore 2.87. Check the approximation: 1.34 × 10⁻³ is 1.3 per cent of 0.10 M, comfortably under the 5 per cent threshold, so the simplification is safe.

**Step 2 — the buffer.** pKa = −log(1.8 × 10⁻⁵) = 4.74. Then pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]) = 4.74 + log(0.20/0.10) = 4.74 + 0.30 = 5.04.

**Step 3 — why the buffer sits higher.** The common ion from the salt suppresses ionisation of the acetic acid, so fewer hydrogen ions are needed to reach the same equilibrium. Adding a strong acid to this buffer barely moves the pH, because the acetate consumes the added hydrogen ions and is converted back into acetic acid.

> ⚡ **Examiner Trap.** Using the strong-acid shortcut on a weak acid and getting pH 1.00 for 0.10 M acid. Weak acids only partly ionise, and that is the entire point of the item. Second trap: inverting the ratio in the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation — it is base over acid, and getting it backwards moves the pH the wrong way by twice the log of the ratio. Third trap: forgetting that a buffer's capacity depends on how much of each component you have, not only on the ratio.

#### Worked item 3 — polarity from shape

**Question.** Which of CO₂, H₂O, BF₃, CCl₄ and NH₃ is polar?

| Molecule | Shape | Do the bond dipoles cancel? | Polar? |
|---|---|---|---|
| CO₂ | linear | Yes, two equal dipoles 180° apart | No |
| H₂O | bent | No | Yes |
| BF₃ | trigonal planar | Yes, three equal dipoles 120° apart | No |
| CCl₄ | tetrahedral | Yes, four equal dipoles | No |
| NH₃ | trigonal pyramidal | No | Yes |

**The reasoning to carry into the answer.** Draw the shape first, then add the vectors. Molecules whose dipoles cancel by symmetry are nonpolar however polar their individual bonds are — CCl₄ has strongly polar C–Cl bonds and no net dipole at all, because the tetrahedron is symmetric.

> ⚡ **Examiner Trap.** Deciding polarity from the bonds alone. Every one of these five molecules has polar bonds, and three of them are nonpolar. Second trap: assuming a molecule with only one polar bond is polar — in CO₂ the two bonds are polar and cancel, in H₂O they do not.

#### Worked item 4 — Le Chatelier and the catalyst

**Question.** For N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g), ΔH negative, what happens when the pressure is raised, when the temperature is raised, and when a catalyst is added?

- **Pressure raised:** the equilibrium shifts to the side with fewer moles of gas, so towards ammonia (four moles of gas on the left, two on the right). Note that K does not change.
- **Temperature raised:** the forward reaction is exothermic, so heat acts as a product and the equilibrium shifts backwards, towards N₂ and H₂. K falls.
- **Catalyst added:** nothing moves. The catalyst lowers the activation energy for both directions, so equilibrium is reached faster and its position is unchanged.

> ⚡ **Examiner Trap.** Believing a catalyst shifts the equilibrium. It does not, and an item offering "shifts towards product, faster" as one option is testing exactly that. Second trap: assuming K changes when a concentration changes. Only temperature changes K.

#### The comparison tables worth memorising

| Bonding | Electrons | Conductivity when molten | Solubility in water |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ionic | transferred | Conducts | Usually soluble |
| Covalent (molecular) | shared | Does not conduct | Depends on polarity — "like dissolves like" |
| Covalent (network) | shared | Does not conduct | Insoluble |
| Metallic | delocalised | Conducts | Insoluble |

| Acid or base | Ionises | Gives a salt with | Ka or Kb |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strong | Completely | A neutral salt | Very large |
| Weak | Partly | A salt that hydrolyses, so it is slightly acidic or basic | Small |

| Electrochemistry | Anode | Cathode | Electron flow | Spontaneous? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Galvanic cell | Oxidation, negative | Reduction, positive | Anode to cathode | Yes |
| Electrolytic cell | Oxidation, positive | Reduction, negative | Anode to cathode | No, it is driven |

#### ⚡ Quick Exam Checklist

- Move identified (picture, inventory, compare, know the number) before any calculation
- Equation balanced, with coefficients only, before any mole ratio is used
- Lewis structure drawn, then electron domains counted, then shape and dipole
- Moles computed with molar masses first, and the equation's coefficients applied in the right ratio afterwards
- Limiting reactant chosen from moles *after* the coefficients, never from grams
- Weak-acid pH computed from Ka, never from molarity
- Buffer pH computed with base over acid
- ΔG, not just ΔH, used to decide spontaneity
- Q compared with K before any Le Chatelier argument is written
- Anode and cathode named in the right order in both cell types
- K treated as unchanged by anything except temperature
