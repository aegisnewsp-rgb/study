# Important Treaties and Agreements

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **treaty** is a binding international agreement between states governed by international law, codified by the **Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT), 1969**. Three signatory concepts recur: **signature** (intent to ratify), **ratification** (domestic consent making it binding), and **accession** (joining after adoption). For LAT, the high-yield instruments are the **UN Charter (1945)**, **Geneva Conventions (1949)**, **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948)**, **Rome Statute (1998)** establishing the **International Criminal Court**, and the **NPT (1968)**. Remember that the **NPT** divides the world into five Nuclear-Weapon States (USA, Russia, UK, France, China) and non-nuclear states. The **VCLT** itself is not retroactive — it applies only to treaties concluded after its entry into force on 27 January 1980.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Treaty-Making Framework
The **Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (1969)** is the constitutional document of treaty law. Article 2(1)(a) defines a treaty as an international agreement concluded between states in written form and governed by international law, whether embodied in a single instrument or two or more. A treaty passes through three stages: **negotiation and adoption**, **signature** (which authenticates text and signals intent), and **ratification** (the domestic act of consent). Entry into force depends on the treaty’s own terms; for the VCLT itself, it required 35 ratifications, achieved on 27 January 1980. A **reservation** is a unilateral statement by which a state purports to modify or exclude the legal effect of certain provisions in its application to that state, allowed unless prohibited by the treaty or incompatible with its object and purpose (Article 19).

#### Peace, Security, and Humanitarian Law
- **Treaty of Westphalia (1648)**: ended the Thirty Years’ War and laid the foundation of modern state sovereignty and non-intervention.
- **UN Charter (1945)**: Article 2(4) prohibits the use of force; Article 51 preserves the **inherent right of individual or collective self-defence**; the Security Council authorizes collective action under Chapter VII.
- **Geneva Conventions (1949)** plus Additional Protocols I and II (1977) and Protocol III (2005, red crystal emblem) form the bedrock of **international humanitarian law (IHL)**, protecting wounded combatants, prisoners of war, and civilians.
- **Rome Statute (1998)**: established the **International Criminal Court (ICC)**, which began functioning on 1 July 2002. It prosecutes **genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and the crime of aggression** — the four core international crimes.

#### Human Rights and Disarmament
- **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948)**: a non-binding General Assembly resolution, but customary law; together with the **ICCPR** and **ICESCR** it forms the **International Bill of Human Rights**.
- **NPT (1968)**: three pillars — non-proliferation, peaceful uses of nuclear energy, and disarmament.
- **Partial Nuclear Test Ban Treaty (1963)**: bans tests in the atmosphere, outer space, and under water — underground tests remain permitted.
- **CTBT (1996)**: bans all nuclear explosions; pending entry into force pending Annex-2 state ratifications.

#### Exam Pattern
LAT typically frames 1–2 MCQs on this topic per paper. Watch for: the **year of adoption vs. entry into force**, the **distinction between UDHR (non-binding) and ICCPR (binding)**, and the **rotating UNSC presidency** trick question.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Adjacent Connections
The **VCLT (1969)** does not apply to agreements between states and international organizations (covered instead by the **1986 Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties between States and International Organizations**, still not in force). Treaties concluded before 1980 are governed by customary international law — a frequent trap. The **Geneva Conventions of 1949** replaced the earlier 1929 versions; the 1977 Additional Protocol I extended protection to wars of national liberation, controversial at adoption. The **Chemical Weapons Convention (1993)** and **Biological Weapons Convention (1972)** are disarmament treaties, not human rights treaties, and are administered by the **OPCW** in The Hague.

#### Bilateral Trade Mechanisms
A **Most Favoured Nation (MFN)** clause obliges a state to extend to its partner any better trade concession it later grants a third state — central to **WTO Article I**. A **Bilateral Investment Treaty (BIT)** protects foreign investors; India’s first model BIT was released in 2015 and updated in 2016 after the **White Industries v. India** arbitral award.

#### Common Mistakes
1. Confusing **signature** with ratification — signature alone does not bind the state.
2. Calling the **UDHR a treaty** — it is a GA resolution, non-binding as such.
3. Treating the **ICC** as a UN organ — it is an independent treaty-based court, not under the UN.
4. Mixing up the **CTBT (1996, not yet in force)** with the **NPT (1968, in force)**.

#### Practice Prompts
1. The Rome Statute defines four crimes over which the ICC has jurisdiction. List them and identify which one was added by the **Kampala Amendment (2010)**.
2. State the three pillars of the NPT and explain why India, Israel, and Pakistan remain outside it despite possessing nuclear weapons.
