# Inferring Thoughts, Feelings and Intentions

Section 2 of HPAT-Ireland is the least familiar section for most candidates, and it is the one where reading speed helps least. The items give you a short written scenario and ask you to work out what the people in it are thinking, feeling or about to do. Nothing is stated directly. Everything has to be read out of behaviour, context and consequence.

## Read for the people, not the problem

The instinct on encountering a scenario is to look for a puzzle in it. There usually is one — a missing report, a clash between two colleagues, a deadline. Section 2 does not ask you to solve it. It asks you to identify the emotion, the motive or the likely next action of a named person.

This reframing is most of the technique. An item reads like a problem and is scored like a character judgement.

Two habits make the shift:

- **Name the people and their relationships first.** Who wants what from whom? Who has the authority? Who is being let down?
- **Read the last line as the tell.** Scenarios in this section typically end on the detail that carries the emotional content — a pause, a choice of words, an action that contradicts what was said. The middle is context.

## What you are actually inferring

**Emotion.** From behaviour to feeling. The candidate answer is the *specific* emotion, not the family of it. If a person declines an invitation in a particular way, the target is the precise emotional state, not "unhappy". The options are deliberately graded from vague to specific, and the specific one is correct — a distractor will always sit a rung too high (a stronger emotion than the evidence supports) or too low (a milder one).

**Motivation or intention.** From what a person has done and said to what they are trying to achieve. The technique is completing the sentence *because*: the action, plus the reason it makes sense to do that. Options that explain an action with an unrelated motive fail immediately.

**Most likely next action.** Given the situation and the person's established character in the scenario, what do they do next. This is a prediction from a consistent model, not a guess.

**Consistency.** Given what the person has said about something, which other statement would they also agree with. The test is whether the second statement follows from the first under the same set of values.

## Where the distractors come from

Section 2 distractors are rarely random. They are drawn from four recurring sources, and recognising one tells you to look elsewhere.

1. **The emotion the situation usually produces, not the one this person produces.** Generic sympathy or frustration where the evidence points at something more specific or more restrained.
2. **An over-reading.** Treating a neutral act as highly significant — a pause read as guilt, a brevity read as hostility.
3. **An under-reading.** Taking the surface-level reading when the scenario has signalled otherwise.
4. **A plausible-but-unrelated motive.** A reason that would explain the action in the abstract but does not fit what this person has established.

**Consequence test.** For any candidate answer, ask: *if this were true, would the rest of the scenario still make sense?* A correct answer fits every detail. A wrong answer usually explains one moment and leaves the others stranded.

**Consistency of scale.** Emotions in a scenario have a consistent register. If a person has been mildly embarrassed throughout and the options include *furious*, the answer is not the strongest available — it is the one at the right temperature for this person.

## Working under time

- **Read the question first**, then the scenario. In a ranking item the question tells you the axis you are ranking along, which is often not the axis you would have chosen.
- **Two passes.** A first pass for the people and the relationships, a second for the specific question. Do not re-read the scenario three times hunting for a sentence that states the answer.
- **Mark, then move.** As with Section 1, leave a mark on the item and return if time allows.
- **Guess rather than leave blank.** There is no penalty for a wrong answer.

## The miscalibration to watch

Candidates frequently answer Section 2 at the wrong register in one of two directions. Some choose the most emotionally extreme option available, assuming stronger is more perceptive. Others choose the mildest and safe-sounding option, assuming restraint is safer. Both are guesses about the test rather than readings of the scenario. The answer is the specific emotion that the evidence in *this* scenario supports, at the temperature *this* character has been established at.

## Self-check

1. A scenario ends with a character declining an invitation and saying it is "probably for the best". What is usually being inferred? *(An emotion beneath the stated position — relief mixed with something the character is not saying aloud, or a hurt masked as practicality. The final line carries the emotional content; the middle is context.)*
2. You find yourself trying to work out what the missing document is or how the schedule clash gets resolved. What have you misread? *(That the item is a puzzle. Section 2 asks for an emotion, a motive, a likely next action or a consistency judgement — the situation is the vehicle, not the task.)*
3. Options range from "mildly awkward" to "devastated". How do you choose? *(Match the temperature the scenario establishes for that character. The correct answer is the specific emotion the evidence supports, not the strongest or the mildest offered.)*
4. Why is the consequence test useful? *(Because a correct answer explains the whole scenario while a wrong one typically resolves a single moment and leaves the other details without explanation.)*
5. In a ranking item, why read the question before the scenario? *(The question names the axis you are ranking along, which is frequently not the axis the scenario seems to be about. Ranking on the obvious axis produces a confident, ordered and wrong answer.)*
