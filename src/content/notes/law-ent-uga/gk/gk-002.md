# Constitutional History of Uganda

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

- **Definition:** General Knowledge Topic 2 for the Uganda Law Admission Examination tests awareness of Uganda's constitutional history, governance institutions, the court hierarchy, and the legal framework a Bar Course candidate is expected to know.
- **Core instrument:** The **Constitution of Uganda 1995** (as amended) is the supreme law; Chapter Four contains the **Bill of Rights**, the most-tested section in GK.
- **Three arms of government:** Executive (President, Cabinet), Legislature (Parliament — unicameral), Judiciary (courts from Magistrates' Courts up to the Supreme Court).
- **Key statutes:** Judicature Act Cap 13, Magistrates Courts Act Cap 16, Penal Code Act Cap 120, Electoral Commission Act Cap 140.
- **Two institutions often confused:** the **Uganda Law Society (ULS)** is the professional body of lawyers; the **Law Development Centre (LDC)** is the bar training institution.
- **Exam pointer:** GK carries 3% weight but is high-yield — questions are short, factual, and reward current-affairs reading on the **East African Community (EAC)** and the **Electoral Commission**.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Constitutional History of Uganda
Uganda's constitutional development runs from the **1894 British Protectorate**, through **1962 Independence Constitution**, the **1966 Pigeon Hole Constitution**, the **1967 Republican Constitution**, to the current **1995 Constitution** promulgated under the NRM government. Each step altered the balance between the Prime Minister, President, and Parliament. The 1995 Constitution introduced a strong executive presidency and a National Resistance Council system later replaced by the Movement to multi-party dispensation by the **2005 constitutional referendum**.

#### The 1995 Constitution: Salient Features
The Constitution declares itself the **supreme law** (Article 2); any law inconsistent with it is void to the extent of inconsistency. It contains **16 chapters**, of which **Chapter Four — the Bill of Rights** is binding on every person, organ, and agency of government. Other notable chapters cover citizenship, the Republic, representation of the people, the judiciary, finance, and the EAC.

#### Arms of Government and Court Hierarchy
Uganda practices a **separation of powers** with checks and balances:

| Arm | Head | Core Function |
|---|---|---|
| Executive | President | Policy execution, assent to Bills |
| Legislature | Speaker of Parliament | Law-making, oversight, budgeting |
| Judiciary | Chief Justice | Adjudication, constitutional interpretation |

The **court hierarchy** runs: Magistrates' Courts → High Court → Court of Appeal → Supreme Court. The Supreme Court is the final appellate court; the Constitutional Court is a specialised division of the High Court handling constitutional petitions.

#### Sources of Ugandan Law
Sources include the Constitution, Acts of Parliament, common law (English doctrines received pre-1962), doctrines of equity, statutes of general application, African customary law (where not repugnant to natural justice), and delegated legislation.

#### Key Institutions
- **Electoral Commission (EC)** — conducts and supervises elections under Article 61 and the Electoral Commission Act Cap 140.
- **Uganda Human Rights Commission (UHRC)** — promotes and protects rights under Chapter Four.
- **Inspectorate of Government (IGG)** — ombudsman against corruption.
- **Law Development Centre (LDC)** — the practical legal training institution for the Bar Course.
- **Uganda Law Society (ULS)** — the statutory bar association.

#### Typical Exam Question Patterns
GK Paper questions test **who, what, when** facts: e.g., "Under which chapter are fundamental rights enshrined?", "Name the body that supervises elections in Uganda", "Which article declares the Constitution supreme?" Candidates should expect multiple-choice and short-answer items that reward memorisation of chapter numbers and institutional mandates.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Constitutional Tensions
Candidates often miss that **Chapter Four rights are subject to limitations** under Article 43 during states of emergency, and that derogations must be non-discriminatory, proportionate, and time-bound. Another subtle point: the **non-derogable rights** in Article 44 (right to life, freedom from torture, freedom from slavery, fair hearing) cannot be limited even in emergency.

#### Common Mistakes Examiners Exploit

> Memorise the **mandate**, not the **acronym**. The IGG fights corruption; the UHRC protects rights; the EC runs elections. Mixing these up is the single most common LDC trap.

- **Wrong constitution year:** Citing 1962 or 1967 provisions when answering 1995 Constitution questions.
- **Confusing LDC with ULS:** LDC trains; ULS regulates the profession.
- **Citing repealed statutes:** Always check for amendments to the Judicature Act, Penal Code, and Magistrates Courts Act.
- **Ignoring EAC obligations:** Uganda is party to the **East African Community Treaty** and the EAC Common Market Protocol, often tested under current affairs.
- **Skipping Chapter Four entirely:** Human-rights-based questions (habeas corpus, fair hearing, freedom of expression) appear in nearly every GK sitting.

#### Worked Micro-Example
**Sample MCQ:** "Which chapter of the 1995 Constitution of Uganda contains the Bill of Rights?"
**Answer:** Chapter Four.
**Reasoning:** Article 45 of the Constitution expressly states that Chapter Four is the Bill of Rights, enforceable against the State and natural persons.

#### Practice Prompts
1. Differentiate the constitutional mandates of the **Inspectorate of Government** and the **Uganda Human Rights Commission**, citing the relevant constitutional provisions.
2. Outline the **sources of Ugandan law** as recognised by the Judicature Act and the 1995 Constitution, with reference to how African customary law fits into the hierarchy.
