# Response and Homeostasis — Leaving Certificate (Established) Biology Notes

Unit 2.4 is the broadest unit in the specification. It carries weight 4 and covers **four response systems** — hormonal, nervous, musculoskeletal and immune — plus plant response, plus homeostasis, plus emerging infectious disease. Read as a list it is unwieldy. Read as a single argument it is simple: **an organism must detect a change, communicate it to a control centre, and act to restore the set point.** Every system in the unit is a different answer to how you detect, how you communicate and how you act.

That framing is what marks in this unit. "Trace a homeostatic feedback loop" is worth far more than eight disconnected definitions, and the endocrine table is worth more if you can see each hormone as a message travelling in blood rather than as a name to be recalled. Learn the loop shape once and you can apply it to blood glucose, body temperature, blood pressure, water balance and blood CO₂ without re-learning anything.

---

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Homeostasis** is the maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment despite change. Control is by **negative feedback**: a change from the set point is detected, a response reverses it, and the response switches off as the set point is reached. Positive feedback is rarer and amplifies a change — it is used in childbirth (contraction stimulates oxytocin release, which strengthens contraction) and in blood clotting.
- **The reflex arc**: receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone in the spinal cord → motor neurone → effector (muscle or gland). It is fast, involuntary, and does not require conscious thought, because the decision is made at the spinal cord before the signal reaches the brain.
- **Neuron structure.** **Cell body** (cytoplasm, nucleus, dendrites), **dendrites** receive impulses, **axon** conducts the impulse away, **myelin sheath** insulates the axon and speeds conduction by allowing saltatory conduction between nodes, **Schwann cells** produce the myelin in the peripheral nervous system. **Neurotransmitter vesicles** in the axon terminal release transmitter into the synapse.
- **Transmission at a synapse.** An impulse arrives at the axon terminal, ion movement depolarises the membrane, vesicles fuse and release neurotransmitter by exocytosis, the transmitter diffuses across the **cleft** and binds receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, generating a new impulse. The transmitter is then either **degraded by an enzyme** in the cleft or **reabsorbed** by the presynaptic neurone and recycled.
- **Disruption of transmission.** Drugs that inhibit the enzymes degrading neurotransmitters, or that block reuptake, leave more transmitter in the cleft and create an enhanced effect. This is how painkillers and psychoactive drugs work, and it is why the effect is a *continuation* rather than a new signal.
- **Nervous vs hormonal coordination.** Nervous: fast (milliseconds), localised, short-lived, precise. Hormonal: slow (seconds to days), widespread, long-lasting, via blood. The specification asks you to compare them on more than speed.
- **Skeletal system.** *Axial*: skull, vertebrae, ribs, sternum, with discs between vertebrae that allow movement and absorb shock. *Appendicular*: pectoral girdle (clavicle, scapula), pelvic girdle, and the limb bones — femur, patella, tibia, fibula, humerus, radius, ulna.
- **Antagonistic muscle pairs.** Muscles work in pairs: one contracts while the other relaxes. The **biceps** flexes the arm and the **triceps** extends it; the pair can only shorten one of them, so opposing action requires relaxation of the other. **Tendons** attach muscle to bone and transmit force; **ligaments** attach bone to bone and stabilise a joint; **cartilage** at the ends of bones reduces friction, absorbs shock and stops bone rubbing on bone in a **synovial joint**.
- **Endocrine glands, locations and secretions** (specification list): **adrenal** (adrenaline, on top of each kidney); **pituitary** (FSH, LH, oxytocin, TSH, ADH — in the brain, the master gland); **thyroid** (thyroxine, in the neck); **parathyroid** (parathyroid hormone); **pancreas** (insulin); **testes** (testosterone); **ovaries** (oestrogen, progesterone).
- **Innate immunity** is present from birth, acts immediately, is not specific to one pathogen, and includes barriers such as skin, mucus, lysozyme and white blood cells such as **monocytes** and **natural killer cells**.
- **Adaptive (acquired) immunity** is specific, slow on first exposure, and has memory. It rests on the **antigen–antibody response**: **B lymphocytes** produce antibodies against a specific antigen and form memory cells; **T lymphocytes** act directly — helper T cells activate B cells and other T cells, killer T cells destroy infected cells, suppressor T cells limit the response, and memory T cells provide long-term protection.
- **Active vs passive immunity.** Active immunity: the body makes its own antibodies after infection or **vaccination**, is slow to develop and **long-lasting**. Passive immunity: ready-made antibodies are transferred by **breastfeeding (colostrum)**, by an antibody booster injection such as anti-tetanus immunoglobulin, or by placenta. It is immediate but temporary.
- **DNA vs RNA viruses.** DNA viruses usually replicate using the host's DNA polymerase; RNA viruses must first make a complementary DNA copy using **reverse transcriptase**, and influenza and coronaviruses use an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase. This is why antibiotics do not work against viral infections.
- **Emergence of infectious disease.** Spread is facilitated by local, regional and global movement of organisms. Emergence depends on interrelated factors: persistence of the pathogen in a host, mutation, antibiotic or chemical resistance, the immunity level present in the population, mobility of affected populations, and the **reproduction number (R₀)**. Understanding these allows strategies to control an increase.

#### Examiner traps

- **Describing negative feedback as "keeping it constant".** The point is that the response *reverses the deviation* and then stops. Naming the set point and the reversing response is the mark.
- **Routing the reflex arc through the brain.** Sensory → relay (spinal cord) → motor. Putting the brain in the path destroys the point of a reflex.
- **Saying the impulse "jumps" across the synapse.** There is no electrical continuity across the cleft; the signal is chemical, and the electrical signal resumes on the other side.
- **Confusing neuropeptides with neurotransmitters.** Neurotransmitters carry the signal; neuropeptides act as co-transmitters or neuromodulators over longer ranges.
- **Forgetting a gland's location when asked for it.** The specification asks for both.
- **Saying vaccines give immediate protection.** Active immunity takes days to weeks; passive immunity gives immediate but temporary protection. Reversing these is a standard trap.
- **Treating R₀ as a percentage.** It is a number: the average number of secondary cases produced by one infected individual in a fully susceptible population. Above 1, an outbreak grows.

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### The homeostasis loop, written out

Learn this five-part frame once. It applies to every homeostatic question in the paper.

> **(1) Stimulus** — the internal or external change. **(2) Receptor** — detects the change from the set point. **(3) Control centre** — compares the value with the set point and decides what to do (nervous: a nucleus in the hypothalamus or spinal cord; hormonal: the pituitary). **(4) Effector** — the organ, muscle or gland that acts. **(5) Response** — reverses the deviation. **The response then reduces the stimulation and the loop switches off.**

*Worked example — blood glucose.* After a meal, blood glucose rises above its normal range. **Receptors** in the wall of the pancreas detect the rise. The **control centre** is the pancreatic cells themselves, which respond directly. The **effector** is the pancreas, which releases **insulin**. Insulin makes target cells, especially liver and muscle cells, take up glucose by facilitated diffusion and converts glucose to **glycogen** for storage. Blood glucose falls back towards the set point, insulin secretion falls, and the loop switches off. When blood glucose falls below the set point, as in fasting, the pancreas releases **glucagon**, which converts glycogen back to glucose and releases it into the blood. Both are negative feedback: the response reverses the original deviation.

#### The synapse and drug action

Draw it. Label the axon terminal, the vesicles, the cleft, the receptor sites on the postsynaptic membrane, the enzyme and the reuptake transporter.

*Worked question.* A drug blocks the reuptake transporter in the presynaptic neurone. Explain its effect on the impulse.
*A.* When an impulse arrives, vesicles release neurotransmitter normally, so an impulse is still generated in the postsynaptic cell. But the transmitter is not removed from the cleft by reuptake, so it remains available and continues to bind receptors. The postsynaptic cell is stimulated repeatedly and for longer than it should be, so an enhanced signal persists after the original impulse has passed. This is the mechanism behind many psychoactive drugs, and the same logic explains how drugs that inhibit the enzymes degrading transmitters — for example those that prevent breakdown of endorphins — produce analgesia. The distinction the specification asks for is that the drug has not created a new signal; it has prevented the normal termination of the existing one.

#### Endocrine glands — the table to memorise

| Gland | Location | Hormone | Key role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pituitary | Base of the brain | FSH, LH, oxytocin, TSH, ADH | Master gland; controls other glands; ADH regulates water reabsorption |
| Thyroid | Neck | Thyroxine | Sets metabolic rate; needs iodine |
| Parathyroid | Behind the thyroid | Parathyroid hormone | Raises blood calcium |
| Adrenal | On top of each kidney | Adrenaline | Short-term fight-or-flight response |
| Pancreas | Abdomen | Insulin | Lowers blood glucose |
| Testes | Scrotum | Testosterone | Male secondary sex characteristics |
| Ovaries | Abdomen | Oestrogen, progesterone | Female cycle, pregnancy, secondary sex characteristics |

Two examples of hormonal control the paper likes. **Blood water balance**: when plasma is too concentrated, osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus release **ADH** from the pituitary, which increases water reabsorption in the kidney, concentrating the urine. **Thyroid**: if the thyroid fails, thyroxine falls, TSH rises, and the raised TSH can cause a goitre — a negative feedback loop with a visible sign. **Hormonal manipulation** includes using thyroxine for hypothyroidism, insulin for diabetes, and anabolic steroids in sport and agriculture, each of which the specification expects you to discuss as a deliberate intervention into a feedback system.

#### The immune response, ordered

1. A pathogen breaches a barrier and is recognised as foreign — an **antigen**.
2. **Innate** response acts immediately: phagocytosis by monocytes/macrophages and natural killer cells, plus inflammation.
3. **Adaptive** response: helper T cells recognise the antigen and activate **B lymphocytes**, which differentiate into plasma cells producing **antibodies** specific to that antigen, and into **memory B cells**. Killer T cells destroy infected cells. Suppressor T cells switch the response off once the threat is cleared.
4. **Memory cells** remain, so a second exposure is met far faster — this is the mechanism vaccination relies on.
5. **DNA viruses** and **RNA viruses** are distinguished in the specification; RNA viruses need reverse transcriptase, which is why antiviral targets differ from antibacterial ones.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Worked Higher-level questions with answers

**Q1.** Explain how a rise in blood CO₂ is detected and corrected, and how this differs from temperature regulation.
*A.* In both cases the mechanism is negative feedback with a set point, a receptor, a control centre, an effector and a reversing response. In CO₂ regulation, chemoreceptors in the **medulla oblongata** detect the rise; the respiratory centre sends impulses to the **diaphragm and intercostal muscles**, which contract more strongly and faster, increasing ventilation so more CO₂ is exhaled. The rise in blood pH acts as the negative feedback. In temperature regulation, **thermoreceptors in the skin and hypothalamus** detect the change; the hypothalamus drives responses through both the nervous system (sweating via sweat glands, shivering via skeletal muscle, and skin blood vessel dilation to lose heat) and the hormonal system via the **thyroid** for longer-term metabolic heat production. The key difference is that CO₂ is corrected almost entirely by a single effector — breathing rate — whereas temperature is corrected through several effectors operating in opposite directions, and it is the balance between them that holds temperature steady.

**Q2.** Explain why breastfeeding provides the infant with immunity that a vaccine does not, and why they are not interchangeable.
*A.* Breast milk contains antibodies produced by the mother's immune system, transferred to the infant as **passive** immunity. The infant's own immune system is not stimulated, no memory cells are formed, and protection lasts only as long as the antibodies persist — so it stops when breastfeeding stops. A vaccine exposes the infant to an antigen, which drives their own B and T lymphocytes to produce antibodies and, crucially, memory cells, so the immune system is genuinely prepared for later exposure. Active immunity is slower to develop but long-lasting; passive is immediate but temporary. Neither is better in general — the two do different jobs, which is why colostrum in the first days of life and the standard vaccination schedule are both used.

**Q3.** A population has a disease with R₀ = 3. Explain what this means and what it predicts.
*A.* R₀ is the average number of secondary infections produced by one infected individual in a fully susceptible population. An R₀ of 3 means each case causes roughly three new cases, so the outbreak grows exponentially. Control requires reducing the *effective* reproduction number below 1, because at exactly 1 the number of cases stays constant and below 1 it falls. The specification asks you to link R₀ to the other emergence factors: **immunity level in the population** (vaccination reduces the susceptible pool), **mobility** (movement of organisms and people between regions speeds spread), **persistence in a host** (a long infectious period keeps R₀ high), **mutation** (an antigenic change can allow immune escape) and **antibiotic resistance** (for bacterial pathogens, resistance lengthens the period of infectiousness). Naming several of these and linking them is what earns the evaluation marks.

**Q4.** Explain why antagonistic muscle pairs are necessary for controlled movement.
*A.* A muscle can only contract and shorten; it cannot actively lengthen. The **biceps** shortens to flex the arm, and the **triceps** shortens to extend it. If only the biceps existed, the arm could flex but could not be straightened again. Because the triceps relaxes as the biceps contracts, and vice versa, the limb can be moved in either direction and — crucially — held steady at an intermediate position by having both muscles partially contracted at once. This opposing arrangement also protects the joint: if both contracted together the humerus would be pulled against the shoulder socket and the tendons would be strained. Fine control of this balance is what allows graded movements rather than all-or-nothing ones.

#### How this unit pays elsewhere

- **Unit 1.3** gives the membrane that neurotransmitters cross and the receptor proteins they bind to.
- **Unit 1.5** explains why antibiotic resistance spreads — the same variation-and-selection account applies to a bacterial population under drug pressure.
- **Unit 2.5** reuses the pituitary hormones FSH, LH and oxytocin, and the ovaries' oestrogen and progesterone, as the control system for reproduction.
- **Unit 2.6** covers the kidney's role in osmoregulation, which is the effector for ADH.
- **Unit 3.1** treats human activity — habitat destruction, overfishing, introduced species — as a disruption of ecological response.
- **Biology in Practice Investigation** (40% of the grade) — effect of exercise on pulse rate, effect of a substance on enzyme-mediated responses, investigating antimicrobial action against a pathogen: each is a natural fit.

#### Mistakes students actually make

1. **Narrating homeostasis without the loop.** Always include set point, receptor, control centre, effector and the reversing response.
2. **Describing negative feedback as maintaining a constant.** External conditions change constantly; what is maintained is the *set point*, and the system keeps correcting towards it.
3. **Leaving off the position of a gland.** The specification lists gland, location and secretion together.
4. **Confusing the immune systems.** Innate is immediate and non-specific; adaptive is slow, specific and has memory; passive is immediate and temporary; active is slow and long-lasting. Four ideas, four different answers to "how fast and how long".
5. **Treating adrenaline as a slow hormonal response.** It is the fast one — the classic fight-or-flight example, chosen precisely because it shows the nervous and hormonal systems working together.
6. **Forgetting the drug's mechanism in a drug question.** The specification asks you to explain effects via inhibition of enzyme degradation or inhibition of reuptake — name which.
7. **Writing "all pathogens are killed by immunity".** The adaptive response is antigen-specific, so a pathogen the body has never met will not be handled quickly, which is precisely why new diseases spread.
