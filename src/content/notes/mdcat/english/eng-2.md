# Synonyms and Antonyms

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your MDCAT English paper.

**Synonyms** are words sharing the same or nearly the same meaning in the same language (e.g., *happy* → *joyful, glad, cheerful*). **Antonyms** carry opposite meanings (e.g., *hot ↔ cold*, *brave ↔ cowardly*). MDCAT items test not just dictionary match but **connotation, register, and context**.

- **Near-synonyms differ** in force or formality: *refuse / decline / reject* — politeness rises across the three.
- **Antonym types**: complementary (*dead ↔ alive*), gradable (*hot ↔ cold*), relational (*buy ↔ sell*).
- **Connotation trap**: *slim* (positive) ≠ *skinny* (negative); *famous* ≠ *notorious*; *stubborn* ≠ *persistent*.

> Memorise word lists **inside sentences**, never in isolation, so MCQ context-clues register fast.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months before MDCAT.

#### Core definitions

A **synonym** is a word whose meaning overlaps significantly with another word in standard usage. A **true synonym** is interchangeable in most contexts; a **near-synonym** overlaps but shifts in tone, formality, or collocational range. An **antonym** is a word whose meaning contrasts with another along an identifiable semantic axis.

#### Why context decides the answer

MDCAT rarely asks for raw dictionary equivalents. Most stems embed the target word in a sentence, and four options that *could* match a thesaurus entry are listed. The correct choice must fit the sentence's **register** (formal/informal), **collocation** (which adjectives sit with which nouns), and **connotation** (positive/negative charge).

| Near-synonym pair | Distinguishing axis |
|---|---|
| *refuse / decline / reject* | Increasing force + decreasing politeness |
| *slim / skinny / lean* | Positive neutral → pejorative → neutral athletic |
| *famous / renowned / notorious* | Neutral → high-prestige → negative-prestige |
| *stubborn / persistent / tenacious* | Negative → neutral → positive |

#### Three classes of antonyms

- **Complementary** — binary opposites with no middle: *alive / dead, true / false, present / absent*.
- **Gradable** — opposites on a continuum admitting degrees: *hot / cold, rich / poor, tall / short*.
- **Relational** — opposites defined by paired roles: *teacher / student, buy / sell, give / receive*.

#### How MDCAT tests this

Typical stems: *"The word most nearly OPPOSITE in meaning to..."* and *"The word closest in MEANING to..."* inside a one-line context. The English section of MDCAT carries roughly **3% weight** of the total score, and vocabulary items appear both as standalone MCQs and embedded in sentence-completion passages.

> Skim every stem for a tone cue — a single adjective like *unwillingly* or *politely* flips the correct answer from *refuse* to *decline*.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students building durable vocabulary depth for MDCAT and beyond.

#### Word-formation shortcuts for unfamiliar items

Prefixes flip polarity predictably: **un-**, **in-**, **im-**, **ir-**, **dis-**, **non-**, **anti-** commonly generate antonyms (*happy → unhappy, legal → illegal, mortal → immortal*). Suffixes such as **-less** (*hopeless*), **-ful** (*hopeful*), and **-ous / -ly** reshape connotation. Studying **roots** (*bene- good, mal- bad, -vert turn, -logy study*) lets you derive meaning of unseen words under exam pressure — a critical MDCAT skill because vocabulary MCQs sometimes include options you have never seen verbatim.

#### Edge cases that examiners exploit

- **Negation prefixes are not universal antonyms**: *unfold* does work as an antonym of *fold*, but *unload ≠ opposite of load* in all contexts, and *involve* has no clean prefix-derived antonym.
- **Relational pairs are trickier than they look**: in some MCQ formats, *enemy* is rejected as the antonym of *friend* because the better relational pair is *stranger / ally* — always check whether the question demands a binary or a relational opposite.
- **Connotation drives near-synonym answers**: *annoyed* and *furious* are *both* anger-words, but they sit far apart on the intensity scale; choosing *furious* for a sentence saying *"she was mildly annoyed"* is a classic trap.

#### Practice prompts

1. Pick the best substitute: *"She gave a **concise** summary."* — *(A) brief (B) curt (C) terse (D) tiny*. Justify why *brief* beats *curt* (which is rude) and *terse* (abrupt tone).
2. Identify the antonym type of *optimistic ↔ pessimistic*: complementary, gradable, or relational? Explain using a scale of intensity.

#### Exam-day strategy

In the English section, budget **under 30 seconds** per synonym/antonym MCQ. Eliminate two options by connotation mismatch, then test the remaining pair against the sentence's register. Words learned only from rote lists collapse here; words anchored in **example sentences** survive.
