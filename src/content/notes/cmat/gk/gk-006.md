# Indian Polity and Constitution

### 🟢 Lite

India's Constitution took effect on **26 January 1950**, drafted by a **Constituent Assembly of 299 members** chaired by **Dr. Rajendra Prasad**, with the Drafting Committee under **Dr. B. R. Ambedkar**. It is the longest written constitution in the world and borrows from a specific list of sources — knowing *which* source gave *which* provision is the fastest way to score on this topic.

#### Three instruments, one contrast

| Instrument | Justiciable? | Part | Added by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fundamental Rights** | Yes — enforceable in court | III | The Constitution itself, 1950 |
| **Directive Principles (DPSP)** | No — guide to governance | IV | The Constitution itself, 1950 |
| **Fundamental Duties** | No — moral obligation | IV-A | **86th Amendment, 2002** |

**Memory hook — "Rights sue, Principles advise, Duties oblige."** Every polity question that turns on enforceability collapses to that one line.

#### Sources of the Constitution — match the borrow, not the country

| Source | Borrowed |
|---|---|
| **Government of India Act, 1935** | Federal structure, administrative detail |
| **USA** | Fundamental Rights, independent judiciary, judicial review |
| **UK** | Parliamentary system, rule of law, conventions |
| **Ireland** | Directive Principles of State Policy |
| **Canada** | Federation with a strong Centre |
| **Japan** | Concurrent List |
| **Weimar (Germany)** | Emergency provisions |

#### The six Fundamental Rights, by article span

| # | Right | Articles |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Equality | 14–18 |
| 2 | Freedom | 19–22 |
| 3 | Against Exploitation | 23–24 |
| 4 | Freedom of Religion | 25–28 |
| 5 | Cultural & Educational | 29–30 |
| 6 | Constitutional Remedies | 32 |

The spans read 14–18, 19–22, 23–24, 25–28, 29–30, **32** — the gap at **31** is itself examinable, because Article 31 (Right to Property) was removed from Part III by the 44th Amendment (1978) and now sits in Part XII.

### 🟡 Standard

#### Making of the Constitution

India became independent on 15 August 1947. The Constitution came into force on **26 January 1950** — Republic Day, because on that date in 1929 the Congress under Nehru had declared *Purna Swaraj*. The **Constituent Assembly** had 299 members and was chaired by **Dr. Rajendra Prasad**; the **Drafting Committee** was chaired by **Dr. B. R. Ambedkar**. The Draft Constitution was submitted on **26 November 1947**, now National Law Day.

#### The Preamble

> *"We, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC and to secure to all its citizens: JUSTICE, social, economic and political; LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; EQUALITY of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all FRATERNITY assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation."*

- **Sovereign** — independent, under no external authority.
- **Socialist** — added by the 42nd Amendment (1976); mixed economy and welfare state.
- **Secular** — added by the 42nd Amendment (1976); no state religion.
- **Democratic** — rule through elected representatives.
- **Republic** — the head of state is elected, never hereditary.
- **Integrity** — added by the 42nd Amendment (1976) to the duty it later created.

#### Structural features

- Longest written constitution in the world — originally 395 articles, now around 470 with schedules.
- Neither rigid nor flexible: some provisions need a special majority plus a half of each House to amend.
- Federal with a **unitary bias** — emergency provisions let the Centre act on the States.
- Parliamentary system, so the executive answers to the legislature.
- Independent judiciary; the Supreme Court is the guardian of the Constitution.
- **Single citizenship** — there is no separate state citizenship.
- Official language: Hindi in Devanagari, with English continuing for official purposes; 22 languages are in the Eighth Schedule.

#### Fundamental Rights in detail (Articles 12–35)

| # | Right | Articles | Key content |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Equality | 14–18 | Art 14 equality before law; Art 15 non-discrimination; Art 16 equal opportunity in employment; Art 17 abolishes untouchability; Art 18 abolishes titles |
| 2 | Freedom | 19–22 | Art 19's six freedoms; Art 20 protection in respect of conviction; **Art 21** life and personal liberty; Art 21A right to education; Art 22 protection against arrest |
| 3 | Against Exploitation | 23–24 | No trafficking or forced labour; no child below 14 in a factory |
| 4 | Freedom of Religion | 25–28 | Conscience and free profession; management of religious affairs; no taxes to promote a religion; no religious instruction in state-funded institutions |
| 5 | Cultural & Educational | 29–30 | Protection of minority interests; right to establish educational institutions |
| 6 | Constitutional Remedies | 32 | Move the Supreme Court to enforce a Right — Ambedkar called this "the heart and soul of the Constitution" |

**Article 21** is the one that moves. The Supreme Court has read into it the right to privacy (*K.S. Puttaswamy*, 2017), the right to die with dignity (2018), and rights to a clean environment, to health, and to sleep.

#### Fundamental Duties (Article 51A)

Added by the **86th Amendment (2002)** on the Swaran Singh Committee's recommendation. Ten duties: abide by the Constitution and respect the flag and anthem; cherish the ideals of the freedom struggle; protect sovereignty, unity and integrity; defend the country when called upon; promote harmony and brotherhood; preserve the composite culture; protect the natural environment; develop a scientific temper; safeguard public property; strive for excellence.

They are **non-justiciable** — moral obligations, not court-enforceable.

#### Directive Principles (Articles 36–51)

Non-justiciable, but binding on the State in governance, and aimed at building a welfare state.

| Article | Content |
|---|---|
| 38 | Social order in which justice informs all institutions |
| 39 | Equal means of livelihood; prevention of concentration of wealth |
| 40 | Organisation of village panchayats |
| 43 | Living wage for workers |
| 44 | Uniform Civil Code |
| 48 | Protection of environment and wildlife |

In **Minerva Mills (1980)** the Supreme Court held that the balance between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs cannot be destroyed, and that Parliament cannot amend the Basic Structure.

#### Parliament

**Lok Sabha** — lower house, maximum **552** seats (530 from States, up to 20 from Union Territories). Directly elected; the voting age fell to 18 with the 61st Amendment (1988). Term of 5 years. A **Money Bill** can be introduced only here, and the Rajya Sabha's power is limited to a 14-day recommendation.

> The two Anglo-Indian nominated seats were discontinued by the **104th Amendment (2019)**; the 106th Amendment (2023) allows a vacant seat to be filled by nomination.

**Rajya Sabha** — upper house, maximum **250** seats. Members are elected indirectly by state legislative assemblies using proportional representation by single transferable vote. Six-year terms with one-third retiring every two years. It alone can initiate bills for creating a new state, and it recommends the creation of All-India Services.

#### The President

Largely ceremonial, since real executive power sits with the Prime Minister. Elected by an electoral college of both Houses of Parliament and the state legislative assemblies. Five-year term, maximum two. Qualifications: citizen of India, aged 35 or above, and not a member of Parliament or a state legislature.

Powers: summons and prorogues Parliament and dissolves the Lok Sabha; appoints the Chief Justice and other judges; appoints the leader who commands a Lok Sabha majority as Prime Minister; declares emergencies; pardons under Article 72, including death sentences.

**Emergencies**

| Kind | Article | Trigger | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| National | 352 | War, external aggression, armed rebellion, on the Cabinet's written advice | 1971 and 1975 |
| State (President's Rule) | 356 | Failure of constitutional machinery in a State | Imposed many times over the decades |
| Financial | 360 | Financial stability threatened | Never used |

#### The Prime Minister and Council of Ministers

The President appoints the majority leader as Prime Minister. The **Council of Ministers** is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha; the **Cabinet** is the senior inner group that settles policy. The PM controls the legislative agenda and is the chief adviser to the President.

### 🔴 Extended

#### Worked item

**Question.** The Right to Property was removed from Part III of the Constitution and placed in Part XII. Which amendment did this, and in which year?

- (A) 42nd Amendment, 1976
- (B) 44th Amendment, 1978
- (C) 86th Amendment, 2002
- (D) 97th Amendment, 2011

**Resolution.**
1. Right to Property was Article 31 in Part III alongside the other Fundamental Rights.
2. The **44th Amendment (1978)** deleted Article 31 from Part III and re-placed it in Part XII.
3. That same amendment also restored the Lok Sabha's term from seven years to five, required written Cabinet advice before a national emergency, and dropped "internal disturbance" from Article 352.
4. Rule out the distractors: the 42nd (1976) *added* the 39th Schedule; the 86th (2002) inserted Article 21A; the 97th (2011) concerns co-operative societies.

**Answer: B**

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** the 42nd and 44th Amendments are neighbours in time and get swapped constantly. Anchor them this way — **42nd = the Indira years, additions; 44th = the Janata years, removals**. A frequent follow-on trap is the "Ten Shah" land reform laws, which sat in the 39th Schedule added by the 42nd and were deleted by the 44th.

#### Edges that change the answer

- **The 39th Schedule came from the 42nd Amendment (1976)**, not the 44th. Many study notes attach it to the 44th; that is the single most-repeated error in this table.
- **The official-language change is the 87th Amendment (2003)**, which continued Hindi as the official language and allowed English to continue for official purposes. The **97th Amendment (2011)** is about **co-operative societies** — it added Part IXB and the 48th Schedule, and parts of Part IXB were struck down by the Supreme Court in 2022.
- **Article 21A did not create the right to education from scratch in spirit only** — the 86th Amendment (2002) inserted it, and it now carries a continuous obligation from 6 to 14 years.
- **Fundamental Duties are not justiciable.** A question asking which instrument a court can enforce has exactly one answer: a Fundamental Right, reached through Article 32.
- **"Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic"** — three of those five words are not in the original 1950 text. Socialist, Secular and Integrity all arrived with the 42nd Amendment.

#### CMAT strategy

Polity in General Awareness rewards pattern recognition over detail. Learn the article-number skeleton (14, 19, 21, 32; 40, 44; 51A; 352, 356, 360) and the amendment-to-content map above, and most options can be eliminated on structure alone: if a choice gives an amendment a year that does not match its era, discard it before reading the rest.

## CMAT Exam Focus

- **Preamble**: Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic; Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity
- **Fundamental Rights**: Six rights, Articles 14–35; Article 21 keeps expanding
- **Fundamental Duties**: Article 51A, ten duties, 86th Amendment (2002), non-justiciable
- **DPSP**: Articles 36–51, non-justiciable; *Minerva Mills* (1980)
- **Parliament**: Bicameral — Lok Sabha (552, direct, 5-year) and Rajya Sabha (250, indirect, 6-year, rotational)
- **President**: Electoral college; emergencies under Articles 352, 356 and 360
- **Amendments**: 42nd (1976, additions), 44th (1978, removals), 73rd/74th (1992, local bodies), 86th (2002, RTE), 87th (2003, language), 93rd (2005, OBC), 97th (2011, co-operatives)
