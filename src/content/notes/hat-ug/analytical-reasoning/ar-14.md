# Venn Diagrams

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **Venn diagram** pictures sets as overlapping circles inside a rectangle that represents the universal set **U**, letting you read off union, intersection, difference, and complement at a glance.

- **Union** A ∪ B = every element in either circle.
- **Intersection** A ∩ B = the overlapping lens-shaped region.
- **Complement** A′ = everything inside U but outside A.

The single formula that solves most HAT-UG numerical items is the inclusion–exclusion rule: **n(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∩ B)**. For three sets, add the triple intersection back once more. Always subtract the pairwise overlaps to avoid double-counting.

- "Only A" means A minus the intersection, never A ∪ B.
- "Neither A nor B" lives in the region of U outside both circles.
- HAT-UG usually gives 2 or 3 sets with 3–4 numeric conditions and asks for a missing region.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Reading the diagram

Each circle bounds one set. The rectangle around them is the **universal set U**, and the blank area inside U but outside every circle holds elements that belong to none of the named sets. When two circles cross, the lens they share is **A ∩ B**; the crescent of A alone (A minus the lens) is **"only A"**. With three circles, the seven distinct regions are: three "only" crescents, three pairwise intersections, one triple intersection, plus the outer "neither" zone.

#### Translating phrases into regions

Word problems in HAT-UG reward precise translation. "Only doctors" means *only the crescent of the Doctors circle* — never the entire circle. "At least one of A, B, C" is the union A ∪ B ∪ C. "None of A, B, C" sits in U outside all three circles.

#### The two working formulas

| Formula | Use case |
| --- | --- |
| n(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∩ B) | Two-set word problems; subtracts the overlap counted twice |
| n(A ∪ B ∪ C) = n(A)+n(B)+n(C) − pairwise sums + n(A∩B∩C) | Three-set problems; adds the triple intersection back once |

#### De Morgan's laws

~(A ∪ B) = ~A ∩ ~B, and ~(A ∩ B) = ~A ∪ ~B. Use them when a question asks for "neither" or "not in either" without drawing the diagram.

- **Double-counting trap:** adding n(A) + n(B) without subtracting the intersection inflates the answer.
- **"Only" trap:** only-A = n(A) − n(A∩B), not n(A) alone.
- **Empty-intersection shortcut:** if A ∩ B = 0, then n(A ∪ B) is just a straight sum.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge cases examiners exploit

The HAT-UG paper occasionally frames a Venn problem where two sets look disjoint but actually share a small element, or where "at least one" is misread as "exactly one". Train yourself to circle the quantifier in the stem. Also watch for **symmetric difference** A △ B, which is the union minus the intersection — useful when a question asks for "belonging to exactly one set".

#### Worked micro-example

In a class of 50, 30 study Biology (B), 25 study Chemistry (C), and 10 study both. Find the number studying at least one subject.

- n(B ∪ C) = n(B) + n(C) − n(B∩C) = 30 + 25 − 10 = **45**.
- Neither subject: 50 − 45 = **5**.
- Only Biology: 30 − 10 = **20**; only Chemistry: 25 − 10 = **15**.

All four numbers (20, 15, 10, 5) sum to 50, confirming the partition.

#### Connections to adjacent topics

Venn logic underpins **syllogisms**, **set-builder notation**, and **probability of events** (P(A ∪ B) uses the same inclusion–exclusion structure). Mastering the diagram here makes conditional probability questions faster later.

#### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| Treating "only A" as the whole circle A | Subtract n(A ∩ B) |
| Ignoring the "neither" region | Always include U outside all circles |
| Misapplying the three-set formula | Add triple intersection once, not twice |

#### Practice prompts

1. In a survey of 100 people, 60 like tea, 50 like coffee, 40 like both. How many like at least one, and how many like neither?
2. Of 70 students, 35 play cricket, 30 play football, 20 play both. A student is picked at random. What is the probability they play exactly one sport?
