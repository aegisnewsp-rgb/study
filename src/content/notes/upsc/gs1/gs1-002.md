# Indian History

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Indian History for UPSC CSE GS1 spans Ancient, Medieval, and Modern epochs. Ancient history anchors on Harappan urbanism, Vedic social stratification, Buddhist-Jain heterodoxy, and imperial statecraft under Mauryan and Gupta polities. Medieval history centers on Delhi Sultanate administrative structures, the Mughal revenue-mansab nexus, and syncretic Bhakti-Sufi traditions. Modern history examines British colonial extraction, constitutional enactments, and anti-colonial mass mobilization leading to independence in 1947.

| Epoch | Primary Turning Point | Administrative / Institutional Core | Canonical Primary Source | High-Frequency UPSC Theme |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ancient** (c. 2600 BCE – 750 CE) | Iron tool adoption, urbanization, and imperial consolidation | Mauryan *Saptanga* statecraft; Gupta land grants (*Agraharas*) | *Arthashastra*, Ashokan Pillar Edicts, Megasthenes\x27 *Indica* | Harappan drainage, Ashokan Dhamma edicts, Gupta temple art |
| **Early Medieval & Sultanate** (750 – 1526 CE) | Tripartite struggle; Turkic centralized military monarchy | *Iqta* system of military revenue assignment; market regulations | *Kitab-i-Hind* (Alberuni), Barani\x27s *Tarikh-i-Firoz Shahi* | Chola local self-government (*Uttiramerur*), Alauddin market reforms |
| **Mughal Era** (1526 – 1707 CE) | Imperial bureaucratization and agrarian revenue rationalization | *Mansabdari* dual rank (*Zat* and *Sawar*); *Zabt-i-Dahsala* | Abul Fazl\x27s *Ain-i-Akbari*, Badauni\x27s *Muntakhab-ut-Tawarikh* | Akbar\x27s *Sulh-i-Kul*, Mughal painting schools, Jagirdari crisis |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### 1. Ancient Period: Harappan Urbanism to Classical Empires

The Indus Valley Civilization (c. 2600–1900 BCE) established urban standardization across the Indus-Saraswati basin. Sites such as Mohenjo-Daro, Harappa, Dholavira, and Lothal demonstrate standardized burnt-brick ratios (1:2:4), multi-tiered covered drainage conduits, and orthogonal grid layouts. Dholavira features stone architecture and sophisticated rainwater harvesting reservoirs cut into rock. Lothal contains an artificial tidal dockyard connected to the Sabarmati basin.

The Vedic transition demonstrates a shift from semi-nomadic pastoralism in the Sapta-Sindhu region to settled agriculture in the Ganga-Yamuna doab. Iron weapons and ploughshares (*ayas*) enabled clearing of dense forests around 1000 BCE. The fluid *Rigvedic* tribal assemblies (*Sabha* and *Samiti*) transformed into hierarchical monarchies (*Mahajanapadas*) by the sixth century BCE. Rigid hereditary *varna* classifications replaced occupational groupings.

| Structural Dimension | Mauryan Empire (c. 322 – 185 BCE) | Gupta Empire (c. 319 – 550 CE) |
|---|---|---|
| **Bureaucratic Structure** | Highly centralized; standing imperial army; expansive espionage network (*Gudhapurushas*) | Decentralized; feudatory administration (*Samantas*); hereditary offices (*Kumaramatyas*) |
| **Agrarian Taxation** | State-managed Crown lands (*Sita*); land tax (*Bhaga*) fixed at 1/6 to 1/4 of total yield | Proliferation of tax-free land grants (*Agrahara* and *Brahmadeya*) to religious recipients |
| **Legal Framework** | Secular state law codified under royal decree (*Dharma-nyaya* override) | Strict adherence to orthodox *Dharmashastras* and custom-based *Smriti* law |
| **Imperial Ideology** | Ashoka\x27s *Dhamma* (ethical paternalism, non-violence, public works) | Brahmanical revivalism, royal *Ashvamedha* sacrifices, *Vaishnavite* imperial legitimacy |
| **Trade and Currency** | Uniform silver punch-marked coins (*Panas*); extensive state-controlled commerce | Abundant high-grade gold coins (*Dinaras*); decline in internal copper coinage |

#### 2. Medieval Governance: Sultanate and Mughal State Systems

The Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526 CE) established the *Iqta* system to extract agrarian surpluses and maintain standing cavalry without draining treasury cash. An *Iqtadar* or *Muqti* collected land revenue from assigned territories, deducted maintenance expenses for troops, and remitted the balance (*Fawazil*) to the central *Diwan-i-Wizarat*. Alauddin Khalji overhauled this by introducing direct land measurement (*Zariba*), fixing revenue at 50% of gross produce, and establishing four distinct regulated price markets in Delhi.

Akbar institutionalized Mughal governance through the *Mansabdari* system in 1571. Every imperial officer held a numerical rank composed of *Zat* (determining personal status, salary bracket, and hierarchical precedence) and *Sawar* (determining the exact cavalry contingent to maintain). In 1582, Raja Todar Mal established the *Ain-i-Dahsala* revenue system. The imperial diwan calculated average yields and average market prices over the preceding ten years (1570–1580 CE). One-third of this ten-year average was fixed as the state demand, payable directly in cash.

#### 3. Colonial Agrarian Restructuring

The East India Company restructured Indian land tenure to guarantee fixed revenue streams for colonial governance and raw material extraction. Three distinct systems partitioned the agricultural landscape.

| Feature | Permanent Settlement (Zamindari) | Ryotwari System | Mahalwari System |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Year & Architect** | 1793; Lord Cornwallis and John Shore | 1820; Thomas Munro and Alexander Read | 1822; Holt Mackenzie and Robert Merttins Bird |
| **Target Territories** | Bengal, Bihar, Odisha, Northern Madras, Varanasi | Madras, Bombay Presidencies, Assam, Coorg | North-Western Provinces, Punjab, Central Provinces |
| **Subcontinent Coverage** | Approximately 19% of British India | Approximately 51% of British India | Approximately 30% of British India |
| **Settlement Partner** | Hereditary Zamindars recognized as absolute soil owners | Direct legal contract with individual cultivating peasants (*Ryots*) | Village community (*Mahal*) represented by village headman (*Lambardar*) |
| **Revenue Assessment** | Fixed permanently in perpetuity; state claim set at 10/11th | Revised periodically every 20 to 30 years; 45% to 55% of gross produce | Revised periodically; assessed on total village agricultural output (50% to 66%) |
| **Sunset Law Enactment** | Strict forfeiture; estates auctioned if unpaid before sunset | Direct revenue attachment; confiscation of peasant holding upon default | Collective joint liability; entire village penalized for individual defaults |
| **Socio-Economic Impact** | Created absentee landlordism, sub-infeudation, and tenant eviction | Rural indebtedness, emergence of village moneylenders (*Mahajans*) | Disruption of traditional village collectives; peasant immiseration |

#### 4. The National Freedom Struggle: Evolution of Resistance

The anti-colonial movement progressed from the constitutional petitions of the Moderates (1885–1905) to direct action under the Extremists (1905–1918) following Lord Curzon\x27s Partition of Bengal. The entry of Mahatma Gandhi transformed Indian nationalism into a disciplined mass mobilization.

| Feature | Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–1922) | Civil Disobedience Movement (1930–1934) | Quit India Movement (1942) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Triggering Pretext** | Rowlatt Act (1919), Jallianwala Bagh massacre, Khilafat issue | British rejection of Nehru Report; refusal of Eleven Points | Failure of Cripps Mission; threat of Japanese invasion |
| **Foundational Demands** | Redress of Punjab atrocities; restoration of Ottoman Khilafat; *Swaraj* in one year | Complete independence (*Purna Swaraj*); abolition of Salt Tax; tariff protection | Immediate orderly withdrawal of British power; unconditional transfer of sovereignty |
| **Inaugural Action** | Surrender of imperial titles and honours by Gandhi and Tagore | 240-mile Dandi March from Sabarmati to Dandi (break salt monopoly) | All-India Congress Committee Bombay session; "Do or Die" resolution |
| **Forms of Protest** | Boycott of foreign cloth, legal courts, government schools, and councils | Defiance of salt laws, forest laws, non-payment of land revenue, liquor picketing | General strikes, sabotage of railway lines and telegraph wires, parallel governments |
| **Social Participation** | Urban middle classes, students, Muslim masses, small traders | Peasantry, merchants, women in unprecedented numbers; limited Muslim base | Working class, peasantry, youth; pan-Indian decentralized defiance |
| **Parallel Governments** | None established | None established | Established at Ballia (Chittu Pandey), Tamluk (*Jatiya Sarkar*), Satara (*Prati Sarkar*) |
| **Termination Mechanism** | Suspended by Gandhi after Chauri Chaura violence (5 Feb 1922) | Suspended via Gandhi-Irwin Pact (March 1931); officially ended in 1934 | Suppressed through military force; leaders imprisoned until 1945 |

#### Quantitative Breakdown: The Drain of Wealth Mechanics

Dadabhai Naoroji formulated the Drain of Wealth theory in his 1876 treatise *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India*. R.C. Dutt expanded this quantitative framework in *The Economic History of India* (1901). The drain represented the unilateral transfer of Indian financial surplus to Britain without equivalent economic or material return.

The mechanism operated through three primary accounting channels:
1. **Home Charges**: Direct payments disbursed in London by the Secretary of State for India from Indian tax receipts. These included pensions for civil and military personnel, furloughs, and office maintenance expenses for the India Office.
2. **Guaranteed Railway Interest**: Under the Old Guarantee System (1849–1869), private British railway companies were guaranteed a 5% return on capital investment backed by Indian revenues. This eliminated efficiency incentives and caused state losses exceeding £50 million by 1900.
3. **Council Bills Mechanism**: The Secretary of State sold Council Bills in London against silver rupees in Indian treasuries. British merchants bought Indian raw goods using these bills, extracting Indian agricultural exports through internal tax collections.

```
Total Economic Drain = Home Charges + Guaranteed Interest Remittances + Merchant Savings Transfer + Unrequited Export Surplus
```

Historical estimates by Naoroji placed the annual outflow at £20 million to £30 million during the late nineteenth century. R.C. Dutt calculated that nearly one-half of the net annual revenues of India were remitted abroad annually.

#### UPSC Edge Cases and Historiographical Debates

- **Subsidiary Alliance Sequence**: Marquess Wellesley introduced the subsidiary treaty framework in 1798. Memorize the chronological order of signing:
  1. Hyderabad (1798)
  2. Mysore (1799, post-Fourth Anglo-Mysore War)
  3. Tanjore (October 1799)
  4. Awadh (November 1801)
  5. Peshwa Baji Rao II (Treaty of Bassein, 1802)
  6. Bhonsle (1803)
  7. Scindia (1804)
- **Doctrine of Lapse Sequence**: Lord Dalhousie annexed states under Hindu succession custom restrictions:
  1. Satara (1848)
  2. Jaitpur and Sambalpur (1849)
  3. Baghat (1850)
  4. Udaipur (1852)
  5. Jhansi (1853)
  6. Nagpur (1854)
  *(Note: Awadh was annexed in 1856 on charges of misgovernance, not Doctrine of Lapse).*
- **1857 Revolt Nature**: British historians (Charles Ball, J.W. Kaye) classified it as a "Sepoy Mutiny." Nationalist historians (V.D. Savarkar) designated it the "First War of Indian Independence." Marxist historians (Irving, Sen) analyzed it as an agrarian peasant uprising spearheaded by dispossessed talukdars.

#### Worked UPSC Mains Model Answer

**Question (UPSC CSE GS1 Mains):**  
*"Examine how the land revenue experiments of the British East India Company dismantled traditional agrarian structures and precipitated widespread rural revolts in the nineteenth century." (250 words, 15 marks)*

**Model Answer Scaffold:**

**1. Introduction (30–35 words):**  
The British East India Company transformed traditional Indian land tenure from customary usufructuary rights into commercialized private property to extract maximum fiscal surpluses, disrupting the village moral economy.

**2. Institutional Mechanisms of Agrarian Disruption (100–110 words):**  
- **Commoditization of Land**: The Permanent Settlement (1793) and Ryotwari systems turned land into saleable, mortgageable real estate. Failure to pay revenue led to immediate land auctions under the Sunset Laws.
- **De-linking of Revenue from Yield**: Revenue was assessed on territorial acreage rather than seasonal harvest volumes, exposing cultivators to severe weather shocks.
- **Rise of Parasitic Intermediaries**: In Bengal, multiple layers of sub-infeudation (*Patnidari*) arose. In the Deccan and South India, cultivators mortgaged their land to village moneylenders (*Sawkars*) to meet rigid cash demands.
- **De-industrialization Feedback**: Destruction of indigenous urban handicrafts forced millions of unemployed artisans onto agricultural land, depressing agrarian wages.

**3. Catalyst for Rural Insurgency (70–80 words):**  
- **Santhal Hool (1855–56)**: Exploitation by non-local merchants (*Dikus*) and revenue officials under the Permanent Settlement triggered an armed revolt led by Sidhu and Kanhu Murmu.
- **Deccan Riots (1875)**: Ryotwari revenue enhancements in Pune and Ahmednagar caused systemic peasant debt, culminating in targeted attacks on moneylenders\x27 debt bonds.
- **Pabna Agrarian League (1873)**: Bengal tenant resistance mobilized legal instruments against illegal cesses (*Abwabs*) and rent enhancements.

**4. Conclusion (30–35 words):**  
Colonial fiscal policies converted self-sustaining village communities into indebted agrarian producers, establishing structural rural poverty and converting peasant grievances into the bedrock of the national liberation movement.
