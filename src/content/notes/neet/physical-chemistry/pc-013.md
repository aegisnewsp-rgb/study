# Colloidal

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Colloidal particles sit in the **1 nm to 1000 nm** size window — bigger than true solutions, small enough to dodge gravity and slip through filter paper. They scatter light (Tyndall effect), jiggle endlessly (Brownian motion), and carry a uniform surface charge that keeps them apart.

| Property | Value | SI Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Particle diameter range | 1 × 10⁻⁹ m < d < 1 × 10⁻⁶ m | m |
| Tyndall scattering wavelength window | comparable to visible light (~400–700 nm) | m |
| Gold number unit | mg (of protective colloid) | mg |
| Coagulation value unit | mmol L⁻¹ | mmol·m⁻³ convertible |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** "**DDB-BBC**" — **D**ispersed phase, **D**ispersion medium, **B**rownian motion, **B**rownian = **B**ounces, **C**oagulation, **C**harge. Charge on colloidal particles is the same sign → mutual repulsion → stability.

Two exam essentials: (1) Tyndall effect distinguishes colloids from true solutions, and (2) **Hardy-Schulze** says coagulating power rises sharply with the **valency of the counter-ion** (Al³⁺ beats Na⁺).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Classification of Colloidal Systems

Colloids divide first by the **physical state of dispersed phase and dispersion medium**. Eight combinations exist; the textbook taxonomy from NCERT Class 12 Chapter 5 lists sol (solid in liquid), gel (liquid in solid), emulsion (liquid in liquid), foam (gas in liquid/solid), aerosol (liquid/solid in gas), and solid sol (solid in solid). A second cut sorts them by solvent interaction: **lyophilic** (solvent-loving, e.g., gum, starch, proteins — reversible, stable) versus **lyophobic** (solvent-hating, e.g., metal sulphides, hydroxides — irreversible, need stabilisers).

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Feature | Lyophilic Colloid | Lyophobic Colloid |
|---|---|---|
| Solvent affinity | Strong; often self-stabilising | Weak; needs stabilising electrolyte |
| Reversibility | Reversible (evaporate → redissolve) | Irreversible |
| Viscosity | Higher than solvent | Nearly equal to solvent |
| Coagulation ease | Requires large electrolyte amount | Coagulates with traces of electrolyte |
| Examples | Gum, starch, gelatin, protein | As₂S₃, Fe(OH)₃, metal sols |
| Charge source | Preferentially adsorbed ions | Ions from dispersion medium |

| Type | Dispersed Phase | Dispersion Medium | Familiar Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sol | Solid | Liquid | Cell fluids, paint |
| Gel | Liquid | Solid | Cheese, jelly |
| Emulsion | Liquid | Liquid | Milk (O/W), butter (W/O) |
| Foam | Gas | Liquid / Solid | Soap lather, foam rubber |
| Aerosol | Solid / Liquid | Gas | Smoke, fog, mist |

#### Tyndall Effect and Brownian Motion

When a beam of light passes through a colloid, particles scatter the photons sideways — that visible **conical light path** is the Tyndall effect. True solutions do not show it because solute molecules are far smaller than the wavelength of visible light.

Brownian motion is the irregular zigzag path colloidal particles trace under the microscope, driven by unbalanced collisions with molecules of the dispersion medium. Two practical consequences: it counteracts gravity (slows sedimentation) and proves the kinetic-molecular picture of matter.

#### Worked Numerical: Hardy-Schulze Valency Ratio

A colloidal sol is coagulated by adding either NaCl or AlCl₃. If the coagulating concentration of NaCl is 50 mmol L⁻¹, predict the coagulating concentration of AlCl₃ using the Hardy-Schulze rule.

**Setup.** Hardy-Schulze states coagulating power ∝ z^n where z is the valency of the **counter-ion** (the ion whose sign is opposite to the charge on the colloidal particles, here the cation if the sol is negatively charged). The chloride anion is the common ion, so the effective counter-ion is Na⁺ (z = 1) vs Al³⁺ (z = 3).

**Step 1.** Coagulating power ratio:
P(Al³⁺) / P(Na⁺) = 3¹ / 1¹ (empirical proportionality for typical sols)

**Step 2.** Coagulating power is **inversely proportional** to coagulation concentration c:
P ∝ 1/c → c(AlCl₃) / c(NaCl) = P(Na⁺) / P(Al³⁺) = 1/3

**Step 3.** Compute:
c(AlCl₃) = (1/3) × 50 mmol L⁻¹ ≈ 16.7 mmol L⁻¹

**Final answer.** AlCl₃ coagulates the sol at roughly 16.7 mmol L⁻¹ — about a third the concentration of NaCl.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students invert the rule. They write "higher valency = lower coagulating power". It is the opposite. Also, Na⁺ and Al³⁺ coagulate a **negatively charged** sol; for a positively charged sol, the anions (Cl⁻ vs the same Cl⁻) would matter, and AlCl₃'s cation does nothing. Always identify the counter-ion first.

#### Coagulation, Flocculation and Protective Colloids

Adding an electrolyte neutralises the surface charge. The **minimum concentration (mmol L⁻¹) required to cause coagulation** is the **coagulation value** (also called flocculation value). Lower coagulation value ⇒ higher coagulating power.

Adding a lyophilic colloid (gelatin, starch) shields lyophobic particles and raises their coagulation value. **Gold number** quantifies this protection: the **mass of protective colloid (in mg) that just prevents coagulation of 10 mL of a standard gold sol by 1 mL of 10% NaCl**. A **smaller** gold number = **better** protective action.

| Quantity | Symbol | Trend |
|---|---|---|
| Coagulation value | c (mmol L⁻¹) | Inversely proportional to coagulating power |
| Coagulating power | P | ∝ z of counter-ion |
| Gold number | G (mg) | Inversely proportional to protective power |

#### Purification Methods

Ordinary filter paper lets colloidal particles through. Three specialised routes clean them:

1. **Dialysis** — a semipermeable membrane (cellophane) lets ions and small molecules diffuse out, retaining colloidal particles.
2. **Electrodialysis** — speeds up ion removal using electrodes.
3. **Ultrafiltration** — uses a special graded filter (collodion membrane) under pressure.

#### Emulsions and Bancroft Rule

An emulsion is a liquid-in-liquid colloid stabilised by an **emulsifier** (soap, detergent, protein). Bancroft's rule: the **phase in which the emulsifier is more soluble becomes the dispersion medium**. Milk (oil-in-water, O/W) — soap is water-soluble. Butter (water-in-oil, W/O) — the emulsifier is fat-soluble.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Where Colloids Sit in the NEET Paper

Expect one MCQ (1 mark) most years, occasionally two, on this topic — roughly 2% weightage. The most-tested sub-areas, in descending frequency: classification examples, Tyndall effect statement, Hardy-Schulze valency direction, lyophilic vs lyophobic difference, emulsion type identification, and the gold-number/coagulation-value inverse logic. Assertion-Reason sets are common, with the reason typically testing whether you remember that **Brownian motion stabilises** rather than destabilises.

#### Langmuir Adsorption Isotherm — Connecting to Surface Chemistry

Many colloidal phenomena reduce to adsorption at the interface. The Langmuir isotherm for a monolayer reads:

$$\frac{x}{m} = \frac{kP}{1 + kP}$$

| Symbol | Quantity | SI Unit | Dimensions |
|---|---|---|---|
| x | Mass of gas adsorbed | kg | [M] |
| m | Mass of adsorbent | kg | [M] |
| P | Equilibrium pressure of gas | Pa | [M·L⁻¹·T⁻²] |
| k | Adsorption equilibrium constant | Pa⁻¹ | [M⁻¹·L·T²] |
| x/m | Extent of adsorption (ratio) | — | dimensionless |

Two limiting behaviours: at very low P, x/m ≈ kP (linear regime); at very high P, x/m → 1 (full monolayer). The constant k depends on temperature and the bond strength between adsorbate and surface — a direct connection to catalytic activity of colloidal Pt, Pd, Ni (high surface area per unit mass).

#### Zeta Potential and Electrophoresis

Colloidal particles acquire a surface charge; counter-ions form a diffuse layer around them. The potential at the **slipping plane** (the boundary between the bound layer and the bulk) is the **zeta potential ζ (V)**. Helmholtz-Smoluchowski gives:

$$\zeta = \frac{4\pi \eta v}{\varepsilon E}$$

| Symbol | Quantity | SI Unit | Dimensions |
|---|---|---|---|
| ζ | Zeta potential | V | [M·L²·T⁻³·A⁻¹] |
| η | Viscosity of medium | Pa·s | [M·L⁻¹·T⁻¹] |
| v | Particle velocity | m·s⁻¹ | [L·T⁻¹] |
| ε | Permittivity of medium | F·m⁻¹ | [M⁻¹·L⁻³·T⁴·A²] |
| E | Electric field strength | V·m⁻¹ | [M·L·T⁻³·A⁻¹] |

A **higher |ζ|** (typically > 30 mV in practice) means stronger mutual repulsion and a more stable colloid; near zero, coagulation is imminent. Electrophoresis (migration of particles towards an electrode of opposite sign) confirms the sign of the charge — Fe(OH)₃ sol moves to the cathode (positive); As₂S₃ moves to the anode (negative).

#### Traube's Rule — Surface Tension in Colloidal Solutions

For homologous fatty acids (RCOOH), surface tension decreases logarithmically with concentration:

$$\gamma = \gamma_0 - a \ln(1 + bC)$$

where γ, γ₀ have SI unit N·m⁻¹ and dimensions [M·T⁻²]; C is in mol·m⁻³. Each additional –CH₂– group multiplies the molar surface activity by roughly three (≈ 3 kJ·mol⁻¹ increment per CH₂), which is why longer-chain soaps are stronger detergents and better emulsifiers.

#### Edge Cases and Frequent Confusions

1. **Aerosol confusion.** Aerosols have a **gaseous dispersion medium**. Smoke = solid in gas; fog = liquid in gas. Don't classify them as gases — they are colloids.
2. **Tyndall vs reflection.** Reflection happens at any surface; Tyndall scattering specifically requires particles in the **1–1000 nm** size window relative to visible-light wavelength.
3. **Gold number inversion.** Many students write "high gold number means strong protection." It is the inverse: gelatin has a gold number of about 0.005 mg (extremely good); starch sits near 25 mg (weak). The **smaller** the gold number, the **more powerful** the protective colloid.
4. **Hardy-Schulze sign pitfall.** Coagulating power depends on the valency of the **ion opposite in sign** to the colloidal particle, not the electrolyte as a whole. AlCl₃ works on negative sols because Al³⁺ is the counter-ion; on positive sols, AlCl₃ is essentially inert compared to, say, Na₃PO₄.
5. **Brownian motion ≠ coagulation cause.** Brownian motion **delays** sedimentation; coagulation is triggered by charge neutralisation, not by motion.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. A gold sol coagulates at 25 mmol L⁻¹ of Na₂SO₄ and at 6 mmol L⁻¹ of Na₃PO₄. The colloidal particles carry what sign of charge? Justify using the Hardy-Schulze relationship, and estimate the relative coagulating power of PO₄³⁻ versus SO₄²⁻.

2. A protective colloid X has gold number 0.01 mg and another Y has gold number 5 mg. (a) Which protects gold sol better? (b) If you add 0.5 mg of X to 10 mL of gold sol and challenge with 1 mL of 10% NaCl, will coagulation occur? Reason in terms of gold-number definition.
