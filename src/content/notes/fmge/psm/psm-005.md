# Sociology, Psychology, and Communication in Health

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your FMGE PSM paper.

**Sociology, Psychology, and Communication in Health** is the behavioural-science module of PSM that links social structure, family, and human behaviour to disease patterns and to the doctor–patient encounter. Talcott Parsons' **sick role** exempts the patient from normal duties, conditional on seeking help and cooperating with the physician. **PQLI** = (literacy rate + life expectancy at birth + infant mortality rate/1000) ÷ 3; **HDI** is the geometric mean of normalised indices of life expectancy, education, and GDP per capita (PPP $).

| Concept | Key fact to remember |
|---|---|
| Szasz–Hollender model | Active-Passive (coma/anaesthesia), Guidance-Cooperation (acute illness), Mutual Participation (chronic/preventive care) |
| Pain history mnemonic | **PQRST** — Provocation, Quality, Radiation, Severity, Timing |
| Health Belief Model | Susceptibility, Severity, Benefits, Barriers, Cues, Self-efficacy |

**Exam pointers:** (1) Match each social problem (prostitution, addiction, suicide) with its specific public-health consequence. (2) Distinguish KAP *survey* from health-education *intervention*. (3) Patient-centred interviewing uses open-ended questions, paraphrasing, and summarising — never directive jargon.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Social structure and the sick role
**Social stratification** by class, occupation, education and culture shapes both disease risk and access to care. Parsons' **sick role** legitimises temporary exemption from work and social duties on four conditions: the patient is not held responsible for the illness, is exempt from normal roles, must try to get well, and must seek technically competent help and cooperate with the physician.

#### Family and socialisation
The **family** acts as a primary social institution and disease-transmission unit. Three structural types matter: **nuclear** (parents + unmarried children), **joint** (multiple generations under one roof with shared property), and **extended** (kin network beyond the household). **Primary socialisation** occurs within the family in the first years of life; **secondary socialisation** continues through school, peers and workplace; **anticipatory socialisation** rehearses future roles (e.g., a medical intern adopting the physician role).

#### Social problems with health consequences
Each social problem has a specific morbidity signature that FMGE questions test:

| Social problem | Specific health consequence to memorise |
|---|---|
| Prostitution | STIs, HIV/AIDS, cervical cancer, infertility |
| Alcohol/drug addiction | Hepatitis B & C, road-traffic trauma, foetal alcohol syndrome |
| Poverty | Undernutrition, tuberculosis, reduced immunisation coverage |
| Suicide attempt | Underlying depression, pesticide poisoning (agricultural areas) |
| Delinquency / crime | Violence-related injuries, substance abuse |

#### Health education and IEC
**Health education** is a planned learning experience that influences health behaviour, not mere information transfer. Principles: capture **interest**, ensure **participation**, move from **known to unknown**, use **learning by doing**, give **reinforcement**, maintain **good human relations**, and reinforce through **home visits** and supervision. Methods are classified as **individual** (most effective — counselling, home visit), **group** (lecture, group discussion, role play), and **mass** (least effective per person — posters, mass media).

> **Trap to avoid:** KAP (**Knowledge–Attitude–Practice**) is a *survey method* used to assess a community; it is not itself a teaching method.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Szasz–Hollender doctor–patient models
Szasz and Hollender (1956) described three idealised models that the NBE frequently tests in vignettes:

| Model | Patient role | Physician role | Clinical setting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Active-Passive | Recipient, unable to participate | Does something *to* the patient | Coma, general anaesthesia, severe trauma |
| Guidance-Cooperation | Coherent but dependent — accepts advice | Tells patient what to do | Acute infection, routine surgery |
| Mutual Participation | Equal partner in management | Helps patient help themselves | Chronic disease, psychosomatic illness, preventive counselling |

#### Communication: process, barriers, skills
**Communication** is a two-way process with six elements: **sender, message, channel, receiver, feedback,** and **barriers**. Barriers fall into four groups — **physical** (noise, distance), **psychological** (prejudice, fear), **semantic** (jargon, language mismatch), and **physiological** (deafness, dysphasia). Core clinical skills include **active listening, paraphrasing, reflection, summarisation, silence, empathy, and confrontation** used judiciously.

> **Exam tip:** Use **PQRST** for pain history — Provocation/Palliation, Quality, Radiation, Severity (0–10), Timing/Trend — and avoid closed or leading questions.

#### Behaviour-change models
- **Health Belief Model:** perceived **susceptibility**, **severity**, **benefits**, **barriers**, **cues to action**, and **self-efficacy** predict health behaviour.
- **Transtheoretical (Stages of Change):** precontemplation → contemplation → preparation → action → maintenance.
- **Theory of Reasoned Action:** **attitude** + **subjective norm** → **intention** → behaviour.

#### Community diagnosis vs clinical diagnosis
**Community diagnosis** is a *qualitative* assessment of a defined population's health needs, problems and resources using epidemiological, demographic and sociocultural tools (e.g., spot map, focus-group discussion, KAP survey). It differs from clinical diagnosis, which assesses an individual patient's signs and symptoms to identify a disease.

#### Practice prompts
1. A 45-year-old insulin-dependent diabetic on twice-daily insulin is counselled about sick-day rules and self-titration of doses. Which Szasz–Hollender model best describes this encounter, and why is the *Mutual Participation* model preferable here?
2. In a rural PHC, a health worker lists "illiteracy, alcohol use, open defecation and consanguineous marriage" as community problems. Convert this into a proper community diagnosis by linking each problem to its specific disease outcome (e.g., consanguinity → autosomal-recessive disorders).
