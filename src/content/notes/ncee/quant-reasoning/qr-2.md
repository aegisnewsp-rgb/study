# Odd One Out (Numbers)

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your NCEE paper.

Odd One Out (Numbers) asks you to pick the single number in a set of four or five options that **breaks a shared mathematical rule** held by the rest. You are selecting an exception, not continuing a series. Speed matters: NCEE Quantitative Reasoning carries 4% weight, and classification items in most keys appear 1–2 times.

The fastest checks, in order, are:

- **Parity** — is every other number even (or odd)?
- **Divisibility** — by 3 (digit sum), 5 (last digit 0/5), 11 (alternating digit sum), or 9.
- **Prime vs composite** — count primes; if three are primes and one is composite, that composite is the odd one.
- **Perfect square or cube** — check whether N equals k² or k³ for some integer k.

> Tip: scan for the rule shared by 3–4 numbers first; the leftover option is your answer.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months before the exam.

#### What the question actually tests
NCEE "Odd One Out (Numbers)" belongs to **classification reasoning**. The stem gives you numbers like 49, 64, 81, 100, 121 and asks which one does not belong. Three to four numbers obey one rule; exactly one violates it. Your job is to name the rule, then expose the violator.

#### Core divisibility shortcuts
Most NCEE items collapse within 10 seconds if you know these tests cold:

| Test | Rule for N |
| --- | --- |
| Divisible by 2 | Last digit ∈ {0, 2, 4, 6, 8} |
| Divisible by 3 | Sum of digits divisible by 3 |
| Divisible by 5 | Last digit ∈ {0, 5} |
| Divisible by 9 | Sum of digits divisible by 9 |
| Divisible by 11 | Alternating digit sum divisible by 11 |

#### Worked logic
Take the set {17, 19, 23, 27, 29}. Three numbers (17, 19, 23, 29) are prime. The digit sum of 27 is 2 + 7 = 9, divisible by 9, so 27 is divisible by 9 and therefore composite. **27 is the odd one out** — it fails the prime rule.

#### Standard problem patterns
- All but one are **perfect squares** (4, 9, 16, 25, 36, …). The exception can be a near-miss like 48.
- Three are **multiples of a hidden base** (e.g., 12, 18, 24, 30 — all multiples of 6), and one breaks the chain.
- Numbers written as **products** that simplify (e.g., 4×4, 5×5, 6×6 vs 7×8) — here 7×8 = 56 is not a perfect square while the rest are.

> Trap: do not pick an option just because it "looks different". Verify a true mathematical property before answering.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Digit-sum and digital-root shortcuts
Repeated digit-sum (the **digital root**) collapses any number to a single digit modulo 9. For 738: 7 + 3 + 8 = 18 → 1 + 8 = 9. So 738 is divisible by 9. This is faster than long division across 3-digit numbers.

#### Edge cases NCEE setters exploit
- **Consecutive products** (n(n+1)): numbers like 6×7 = 42, 7×8 = 56, 8×9 = 72 are never perfect squares — a question can mix these with true squares (36, 49, 64) to test whether you recall that n(n+1) has no integer square root for n ≥ 1.
- **Numbers in words or Roman numerals**: 14, XV, 16 — three are even, XV is odd. Convert all to one form before classifying.
- **Reversed digits**: 19 vs 91 — only one can share a property like digit sum or prime status with the others.
- **Mixed bases disguised**: 100 (decimal) vs 64, 81, 144 — three are squares (8², 9², 12²); 100 = 10² is also a square, so a setter hides the odd one as 96 = 16 × 6.

#### Common mistakes
- Treating the task as "next in series" and trying to extend the pattern.
- Stopping after one test (only parity) when a deeper rule (prime, square, digit-sum) is the real classifier.
- Assuming the prime is always the exception — sometimes the three primes share a property (e.g., all end in 7) and the composite is the odd one.

#### Practice prompts
1. From {121, 144, 169, 200, 196}, which number is the odd one out? (Answer logic: 121 = 11², 144 = 12², 169 = 13², 196 = 14²; 200 is not a perfect square.)
2. From {35, 49, 77, 91, 121}, identify the exception and name the property you used. (Hint: check digit-sum divisibility by 7.)

> Exam strategy: budget 30–45 seconds per odd-one-out item. Read all options once, pick the most restrictive rule, and confirm with one quick test.
