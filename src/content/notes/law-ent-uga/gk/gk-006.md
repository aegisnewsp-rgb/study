# Human Rights in Uganda

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Human rights in Uganda are the fundamental rights and freedoms guaranteed by **Chapter Four (Articles 20–75)** of the **1995 Constitution**, supplemented by ratified treaties and statutes such as the **Human Rights Enforcement Act, 2019**. Non-derogable rights under **Article 44** — life, freedom from torture, freedom from slavery, fair hearing, and the prohibition on retroactive criminal liability — can never be limited, even during a state of emergency declared under **Article 110**.

- **Foundational instruments:** 1995 Constitution (Chapter Four), UDHR 1948, ICCPR 1966, ICESCR 1966, African Charter (Banjul) 1981, Maputo Protocol 2003.
- **Enforcement bodies:** Uganda Human Rights Commission (UHRC, Articles 51–55), Human Rights Tribunal (2019 Act), High Court, Constitutional Court (Article 137).
- **Limitation test (Article 43(2)):** any limit must be *prescribed by law*, *necessary*, *proportionate*, *non-discriminatory*, and *consistent with international obligations*.
- **Environmental rights:** sit in **Article 28A**, not Article 28 — the 1998 amendment inserted them after Article 28 (fair hearing).

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"UHRC Quits-Criminal-Cases, Courts Convict"** — the **UHRC** investigates and awards compensation (quasi-judicial), but only **courts** try criminal human-rights violations. Pair this with **"44 is Forever"** — the five Article 44 rights are *non-derogable*, full stop.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Architecture of Chapter Four

Chapter Four groups 16 substantive rights into clusters: general provisions (Arts 20–21), personal protections (Arts 22–27: life, liberty, dignity, slavery, property, privacy), fair hearing (Art. 28), environmental rights (Art. 28A, 1998 amendment), freedoms (Arts 29, 29A, 29B: conscience, expression, assembly/association), women's rights (Art. 32), children's rights (Art. 34), disability rights (Art. 32(2)), cultural and civic rights (Art. 36 onwards), and duties of citizens (Arts 17–17A).

#### The Non-Derogability Rule

Article 44(1) lists the irreducible core: rights to life, freedom from torture or inhuman treatment, freedom from slavery or servitude, the right to a fair hearing, and protection against conviction for acts that were not offences when committed. These five survive any emergency. Article 45 reinforces non-retrogression by outlawing any constitutional amendment that removes them.

#### Comparison Matrix — Easily Confused Concepts

| Feature | UHRC (Art. 51) | Human Rights Tribunal (2019 Act) | Constitutional Court (Art. 137) | High Court |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Established by | Constitution | Statute | Constitution | Constitution |
| Core mandate | Investigate violations; quasi-judicial orders | Civil claims for human rights breaches | Sole interpreter of the Constitution | Habeas Corpus, Judicial Review, statutory petitions |
| Powers | Subpoena, award compensation, recommend prosecution | Award damages, declaratory relief | Strike down legislation, interpret Constitution | Issue prerogative writs, hear constitutional petitions under Art. 50 |
| Criminal jurisdiction | None — recommends; courts try | None | Limited (contempt) | Yes, via subordinate courts on referral |
| Locus standi | Broad (any aggrieved person) | Broad under s.5, 2019 Act | Broad for constitutional matters | Article 50 liberalises standing |

#### Limitations Test (Article 43(2)) — Step-by-Step Application

1. Identify the right claimed and the specific clause.
2. Identify the law or act limiting it.
3. Confirm the law is **prescribed** (statute, not custom or directive alone).
4. Test **necessity** — is the limitation required by a compelling public interest?
5. Test **proportionality** — is the limit no greater than needed?
6. Confirm the limit is **non-discriminatory** and **consistent with international obligations**.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** A state of emergency is declared in Uganda following civil unrest. The Minister of Internal Affairs issues a directive suspending freedom of movement and ordering indefinite holding of suspected agitators without trial. **Advise** whether the directive is consistent with the 1995 Constitution, citing the relevant articles.

#### Solution:
- **Step 1 — Identify the source of emergency powers:** Article 110 empowers the President (with Cabinet approval) to declare a state of emergency by statutory instrument; the Constitutional Court must confirm it within 21 days or it lapses.
- **Step 2 — Apply the derogation rule:** Article 43 permits limitation of rights during an emergency only to the extent *strictly required*; Article 44 lists rights that are *non-derogable*.
- **Step 3 — Test each measure against Article 44:** indefinite detention without trial violates the **right to a fair hearing (Art. 28)** — a non-derogable right — because Article 44(1)(d) protects it absolutely. Suspension of movement is not on the non-derogable list, so it may be limited only if the limitation is strictly necessary and proportionate under Article 43(2).
- **Step 4 — Conclusion:** The directive is **unconstitutional** to the extent it orders detention without trial (breaches Articles 28 and 44). Suspension of movement may be permissible only if shown strictly necessary, proportionate, non-discriminatory, and consistent with Uganda's ICCPR obligations (Art. 43(2)).
- **Step 5 — Remedies:** Detainees may apply for **habeas corpus** in the High Court (Article 23) and file a constitutional petition under **Article 50**, since standing is liberalised for rights claims.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students frequently cite *only* Article 43 and forget Article 44. The correct answer must explicitly name the non-derogable core — indefinite detention without trial is *always* unconstitutional, regardless of how severe the emergency is described in the question.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Boundary Conditions

- **Progressive realisation of ESC rights:** Article 20(2) recognises that economic, social and cultural rights (health, education, water, housing) are subject to progressive realisation based on available resources — this matches ICESCR Article 2(1) language, but the Constitutional Court has read a **minimum core obligation** into certain rights in *Centre for Health, Human Rights and Development v. Attorney General*.
- **Hate speech vs. freedom of expression:** Article 29(2) expressly permits restrictions on hate speech, mirroring ICCPR Article 20 — examine the *necessary and proportionate* test under Article 43(2) before concluding a regulation is valid.
- **Property rights under Article 26:** Government compulsory acquisition requires prompt, fair compensation; *Uganda Land Alliance v. Attorney General* clarified that compensation must be *just*, not merely nominal.
- **Article 50 standing:** The Constitution and Section 5 of the 2019 Act allow any person acting in good faith to file a human rights petition without proving personal injury — students frequently miss this and incorrectly apply ordinary civil procedure.
- **International law as interpretive aid:** Article 45 and the Constitutional Court's jurisprudence in *Khawam v. Attorney General* require courts to interpret Bill of Rights clauses consistently with ratified international instruments.
- **Death penalty moratorium:** Although Article 22 protects the right to life, the Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of the death penalty in *Kigula & Others v. Attorney General* (2019), but Parliament has declined to authorise executions — a de facto moratorium.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. **Application:** A non-governmental organisation wishes to challenge a directive requiring all foreign-funded NGOs to renew operational certificates annually. Which constitutional articles and international instruments would you invoke, and which forum is most appropriate — the UHRC, the Human Rights Tribunal, or the Constitutional Court? Justify your choice with reference to standing, remedies and precedent.
2. **Comparison:** Distinguish the mandates of the **African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights** (Banjul) and the **African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights** (Arusha), explaining how a Ugandan complainant can access each, the nature of remedies available, and Uganda's specific obligations under the 2010 declaration accepting direct individual access to the Court.

#### Common Traps for Advanced Candidates

| Trap | Why it costs marks | Correct framing |
|---|---|---|
| Citing Article 28 for the environment | Article 28 is fair hearing; environmental rights sit in Article 28A (1998 amendment) | Always write "Article 28A" for environmental claims |
| Treating the UHRC as a criminal court | UHRC has no criminal jurisdiction | UHRC investigates, recommends, awards ex gratia compensation |
| Assuming all rights are derogable | Article 44 lists five non-derogable rights | Memorise the Article 44 list verbatim |
| Conflating the Banjul Commission and Arusha Court | Two distinct bodies with different procedures | Commission = quasi-judicial, promotional; Court = binding judgments |
| Ignoring Article 45's non-retrogression rule | Even Parliament cannot amend away Article 44 rights | Frame amendments in light of Article 45's entrenchment clause |

> 📌 **Formula Check:** Although this topic is doctrinal rather than quantitative, the **limitations test (Article 43(2))** operates as a four-part proportionality equation: *Legality + Necessity + Proportionality + Non-discrimination + International-consistency = 1*. Each limb must be satisfied; failure on any one invalidates the limitation.
