The Situational Judgement subtest reports its result as one of four bands. The published band descriptions from the UCAT Consortium tell you what each band means about your judgement relative to a panel of senior doctors and admissions tutors. Knowing the bands shapes how you study: the difference between a band 2 and a band 3 candidate is calibration, not luck. This note covers the banding system, the partial-mark scoring that supports it, and the six reasoning traps that move candidates down a band.

Subtest anchor: 69 questions in 26 minutes, after a one-and-a-half-minute instruction section, with one mark awarded for matching the published ideal response and partial marks for a "close to ideal" response. That shape and that scoring rule are on the UCAT Consortium test-format page and they are the facts you may rely on. Anything about cohort averages, year-on-year distributions, or per-university cut-offs should be verified on ucat.ac.uk before each admissions cycle.

---

### Quick revision

- **Bands 1 to 4, with 1 the highest.** The Consortium publishes the band descriptors; the order matters.
- **Partial marks make the band.** A response close to ideal scores more than a wild guess. That is where band 2 separates from band 3.
- **Six reasoning traps are responsible for most band drops.** This note names each one and shows the wrong move and the right move.
- **Calibrate, do not over-commit.** Confidence and correctness are not the same.

### Standard conceptual deep dive

#### The published band descriptors

The four bands, with the published interpretations:

- **Band 1 — Excellent.** Your judgement matched the panel of experts in most cases. The published description: "demonstrated an excellent level of performance, showing similar judgement in most cases to the panel of experts."
- **Band 2 — Good, solid.** Your judgement was appropriate frequently and many responses matched model answers. The published description: "demonstrated a good, solid level of performance, showing appropriate judgement frequently, with many responses matching model answers."
- **Band 3 — Modest.** Your judgement was appropriate for some questions and substantial differences appeared for others. The published description: "demonstrated a modest level of performance, with appropriate judgement shown for some questions and substantial differences from ideal responses for others."
- **Band 4 — Low.** Your judgement differed substantially from the ideal responses in many cases. The published description: "the performance of those in Band 4 was low, with judgement tending to differ substantially from ideal responses in many cases."

The key tells from these descriptors:

- "Substantial differences from ideal" is the band 3 to band 4 boundary. Substantial means several items in a row, not one slip.
- "Many responses matching model answers" is the band 2 criterion. Many is the word — it is not "most."
- "Similar judgement in most cases" is the band 1 criterion. Most means the majority.

#### What partial marks do

The scoring rule on the Consortium pages says: full marks for matching the correct response, partial marks for being close to it. Close-to-ideal means the same intent with a different route — a senior clinician would describe both as recommending the same approach.

Partial marks matter most at the bands-2-versus-3 boundary. A candidate who is close-to-ideal on half the items and off on the rest lands in band 2. A candidate who is close-to-ideal on a quarter and off on the rest lands in band 3. The difference is not knowledge; it is calibration.

#### The six reasoning traps

These traps are in order of how much they move a candidate's band. Each is described with the wrong reflex, the right anchor, and an example.

**Trap 1 — Personal moral reflex.** The instinct is to rate a response on what you would do, not on what the professional panel would do. The right anchor is the GMC's Good Medical Practice duties and the ABPI code where applicable.

```
  Scenario: A junior doctor is asked by a patient's relative to
  hide a diagnosis from the patient because the family believes
  the diagnosis would distress the patient.

  Response: "Comply with the family's request and avoid telling
  the patient for now."

    Wrong reflex: rating this as appropriate because the family
    request is sympathetic.
    Right anchor: honesty with the patient and respect for patient
    autonomy are GMC duties. The relative's wishes are a
    consideration, not the deciding factor.

    Rating: Very inappropriate (D).
```

**Trap 2 — Group loyalty over patient outcome.** A reflex to protect colleagues, to defer to seniority, or to avoid escalation when patient safety or dignity is at risk.

```
  Scenario: A fellow foundation-year doctor falsifies a patient's
  drug chart to cover a missed dose.

  Response: "Say nothing because reporting would end their career."

    Wrong reflex: rating this as inappropriate-but-not-awful because
    loyalty is admirable.
    Right anchor: patient safety is the highest duty. Loyalty does
    not extend to covering harm.

    Rating: Very inappropriate (D).
```

The trap is the loyalty itself. The right response is to escalate to a senior or to the relevant trust process, not to confront the colleague directly. Both are appropriate; silence is not.

**Trap 3 — Confusing importance with relatedness.** A factor is mentioned in the scenario, so it gets rated as important. Related is not the same as weighty.

```
  Scenario: A patient on a clinical trial has a serious adverse
  event in the early hours. The trial protocol requires reporting
  within 24 hours.

  Consideration: "The current workload of the on-call team."

    Wrong reflex: rating this as important because it is mentioned.
    Right anchor: it is related to whether the team can act, not
    to whether the report is due. Workload is a real constraint
    but not the deciding factor.

    Rating: Of minor importance.
```

**Trap 4 — Authoritative tone bias.** Longer, more decisive-sounding options are read as more appropriate than shorter, less assertive ones. The panel scores outcomes, not tone.

```
  Scenario: A nurse asks a junior doctor to clarify a prescription.

  Response A: "Take the prescription to the senior doctor and have
  them explain it."

  Response B: "Read the prescription back to the nurse and ask her
  to confirm her concern."

    Wrong reflex: rating A as more appropriate because it is more
    decisive.
    Right anchor: the patient gets a correct prescription either
    way. The simpler step (B) is closer to ideal because it
    clarifies without escalation, and the senior-doctor step (A)
    is appropriate but not ideal.

    Ratings: A is B (Appropriate, but not ideal). B is A (Very
    appropriate).
```

**Trap 5 — Anchoring on the first response.** A subtest cluster presents four responses. Rating response 1 at "Very appropriate" makes responses 2, 3, and 4 feel less appropriate by comparison. Read all four before rating any.

Practical rule: read all four options twice. The first read picks up content; the second read picks up relative differences. Anchor on the absolute scale (what is the right answer in isolation), then calibrate the relative differences.

**Trap 6 — Conflating partial correctness with full correctness.** "Right answer, wrong reason" still scores in UCAT SJT. The scoring shape — full marks for matching ideal, partial marks for close — rewards agreement with the panel, not agreement with your reasoning.

```
  Scenario: A patient asks the junior doctor for a prognosis they
  are not qualified to give.

  Response A: "Explain what is known about the average prognosis
  in similar cases, name the limits of your certainty, and
  recommend the consultant for specific numbers."

    Ideal: A (Very appropriate).

  Response B: "Recommend the consultant and decline to share what
  you know."

    Wrong reflex: rating B as appropriate because it is honest.
    Right anchor: B is honest but loses an opportunity to inform.
    It is appropriate but not ideal.

    Ratings: A is A. B is B.
```

A and B would both be marked with some credit. The scoring reward for partial-credit alignment is real and worth pursuing.

#### How the traps combine

A scenario that triggers Trap 1 and Trap 2 at once produces a candidate who rates a wrong action as appropriate because of loyalty and family concerns. Calibration slips to "Appropriate but not ideal" or even "Inappropriate," and the band drift happens across a few clusters, not one.

A scenario that triggers Trap 4 and Trap 5 together produces a candidate who picks the long, confident-sounding option and rates it as Very appropriate while the shorter, correct option sits at "Appropriate but not ideal." Same drift.

The fix is to rate each response in isolation against the absolute scale, then adjust with the calibration in mind. The trap is real and worth flagging in your own answer review.

#### Worked example — full-scenario calibration

Scenario:

```
  A 17-year-old patient is admitted after a paracetamol overdose.
  The patient is awake but distressed. Their parent is in the
  waiting room and has asked the junior doctor not to tell the
  patient anything they might find distressing.
```

Three questions on this scenario (notional; UCAT typically uses two to four). All three use the same trap combinations: personal reflex (Trap 1), group-loyalty reflex (Trap 2), authoritative-tone bias (Trap 4).

```
  Question 1 — Appropriateness: rate each of four responses.

    R1: "Assess the patient's mental state and capacity before
    deciding what to tell them."

      Anchor: GMC guidance — patient autonomy, capacity-led
      disclosure. Whether to tell is the patient's choice once
      capacity is established. The parent's request is a
      consideration, not the deciding factor.

      Rating: A (Very appropriate).

    R2: "Tell the parent everything will be fine and ask them to
    calm the patient."

      Anchor: misleading reassurance, ignores capacity, defers to
      parent over patient.

      Rating: D (Very inappropriate).

    R3: "Assess capacity, then discuss the admission and treatment
    plan with the patient only."

      Anchor: close to ideal but ignores the parent's reasonable
      request to be involved where appropriate.

      Rating: B (Appropriate, but not ideal).

    R4: "Tell the patient nothing, follow the parent's wishes."

      Anchor: defers to parent over patient.

      Rating: D (Very inappropriate).

  Question 2 — Importance: rate each of four considerations.

    C1: "The patient's capacity to make decisions about their
    own care."

      Anchor: highest duty.

      Rating: Very important.

    C2: "The parent's stated wishes."

      Anchor: relevant, but secondary to the patient's autonomy.

      Rating: Of minor importance.

    C3: "Mental-health assessment and safeguarding referral."

      Anchor: directly relevant, time-critical.

      Rating: Very important.

    C4: "The junior doctor's own comfort with the case."

      Anchor: relevant to the doctor's wellbeing, but not the
      deciding factor.

      Rating: Of minor importance.

  Question 3 — Appropriateness, second angle: a different patient
  with English as a second language.

    R1: "Use a professional interpreter instead of the family
    member."

      Anchor: GMC and NHS guidance — use professional interpreters
      unless the patient prefers otherwise.

      Rating: A (Very appropriate).

    R2: "Use the family member to translate quickly and arrange
    follow-up with an interpreter later."

      Anchor: compromise of confidentiality and accuracy.

      Rating: C (Inappropriate, but not awful).

    R3: "Proceed without translation because the case is
    urgent."

      Anchor: omits consent.

      Rating: D (Very inappropriate).

    R4: "Ask the patient which they prefer."

      Anchor: patient choice.

      Rating: B (Appropriate, but not ideal — the default should
      be a professional interpreter; asking the patient without
      recommending it is close to ideal but not quite right).
```

#### How to study the traps

The six traps are not equally likely. Trap 1 (personal moral reflex) and Trap 2 (group loyalty) account for the largest band drops. Trap 3 (importance as relatedness) and Trap 4 (authoritative tone bias) account for the second-largest. Traps 5 and 6 cause clusters of small slips that add up.

A weekly study plan that targets all six:

1. **Day 1.** Take ten scenarios. Read each scenario, then write down which trap you are most at risk of falling into. Rate the responses.
2. **Day 2.** Re-read the answers. For each response where you rated B or C, defend the rating in one sentence. For each response where you rated A or D, check for Trap 4 (authoritative tone bias).
3. **Day 3.** Take ten more scenarios, this time with the trap names written on a card you can glance at.
4. **Day 4.** Combine with a Verbal Reasoning mock to test pacing under subtest-switch fatigue.

#### Practice after one cycle

After one cycle of the four-day plan, your wrong answers should cluster in one or two traps, not all six. Identify which traps still bite you. The second cycle targets those specifically.

### Memory anchors

- **Read all four options twice before rating any.** Anchor on the absolute scale.
- **Patient safety, dignity, consent, honesty, professional standards.** Highest-rated considerations.
- **Loyalty to colleagues does not extend to covering harm.** The senior escalation route is appropriate.
- **Calibrate the mid-scale ratings carefully.** That is where partial marks come from.
- **One sentence defence.** If you cannot defend a rating in one sentence, re-rate.

---

### Common traps

- Rating a sympathetic but wrong action as appropriate because the patient or family is in distress.
- Defending silence or loyalty over patient safety.
- Rating a factor as important because it is mentioned.
- Letting the longest, most decisive-sounding option rank higher than the correct short option.
- Skipping the second read of the four options.

---

### What to do next

1. Take ten SJT scenarios and rate every response. After rating, write one sentence defending each rating. If the defence is shaky, that response is where your calibration needs work.
2. Build a flashcard set of the six traps with one example for each. Read the set before each practice session.
3. Sit one full SJT mock under timed conditions. After, count your answers in each band; the distribution tells you which trap is biting you most.
4. Repeat the cycle with a second mock. The official UCAT practice tests are the right benchmark for the 69-question, 26-minute shape and the published band descriptors. Cohort-level figures and any year-on-year statistics are on ucat.ac.uk, and they are the only numbers worth carrying into your booking cycle.
