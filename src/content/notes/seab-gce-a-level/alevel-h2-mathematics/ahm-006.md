# Probability and Statistics — H2 Mathematics (9758) Notes

Probability and Statistics is the second-largest content strand in the H2 Mathematics 9758 syllabus by marks, after Calculus. It spans the second paper (Paper 2) and covers permutations, combinations, the binomial and Poisson distributions, the normal distribution, sample means and the Central Limit Theorem, unbiased estimators, confidence intervals, and hypothesis testing for the mean and proportion. A JC2 student aiming at an A or B grade must secure this strand alongside Calculus.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### The seven facts every JC2 student needs

1. **Permutation**: an arrangement of r items from n distinct items, order matters. P(n, r) = n! / (n − r)!.
2. **Combination**: a selection of r items from n distinct items, order does not matter. C(n, r) = n! / [r! (n − r)!].
3. **Binomial distribution**: the number of successes in n independent Bernoulli trials, each with success probability p. X ~ B(n, p); E(X) = np; Var(X) = np(1 − p).
4. **Poisson distribution**: the number of events in a fixed interval, when events occur independently at a constant mean rate λ. X ~ Po(λ); E(X) = λ; Var(X) = λ.
5. **Normal distribution**: a continuous distribution symmetric about the mean μ with standard deviation σ. X ~ N(μ, σ²); the standard normal Z = (X − μ) / σ has mean 0 and standard deviation 1.
6. **Central Limit Theorem**: the distribution of the sample mean X̄ of n independent observations from a population with mean μ and variance σ² approaches N(μ, σ²/n) as n → ∞.
7. **Hypothesis test for the mean**: state H₀ and H₁, compute the test statistic, find the p-value or critical value, conclude at the chosen significance level.

#### Key notations

- X ~ B(n, p) — X has a binomial distribution with parameters n and p
- X ~ Po(λ) — X has a Poisson distribution with parameter λ
- X ~ N(μ, σ²) — X has a normal distribution with mean μ and variance σ²
- X̄ — the sample mean
- s² — the sample variance (with denominator n − 1)
- z_{α/2} — the upper α/2 critical value of the standard normal
- t_{α/2, n−1} — the upper α/2 critical value of the t-distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Permutations and combinations

A **permutation** is an arrangement where order matters. P(n, r) = n! / (n − r)!. Example: the number of ways to arrange the first three places from a class of 30 is P(30, 3) = 30 × 29 × 28 = 24 360.

A **combination** is a selection where order does not matter. C(n, r) = n! / [r! (n − r)!]. Example: the number of ways to choose a committee of 3 from a class of 30 is C(30, 3) = 30 × 29 × 28 / (3 × 2 × 1) = 4 060.

A useful identity: C(n, r) = C(n, n − r).

For arrangements with repeated items (e.g. the letters of MISSISSIPPI), the number of arrangements is n! divided by the product of the factorials of the multiplicities: 11! / (1! × 4! × 4! × 2!).

#### Binomial distribution

A binomial random variable X counts the number of successes in n independent trials, each with the same success probability p. X ~ B(n, p).

Probability mass function: P(X = r) = C(n, r) p^r (1 − p)^(n − r).

Expected value: E(X) = np.

Variance: Var(X) = np(1 − p).

Cumulative probabilities P(X ≤ r) are usually read from statistical tables or computed with a calculator.

#### Poisson distribution

A Poisson random variable X counts the number of events in a fixed interval (of time, space, etc.), when events occur independently at a constant mean rate λ. X ~ Po(λ).

Probability mass function: P(X = r) = e^(−λ) λ^r / r!.

Expected value: E(X) = λ.

Variance: Var(X) = λ.

The Poisson distribution can be used as an approximation to the binomial distribution when n is large and p is small (with λ = np).

#### Normal distribution

A normal random variable X has the famous bell-shaped distribution with mean μ and variance σ². X ~ N(μ, σ²).

The standard normal Z = (X − μ) / σ has mean 0 and standard deviation 1. Cumulative probabilities P(Z ≤ z) are read from standard-normal tables or computed with a calculator.

For a sum or difference of independent normals: X + Y ~ N(μ_X + μ_Y, σ²_X + σ²_Y) and X − Y ~ N(μ_X − μ_Y, σ²_X + σ²_Y).

#### Sampling and the Central Limit Theorem

A **simple random sample** of size n is a sample where every subset of size n from the population is equally likely to be chosen.

The **sample mean** X̄ = (X₁ + X₂ + … + Xₙ) / n has mean μ (the population mean) and standard deviation σ / √n (the standard error of the mean).

The **Central Limit Theorem**: for large n, the distribution of X̄ is approximately normal with mean μ and standard deviation σ / √n, regardless of the distribution of the underlying population (provided the population has finite variance).

This is the foundation for confidence-interval estimation and hypothesis testing.

#### Confidence intervals

A confidence interval for the population mean μ is X̄ ± z × σ / √n (when σ is known) or X̄ ± t × s / √n (when σ is estimated by s from the sample, using a t-distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom).

A confidence interval for the population proportion p is p̂ ± z × √[p̂(1 − p̂) / n].

A confidence level of c% means that if the same procedure is repeated many times, c% of the intervals constructed will contain the true parameter. It is a property of the procedure, not of any specific interval.

#### Hypothesis testing

A hypothesis test is a procedure for deciding between two competing claims about a population parameter.

**One-sample z-test for the mean** (σ known): H₀: μ = μ₀ vs H₁: μ ≠ μ₀ (two-sided), μ > μ₀, or μ < μ₀ (one-sided). Test statistic Z = (X̄ − μ₀) / (σ / √n). Compare to z_{α/2} (two-sided) or z_α (one-sided), or compute the p-value.

**One-sample t-test for the mean** (σ unknown): same procedure but use s in place of σ and the t-distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom.

**One-sample z-test for the proportion**: H₀: p = p₀ vs H₁: p ≠ p₀ (two-sided). Test statistic Z = (p̂ − p₀) / √[p₀(1 − p₀) / n].

**Conclusion**: reject H₀ if the p-value is less than the significance level α (typically 0.05); otherwise do not reject H₀. Not rejecting H₀ does not prove H₀; it just means the data do not provide sufficient evidence against it.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Where Probability and Statistics appears across the H2 Mathematics papers

- **H2 Mathematics 9758 Paper 2**: the dominant paper for this strand. Permutations, combinations, binomial and Poisson distributions, normal distribution, Central Limit Theorem, confidence intervals and hypothesis testing all appear on Paper 2.
- **H2 Mathematics 9758 Paper 1**: probability items appear occasionally (e.g. as a prelude to a statistics item), but the main Probability and Statistics load is Paper 2.
- **Across the papers**: stating the null and alternative hypotheses explicitly before computing the test statistic is the highest-leverage exam technique for hypothesis-test items.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Permutations and combinations are the same." Permutations count arrangements (order matters); combinations count selections (order does not).
- "The binomial distribution requires normality." The binomial distribution is exact for any n and p; the normal distribution is an approximation when n is large.
- "The sample mean equals the population mean." X̄ is an estimator of μ; they are equal only on average. The standard error of X̄ is σ / √n.
- "A 95% confidence interval has a 95% chance of containing the true parameter." The interval either contains the true parameter or it does not; the 95% refers to the long-run success rate of the procedure.
- "Rejecting H₀ proves the alternative." Rejecting H₀ at level α means the data provide sufficient evidence against H₀; it does not prove H₁ beyond any doubt.

#### Exam technique

- For permutation / combination questions, state whether order matters before choosing the formula. The order statement is the method mark.
- For binomial / Poisson / normal distribution questions, state the parameters of the distribution before computing. The parameter statement is the method mark.
- For confidence-interval questions, write the formula with the sample values substituted. The substituted formula is the method mark.
- For hypothesis-test questions, state H₀ and H₁ explicitly; compute the test statistic; find the critical value or p-value; conclude in context.
