# World Geography

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**World Geography** maps the Earth's physical systems (continents, oceans, landforms, climate) and human systems (nations, populations, capitals) onto a grid of **latitude** (north–south, 0° at the **equator**) and **longitude** (east–west, 0° at the **Prime Meridian** through Greenwich). The seven **continents** — Asia, Africa, North America, South America, Antarctica, Europe, Australia/Oceania — sit amid five oceans led by the **Pacific** (largest, ~165 million km²). Highest point: **Mt. Everest at 8,848.86 m**; deepest: **Mariana Trench Challenger Deep (~11,034 m)**. Longest river: **Nile (~6,650 km)**; largest by discharge: **Amazon**. Most populous country since 2023: **India**; largest by area: **Russia**. Remember **1° latitude ≈ 111 km** and **15° longitude = 1 hour** of time. For LAT GK, expect match-the-following on capitals, straits, and climate belts.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Earth's Coordinate Grid
Every point on Earth is fixed by **latitude** (φ, angle from equator, 0°–90° N/S) and **longitude** (λ, angle from the Prime Meridian, 0°–180° E/W). One degree of latitude spans roughly **111 km** because meridians are great circles of nearly constant length. Longitude lines (parallels of meridian) converge at the poles, so 1° longitude equals **111 × cos(φ) km** — at the equator the same as latitude, but zero at the poles. **Time zones** rotate at 15° per hour because Earth completes 360° in 24 h: *Local Time = GMT ± (λ ÷ 15)*, giving **4 minutes per degree** of longitude.

#### Continents, Oceans, and Tectonics
The seven continents cover ~29% of Earth's surface; the five oceans cover ~71%. The **Pacific** is largest and deepest; the **Arctic** the smallest and shallowest. The lithosphere is broken into seven major **tectonic plates** (Pacific, North American, Eurasian, African, South American, Indo-Australian, Antarctic). Their interactions produce the **Pacific Ring of Fire** (subduction zones around the Pacific rim), the mid-Atlantic **rift valley**, and the **Himalayas** (Indian–Eurasian collision). LAT questions often test plate boundaries that produce earthquakes and volcanoes.

#### Climate Belts and Biomes
Köppen classification divides climate by temperature and precipitation: **Af** (tropical rainforest — Amazon, Congo), **BWh/BWk** (hot/cold desert — Sahara, Gobi), **Csa/Csb** (Mediterranean — borders of the Mediterranean Sea), **ET** (tundra — Arctic fringes), **Dfa/Dfb** (humid continental — NE USA, Russia). Latitudinal controls produce predictable belts: equatorial low pressure (ITCZ, heavy rain), subtropical highs (deserts at ~30° N/S), mid-latitude westerlies, polar easterlies.

#### LAT-Specific Patterns
Three recurring question formats: (1) **Capital–country match** (Brasília → Brazil, Nur-Sultan/Astana → Kazakhstan); (2) **Straits and canals** that control maritime trade — **Hormuz** (Persian Gulf oil), **Malacca** (Indian–Pacific shipping), **Gibraltar** (Atlantic–Mediterranean), **Bering** (Pacific–Arctic); (3) **Map-based** location of deserts, ranges, rivers.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Traps
- **"Continent" vs "country":** Australia is both a country and a continent; Antarctica has no sovereign states. The Eurasian boundary along the **Ural Mountains, Ural River, Caspian, Caucasus** is conventional, not physical.
- **Nile vs Amazon:** The Nile is longest (6,650 km) but the Amazon carries more discharge (~6,591 km long but ~209,000 m³/s flow vs Nile's ~2,800 m³/s).
- **Population rank:** India surpassed China in 2023 (~1.43 bn vs ~1.41 bn) — older LAT prep material may still list China first.
- **Tropic latitudes:** Tropic of Cancer = **23.5° N**, Tropic of Capricorn = **23.5° S**, Arctic Circle = **66.5° N**, Antarctic Circle = **66.5° S**. Sun is overhead at the equator on the **equinoxes** (21 Mar, 23 Sep) and at the tropics on the **solstices** (21 Jun, 22 Dec).
- **El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO):** Periodic warming of central-eastern Pacific trade winds reversal, causing global rainfall anomalies — droughts in Australia/India, floods in Peru.
- **Monsoon mechanism:** Differential heating of Asian landmass vs Indian Ocean creates a low-pressure pull over the Tibetan Plateau in summer, reversing wind direction and delivering the **Southwest Monsoon** to the Indian subcontinent (June–September).
- **Biodiversity hotspots:** 36 recognized hotspots (e.g., Western Ghats, Madagascar, Atlantic Forest); together they hold >50% of endemic plant species despite covering <3% of land area.

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics
World Geography links to **Indian Polity** (Indian Ocean geopolitics, Chabahar port), **International Relations** (strait choke points in trade law), and **Environment** (desertification, deforestation, climate-change litigation). For LAT's Legal Reasoning section, **maritime boundary disputes** (e.g., South China Sea arbitration, UNCLOS Article 76) draw directly on geographic facts.

#### Common Mistakes
1. Confusing **Greenwich Mean Time (GMT)** with **Coordinated Universal Time (UTC)** — they differ by <1 second but are technically distinct standards.
2. Treating the **International Date Line** (≈180° longitude) as straight — it zig-zags around Kiribati, Samoa, and the Aleutians.
3. Mixing up **strait** (narrow water passage between landmasses), **isthmus** (narrow land bridge between water bodies — Panama), **peninsula** (land surrounded by water on three sides), and **archipelago** (island chain).

#### Practice Prompts
1. If it is 12:00 GMT at Greenwich, what is the local solar time at **80° E**? *(Answer: 12:00 + 80/15 = 17:20, or 5:20 PM.)*
2. A ship sails from the **Persian Gulf** to **Japan** carrying crude oil. Name the three critical chokepoints it must transit. *(Answer: Strait of Hormuz → Strait of Malacca → East China Sea.)*
