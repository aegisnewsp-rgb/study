# Fill in the Blanks

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your CLAT English section.

Fill in the Blanks is a cloze-style item where you drop a single word into a sentence so that grammar, meaning, and idiom all hold together. CLAT English (Section A, ~22–26 questions) in standard papers carries **4–6 fill-in-the-blank items**, worth roughly **3% of total weightage** — small but fast-scoring if you eliminate by part of speech first.

- **Identify the slot:** look at the word right before and after the blank — article + blank = noun; modal + blank = base verb; verb + blank = adverb.
- **Match the form:** singular/plural, tense (past, present, past participle), and voice (active/passive) must align with the rest of the sentence.
- **Read for cues:** contrast words (*but, however, unlike*) flip the meaning; cause words (*because, therefore*) push a logical direction.
- **Eliminate on idiom:** reject options that break collocations — *heavy rain*, not *strong rain*; *commit a crime*, not *perform a crime*.

| Quick filter | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| Article (a/an/the) before blank | Needs a noun |
| Modal (can/can/will) before blank | Needs base verb |
| Adjective before blank | Needs a noun |
| Verb before blank | Needs an adverb |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### What the question actually tests

Each CLAT fill-in-the-blank item probes three competencies in one shot: **vocabulary** (does the candidate know the word?), **grammar** (does the word fit the slot?), and **idiom** (does the combination sound native?). A wrong answer in most keys fails on one of these three even when the meaning seems right. The Consortium of NLUs' CLAT syllabus lists "vocabulary" and "grammar" explicitly, which is why this item type recurs every year.

#### The four-step elimination method

1. **Decode the slot.** Identify the part of speech demanded by the surrounding structure. If the blank sits between "the" and a verb, it must be a noun acting as subject or object.
2. **Scan for context signals.** Conjunctions like *although, whereas, since, therefore* reshape the expected meaning — note whether the blank needs a positive or negative word.
3. **Match word form.** If the sentence reads *"The data ____ collected"*, only the past participle *were* or *has been* fits. Drop options in the wrong tense.
4. **Test collocation.** Plug the surviving option back mentally. *Make a mistake*, not *do a mistake*. *Reach a conclusion*, not *arrive a conclusion*.

#### Word-form traps examiners use

CLAT setters love pairing words from the same family to test inflection — *economy, economic, economical*; *decide, decisive, decision*. They also use near-synonyms that differ in register: *reject* (formal, strong) vs *refuse* (general) vs *decline* (polite).

| Trap | Wrong pick | Correct pick |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adverb needed after verb | He works *hard* | He works *hardly* (means "barely") |
| Preposition idiom | good *for* | good *at* |
| Connotation clash | *cheap* furniture | *inexpensive* furniture (formal) |
| Tense shift | She *go* yesterday | She *went* yesterday |

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge cases that decide top ranks

The difference between 90 and 99 percentile in English in most keys comes from items where two options are grammatically valid but only one is idiomatic. Consider: *"The committee ____ divided in its opinion."* Both *was* and *were* are grammatically defensible (committee = collective noun), but CLAT conventions and most style guides prefer the singular verb for a single body acting as one unit, while *were* works when individual members are emphasised. Recognising such nuance requires reading widely — editorials in *The Hindu* and *The Indian Express* model the kind of formal register CLAT rewards.

#### Connection to adjacent CLAT items

Fill-in-the-blank skills transfer directly to **cloze passages** (longer cloze tests in Section A) and **sentence correction**. The same four-step method applies; only the option set grows. Strong performance here also feeds **reading comprehension**, because the ability to parse subject–verb chains and modifier placement improves active reading.

#### Common mistakes

- Choosing a word that fits meaning but breaks the required part of speech.
- Ignoring tense or number consistency across the clause.
- Confusing homophones — *affect/effect*, *principal/principle*, *discrete/discreet*.
- Skipping the contrast cue (*but, yet, however*) and filling a word that contradicts the second clause.

> **Exam-day tip:** Spend 45–60 seconds per item; if two options survive the grammar filter, pick the one whose collocation you've seen most in many papers in editorials.

#### Practice prompts

1. *Despite the heavy rains, the match ____ proceeded as scheduled.* — options: (a) still (b) yet (c) always (d) never. *(Answer: still — adverb modifying the verb, no contrast reversal.)*
2. *The committee's decision was ____ by a narrow margin.* — options: (a) accept (b) accepted (c) accepting (d) acceptance. *(Answer: accepted — past participle for passive voice.)*
