# Ratio Analysis

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Ratio analysis expresses relationships between financial statement line items to judge profitability, liquidity, activity, and gearing. In ICAN Accounting (3% weighting), examiners test both arithmetic calculation and interpretation of what the resulting numbers signal about a business.

Key formulas to memorise:

- **Gross Profit Margin** = (Gross Profit ÷ Revenue) × 100
- **Net Profit Margin** = (Profit after Tax ÷ Revenue) × 100
- **Current Ratio** = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities (times)
- **Quick Ratio** = (Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities (times)
- **Inventory Turnover** = Cost of Sales ÷ Average Inventory (times)

High-yield pointers:

- Always pair a result with a benchmark (prior year, budget, or industry average).
- The quick ratio deliberately excludes inventory to test instant liquidity.
- Never quote a ratio without commenting on whether it improved or worsened.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Classification of Ratios

ICAN examiners expect students to slot every ratio into one of four families. Misclassification is a frequent reason for losing the easy marks in a theory question.

#### Formula Reference Table

| Ratio | Formula | What it measures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gross Profit Margin | (Gross Profit ÷ Revenue) × 100 | Pricing power and direct cost control |
| Net Profit Margin | (PAT ÷ Revenue) × 100 | Overall profitability after all expenses |
| Return on Capital Employed | Operating Profit ÷ Capital Employed × 100 | Efficiency of long-term capital use |
| Current Ratio | Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities | Short-term solvency |
| Quick (Acid Test) Ratio | (Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities | Immediate liquidity without stock reliance |
| Inventory Turnover | Cost of Sales ÷ Average Inventory | Speed of stock conversion to sales |
| Trade Receivable Days | (Trade Receivables ÷ Revenue) × 365 | Collection efficiency in days |

#### Interpretation Workflow

- **Step 1:** Compute each ratio for at least two periods (current vs prior).
- **Step 2:** Compare movement — state explicitly whether the ratio rose, fell, or held.
- **Step 3:** Link the change to a plausible business cause (rising sales, slower collections, higher gearing).
- **Step 4:** Cross-reference related ratios; falling liquidity alongside rising gearing often signals distress.
- **Step 5:** Conclude with a recommendation or risk flag.

#### Common Exam Traps

- Swapping cost of sales into the margin formula denominator.
- Computing ROCE using profit before interest and tax without restating capital employed.
- Quoting year-end inventory rather than the average, which inflates turnover when stock is building.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Worked Micro-Example

Extract from Ndek Limited (₦ millions): Revenue 800, Cost of Sales 480, PAT 96, Current Assets 320, Inventory 120, Current Liabilities 160.

- Gross Profit Margin = (800 − 480) ÷ 800 = **40%**
- Net Profit Margin = 96 ÷ 800 = **12%**
- Current Ratio = 320 ÷ 160 = **2.0 times**
- Quick Ratio = (320 − 120) ÷ 160 = **1.25 times**
- Inventory Turnover = 480 ÷ 120 = **4.0 times**

Interpretation: a 2:1 current ratio with a quick ratio above 1 suggests comfortable liquidity, while the 25 percentage-point gap between gross and net margins indicates overhead and finance costs absorb roughly half of operating profit.

#### Edge Cases and Limitations

- Ratios built on historical cost ignore inflation, distorting long-lived asset bases.
- Window-dressing at year-end (e.g., paying creditors just before reporting date) can flatter the current ratio without genuine improvement.
- Different accounting policies (FIFO vs weighted average) make inter-firm comparison unreliable unless disclosed.
- A single ratio is meaningless — gearing interacts with profitability through interest cover, and liquidity interacts with activity through receivables days.
