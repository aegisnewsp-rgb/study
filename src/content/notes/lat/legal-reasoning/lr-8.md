# Indian/Pakistani Legal History

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Indian/Pakistani Legal History** traces the subcontinent's legal evolution across four phases: ancient customary law, Islamic jurisprudence after the 712 AD Arab conquest of Sindh, British colonial legislation (1772 onwards), and post-1947 Pakistani statutes built on the 1935 Act via the **Pakistan (Adaptation and Modification) Order 1964**.

Key statutes to memorise for LAT MCQs:

- **Regulating Act 1773** — first British attempt to regulate the East India Company; created the Governor-General and Supreme Court at Calcutta.
- **Charter Act 1833** — ended the Company's commercial functions; made the Governor-General of India the legislative head.
- **Government of India Act 1919 (Montagu-Chelmsford)** — introduced **diarchy** at the provincial level.
- **Government of India Act 1935** — provided provincial autonomy and established the **Federal Court**.
- **Indian Independence Act 1947** — created the dominions of India and Pakistan, ending the Privy Council's appellate jurisdiction.

The **Shariat Application Act 1937** was enacted under British India, not in Pakistan, and applies Muslim personal law to all Muslims.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Colonial Legislative Timeline

Each British statute reorganised governance on the subcontinent and shaped the legal system Pakistan inherited. The Regulating Act 1773 followed the Bengal famine of 1770 and curbed Company corruption. Pitt's India Act 1784 created a dual system of political and commercial control through a Board of Control. The Charter Acts of 1833 and 1853 centralised legislative authority in the Governor-General-in-Council, paving the way for a unified codal system later realised in statutes like the Indian Penal Code 1860 and the Code of Civil Procedure 1908.

#### The Diarchy-to-Autonomy Shift

The Government of India Act 1919 split provincial subjects into **Reserved** (finance, police, justice — under the Governor) and **Transferred** (education, health, local self-government — under Indian ministers). This experiment failed because the elected ministers lacked fiscal control. The Government of India Act 1935 abolished diarchy and introduced full provincial autonomy, with elected provincial legislatures wielding substantial power from 1937 onwards.

#### Pakistani Constitutional Inheritance

| Statute | Relevance to Pakistan |
| --- | --- |
| Government of India Act 1935 | Operated as Pakistan's interim constitution from 1947 |
| Indian Independence Act 1947 | Created the dominion; abolished Privy Council appeals |
| Adaptation and Modification Order 1964 | Adapted all pre-1947 statutes to Pakistan |
| Shariat Application Act 1937 | Applied uncodified Muslim personal law to Muslims |

- The **Two-Nation Theory**, articulated by the All-India Muslim League, justified partition.
- The Federal Court of India (established 1937) became the Federal Court of Pakistan in 1947, operating under Section 204 of the 1935 Act.
- The Supreme Court of Pakistan replaced the Federal Court only in 1956 under the first Constitution.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Doctrines of Lapse and Paramountcy

**Lord Dalhousie's Doctrine of Lapse** (1848–1856) annexed princely states whose rulers died without natural heirs, provided no adopted son existed. This policy, applied to Satara, Jhansi, and Nagpur, accelerated British territorial expansion but generated resentment exploited during the 1857 rebellion. **Paramountcy** referred to the Crown's claimed overarching authority over Indian princely states, exercised through Residents and political agents. When paramountcy lapsed on 15 August 1947, rulers faced three options: accession to India, accession to Pakistan, or independence. Hyderabad's refusal triggered **Operation Polo** (1948); Junagadh's accession to Pakistan led to a plebiscite favouring India.

#### Anglo-Muhammadan Law and Equity

Courts in British India applied **Anglo-Muhammadan Law** to Muslim personal matters, drawing from the Quran, Sunnah, Ijma, and Qiyas as interpreted by British judges like Syed Amir Ali and Sir William Jones. When Anglo-Muhammadan Law was silent, courts applied **Equity and Good Conscience** — a flexible residual doctrine that later incorporated English equity principles. The Shariat Application Act 1937 abolished custom where it conflicted with Quranic injunctions in personal law matters, redirecting jurisprudence from British-interpreted doctrine to statutory Islamic law.
