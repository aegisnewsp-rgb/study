# Argument Structure and Thesis

GAMSAT's Written Communication section is two essay responses, and ACER describes it as assessing "the ability to generate and develop ideas in writing". Two essays inside a fixed allowance is a small number of chances to make an impression, and there is no partial credit for effort. What gets marked is whether an argument exists, whether it holds together, and whether it develops past its first paragraph.

## What an essay is actually being marked on

Four things, roughly in order of how often they fail.

A position — did you actually answer the question, or did you write about the topic? An essay that surveys without committing has not argued. Then development: did each paragraph advance the argument, or restate the introduction in different words? Padding is highly visible here. Then reasoning: are the claims supported, and do the supports follow? Unsupported assertion is the single most common failure. And finally control: can the reader follow it, and did you say it in the time allowed?

Nobody marks your essay because it is long. Two tightly built paragraphs beat four vague ones.

## Find the question under the prompt

Prompts are phrased as topics, not questions. "Discuss the value of competition" is not asking for a summary of both sides. Before writing, turn the topic into an argument in one sentence:

> Topic: competition in sport.
> Argument: without competition, standards fall, because the incentive that drives improvement is removed.

That sentence is your thesis. If you cannot write it, you do not yet know what your essay is, and every paragraph after it will drift.

Some prompts have no single right answer. That is the point: you are being marked on whether you take a position and develop it, not on which position you take. But take one. A prompt that admits several positions still penalises the essay that refuses to.

## Paragraph shape that works

Every body paragraph does the same four jobs in the same order. It opens with a claim — one sentence stating what this paragraph argues, which is a point rather than a topic. It then does the reasoning, and this is where the marks live. It follows with support: an example, a case, a mechanism, a consequence, and concrete beats abstract every time. Last, it links back to the thesis, so the paragraph is doing work rather than floating.

The common failure is four paragraphs that each begin with a different claim. Read the paragraph openings on their own and ask whether they build toward each other. If they do not, you have not written an essay, you have written a list of observations — nothing is accumulating.

## Ordering matters more than you think

Once you have three or four claims, put them in an order that does some work. Strongest first is the right call in a short essay where you might not finish. Building from the least to the most contested carries the reader to your real point. And when the prompt contains real tension, a contrast — an objection, then a reply — uses it instead of ignoring it.

Do not save your best paragraph for last unless you are confident you will reach it. An essay that ends mid-thought loses more than a slightly weaker final paragraph would.

## Handling a prompt you dislike

You will get an idea you find dull or one you disagree with, and you still have to write it. Three moves work.

Take the defensible side. There is almost always a reasonable position available, and finding it and arguing it properly beats spending the essay fighting the prompt.

Or argue the topic honestly and usefully. Even one paragraph of genuine substance is worth more than three of padding.

Or refine the question. A single early sentence that narrows an over-broad prompt is legitimate and often the strongest move available — but you then have to commit to the narrowed question rather than retreating into surveying everything again.

## Before you move on

Two minutes of checking is worth more than two extra minutes of writing. Can you name what each paragraph argues, in order? Have you answered the question that was asked, or one you preferred? Is there a paragraph you could delete without the essay breaking? And did you leave time to write the conclusion at all?

## Common mistakes

The most frequent one is writing an essay that explains the topic rather than arguing a position — it reads as informative and scores badly, because there is nothing for the marker to weigh.

Next is the conclusion that repeats the introduction with "in conclusion" added, which is padding dressed as a formality. Six paragraphs making the same point at increasing length is the same failure at larger scale. Asserting a claim and then offering a restatement of it as if it were support wastes a paragraph that should have been your strongest.

And never naming the strongest objection reads as not having thought about the topic, which is exactly what it is.
