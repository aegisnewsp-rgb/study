# Listening for Attitude, Modality and Register

The official OET Test Handbook 2026 sets out a Listening sub-test of approximately 40 minutes, three parts and 42 questions, with one mark per correct answer and the score out of 42 converted to the 0 to 500 scale. It also states something that should shape how you prepare: "Listening and Reading are the same for all candidates, regardless of profession."

That means the Listening sub-test is not a reading comprehension exercise with medical nouns in it. It is an exercise in tracking what a speaker is doing, and the distinctions below are where the marks are lost.

## Modality is the single most-missed feature

English speakers signal certainty and obligation with small grammatical words, and they mark them with stress when they matter. Strip those out and the meaning changes.

"Could you increase the dose?" is a request. "Can you increase the dose?" is a question about capability. "Would you mind increasing the dose?" is a more polite request that is still a request. In a healthcare setting these three carry different amounts of instruction, and an item that tests a change in the plan often turns on which one you heard.

The same applies to modals generally. "The patient must stop the medication" and "the patient should stop the medication" and "the patient may stop the medication" are three different clinical instructions.

Stress changes it further. In ordinary conversation, *should* and *must* can sound identical when unstressed, because English speakers often reduce them. A speaker who stresses *must* is overriding something; a speaker who reduces it is offering a default. This is why audio, not text, is the medium — the distinction does not survive being read.

## Register tells you who is speaking and what they want

A consultant, a junior doctor, a pharmacist, a patient and a telephone operator use different vocabulary and different sentence structures for the same event, and the register tells you which is which before you process the content.

"Could you bring Ms Adeyemi's notes forward, please?" — administrative, deferential, a request passed along.

"I'd like to review Ms Adeyemi before the ward round." — a doctor issuing an instruction.

"I went in the night and she was just... I don't know, she wasn't right." — a patient reporting, with vagueness, hedging and an unfinished sentence that carries the emotional weight.

Candidates who try to process every word miss the register cue and then lose the content. Use register to predict what comes next, then listen for the content.

## Advice versus instruction

"Something you might consider is a lower-dose preparation" is advice the listener may decline. "Start on the lower-dose preparation" is an instruction.

A frequent OET item gives you a plan change and asks which speaker expressed it. The distractor is usually a phrase that sounds informative but carries no instruction, or an instruction the speaker attributed to someone else — "the pharmacist said I should…" is a report of advice, not advice itself.

Watch for reported speech generally. "Dr Ahmed recommends a repeat scan" tells you a recommendation exists, not that a decision has been made. The distinction matters clinically and it is exactly what these items test.

## Two habits that fix most of it

Predict before the answer arrives. Having read the question stem, you know what kind of information is coming, so you are listening for that slot rather than for everything at once. The audio is dense and the questions are not; the questions are your filter.

Answer on the words you heard, not the meaning you inferred. If the item asks what the speaker advised and you concluded something different from what was said, choose the one that was said. Inference belongs to the Reading sub-test; here the transcript is the authority.

## Common mistakes

Reading the options while the speaker is still talking. You miss the modality word that changes the answer.

Treating reported speech as instruction. "The registrar said to increase the dose" is a report; the decision belongs to whoever says it.

Confusing a nurse\'s polite request with a doctor\'s instruction. Register signals the role, and the role decides how binding the words are.

Letting the emotional content of a patient\'s speech disrupt your listening. Panic in a speaker\'s voice does not change the information being conveyed.

Assuming hedges are filler. "It might possibly be worth reviewing" is doing real work.

Answering from clinical plausibility rather than from the audio. Plausible alternatives are deliberately included, and they are often more clinically obvious than the correct one.
