# Rights and Duties

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **legal right** is an interest recognised and protected by the legal system, enforceable through courts; a **legal duty** is the corresponding obligation owed by another party to respect, protect, or fulfil that right. **Hohfeld's correlatives** give the exam its analytical spine: every right pairs with a duty, every privilege with a no-right, every power with a liability, and every immunity with a disability. In Pakistan, the **Constitution of Pakistan 1973** is the primary source — **Part II** (Articles 8–28) lists justiciable **Fundamental Rights**, while **Part III** (Articles 29–40) contains non-justiciable **Principles of Policy**. High-yield pointers: (1) the right to life (**Article 9**), due process (**Article 10-A**), and equality (**Article 25**) attract 1–2 questions each in LAT; (2) writ remedies under **Article 199** (High Court) and **Article 184(3)** (Supreme Court) enforce fundamental rights; (3) rights are never absolute — they bend to **reasonable restrictions** in the public interest.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Sources of Rights and Duties in Pakistan
Pakistani jurisprudence draws rights and duties from five sources: the **Constitution of Pakistan 1973** (supreme), **statutory law** enacted by Parliament and provincial assemblies, **Islamic law (Sharia)** as mandated by Article 227, **judicial precedent** (binding obiter of the Supreme Court under Article 189), and **customary law** where not repugnant to statute. For LAT purposes, constitutional rights and the writ jurisdiction that protects them are tested most frequently.

#### Hohfeldian Analysis (Jural Correlatives)
Wesley Hohfeld's framework breaks every legal relation into eight concepts arranged in four correlative pairs:

| Jural Opposites (same party) | Correlatives (opposite party) |
|---|---|
| Right | Duty |
| Privilege (Liberty) | No-right |
| Power | Liability |
| Immunity | Disability |

If A has a **right** against B, then B owes a **duty** to A. If A has a **power** (to alter legal relations by an act, e.g. a landlord terminating a tenancy), B is under a **liability**. LAT questions often present a fact pattern and ask you to identify which Hohfeldian relation applies — read carefully whether the actor is *claiming*, *doing without interference*, *changing status*, or *being protected from change*.

#### Classification of Rights
- **Justiciable Fundamental Rights** (Part II): equality (Art. 25), non-discrimination (Art. 25-A on education added via 18th Amendment), freedom of speech (Art. 19), right to life (Art. 9), due process (Art. 10-A), freedom of religion (Arts. 20–21), property (Art. 23), privacy (read into Art. 14 by *Zahoor Ahmad Wattoo v. PPP*).
- **Non-justiciable Principles of Policy** (Part III): elimination of exploitation, social security, free education, labour rights, Islamic way of life — these guide the State but cannot be enforced through writs.
- **Rights in rem** vs **Rights in personam**: in rem run against the whole world (e.g. ownership of property); in personam run against a specific person (e.g. contractual right).

#### Duties of Citizens
**Article 5** of the Constitution imposes loyalty to the State and obedience to the Constitution. Specific duties include obeying law, paying taxes honestly, defending Pakistan when called, and preserving public property. The 1973 Constitution balances individual **negative duties** (refrain from harming others' rights) with State **positive duties** (provide education, healthcare, security).

#### Exam Pattern
LAT typically frames 2–3 questions per paper around: (a) identifying the correct article, (b) choosing the appropriate writ (habeas corpus for illegal detention, mandamus to compel a public authority, certiorari to quash unlawful orders, prohibition to prevent future action, quo warranto to challenge holding of public office), and (c) distinguishing justiciable from non-justiciable principles. The 3% weightage in Legal Reasoning means mastering 5–6 well-typed questions is achievable.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Limitations on Fundamental Rights
No fundamental right is absolute. Each right carries an internal limitation clause: **Article 15** permits special provisions for women, children, and backward classes; **Article 16** allows reasonable residence requirements for public office; **Article 19** subjects free speech to "reasonable restrictions in the interest of the glory of Islam, the integrity, security or defence of Pakistan, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, or in relation to contempt of court"; **Article 23** property rights are subject to "public interest" acquisition with compensation. Courts apply the **proportionality test** — any restriction must pursue a legitimate aim, be rationally connected, be the least restrictive means, and strike a fair balance (*Liaqat Hussain v. Federation of Pakistan*, 1999 PLD 504).

#### Enforcement: Writ Jurisdiction
- **High Court** (Art. 199) issues writs against a person, government, or authority within its territorial jurisdiction — used by private litigants.
- **Supreme Court** (Art. 184(3)) takes cognizance when a question of "public importance with respect to the enforcement of any of the Fundamental Rights" arises — invoked as a class action.
- **Laches** does not bar fundamental rights enforcement, but delay weakens discretion.

#### Common Mistakes in LAT
1. Confusing **privilege** (liberty to act) with **right** (power to demand another act). Walking on a public road is a *privilege/no-right*, not a *right/duty*.
2. Treating Directive Principles as enforceable — they are not; only Fundamental Rights attract writs.
3. Assuming **moral duties** (filial piety, charity) are legal duties — they bind conscience, not courts.
4. Selecting the wrong writ: *mandamus* lies only against a public authority with a **legal duty** to act, not a private body.

#### Practice Prompts
- A landlord hands a tenant a one-month notice to vacate. Identify the Hohfeldian relation between landlord and tenant during the notice period.
- A provincial statute bars speech that "offends religious sentiment" without defining offence. Applying Art. 19, is the restriction valid? Analyse proportionality.
