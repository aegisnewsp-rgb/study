# Separations, Spectrophotometry and Laboratory Techniques — MCAT Chem/Phys

Separations, spectrophotometry, and laboratory techniques form part of AAMC Foundational Concept 5 of the Chem/Phys section and tie directly into the AAMC Scientific Inquiry and Reasoning Skills (SIRS). The MCAT tests the principles of distillation, extraction, chromatography, electrophoresis, mass spectrometry, and UV-Vis spectrophotometry, and asks students to interpret data from these techniques in the context of biological and chemical problems.

### Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

#### Core techniques and principles

- **Distillation:** separates liquids by boiling point. Simple distillation for large differences in BP; fractional distillation for small differences.
- **Extraction:** transfers a solute from one solvent to another using differential solubility. Used to separate organic compounds from aqueous mixtures.
- **Chromatography:** separates compounds by differential affinity for a stationary phase and a mobile phase. Types: paper, thin-layer (TLC), column, gas (GC), and high-performance liquid (HPLC).
- **Electrophoresis:** separates charged molecules (DNA, RNA, proteins) by size and charge in an electric field. Agarose gel for nucleic acids; SDS-PAGE for proteins.
- **Mass spectrometry:** identifies compounds by mass-to-charge ratio. Output is a mass spectrum with peaks at characteristic m/z values.
- **UV-Vis spectrophotometry:** measures absorbance of light at a specific wavelength. Beer-Lambert law: A = ε × c × l.
- **Centrifugation:** separates components by density. Higher g-force for smaller particles.

#### One-pass drill

1. Identify the technique: separation, identification, or quantification.
2. Identify the property being exploited (boiling point, solubility, size, charge, mass, absorbance).
3. Predict the order of separation or the expected output.

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing the stationary and mobile phases in chromatography. The stationary phase stays put; the mobile phase moves.
- Treating all chromatography as the same. TLC, GC, and HPLC differ in mobile phase and application.
- Forgetting the path length in Beer-Lambert. A = ε × c × l requires l in cm.

### Standard — Regular Study (2d-2mo)

#### Separation techniques

**Distillation:** the MCAT tests simple distillation (separating liquids with very different boiling points) and fractional distillation (separating liquids with close boiling points, using a fractionating column).

**Extraction:** uses an immiscible solvent (often an organic solvent and water) to transfer a solute. The MCAT tests the distribution coefficient (K_d) and the calculation of extraction efficiency.

**Chromatography:** the MCAT tests the principle that compounds with higher affinity for the mobile phase travel faster; compounds with higher affinity for the stationary phase travel slower. Rf value = distance travelled by compound / distance travelled by solvent front.

#### Identification techniques

**Mass spectrometry:** the compound is ionised, fragmented, and detected by m/z. The molecular ion peak (M⁺) gives the molecular weight; fragment peaks give structural information.

**UV-Vis spectrophotometry:** the compound absorbs light at a specific wavelength (e.g., 260 nm for nucleic acids, 280 nm for proteins, 540 nm for the Bradford assay). The Beer-Lambert law relates absorbance to concentration: A = ε × c × l.

#### Quantification and data interpretation

The MCAT tests:

- Reading a calibration curve to determine an unknown concentration.
- Interpreting a chromatogram (peak position = identity; peak area = quantity).
- Interpreting a mass spectrum (molecular ion peak; characteristic fragments).
- Interpreting a UV-Vis spectrum (wavelength of maximum absorbance; relationship between absorbance and concentration).

#### Practice routine

- Take 5 distillation items. Identify which type of distillation fits the separation problem.
- Take 5 extraction items. Compute extraction efficiency after one or more extractions.
- Take 5 chromatography items. Predict the order of elution; calculate Rf values.
- Take 5 spectrophotometry items. Apply Beer-Lambert; read a calibration curve.
- Take 5 mass-spec items. Identify the molecular ion peak; recognise characteristic fragments.

### Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo-6mo)

#### How this topic connects to the other Chem/Phys topics

- **Bonding and intermolecular forces** explain the basis for separations (polarity differences drive extraction; size and polarity drive chromatography).
- **Acid-base chemistry** connects to electrophoresis (charge depends on pH) and to ion-exchange chromatography.
- **Phases and solutions** connect to distillation (boiling points) and to extraction (solubility in immiscible solvents).

#### Common errors and the correction

- **"Higher Rf means stronger adsorption."** Higher Rf means weaker adsorption to the stationary phase (the compound travels further with the mobile phase).
- **"A higher absorbance means a lower concentration."** No — higher absorbance means higher concentration (Beer-Lambert: A is proportional to c).
- **"The molecular ion is always the base peak."** The molecular ion is the unfragmented ion. The base peak is the most abundant peak in the spectrum; it is often a fragment.

#### Exam technique

- For separation items, identify the property being exploited before choosing the technique.
- For chromatography items, identify the stationary phase and the mobile phase; predict the order of elution based on affinity.
- For spectrophotometry items, use Beer-Lambert: A = ε × c × l. Convert units if needed.
- For mass-spec items, find the molecular ion peak (often labelled M or M⁺) and the base peak (the tallest peak).
- Use the platform's calculator for arithmetic.
