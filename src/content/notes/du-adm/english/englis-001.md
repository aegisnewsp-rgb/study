# Reading Comprehension — DU Unit B Admission (Science)

Reading Comprehension is the highest-weight English item on the DU Unit B paper and the one where the difference between a top ranker and a mid-pack candidate shows up. Unlike Grammar or Vocabulary, where you can grind rules and lists, RC rewards a small set of habits that you either pick up from timed practice or don't pick up at all. The chapter is drawn from the kind of unseen passages used across Bangladeshi science admission tests: editorials from *The Daily Star* and *Prothom Alo*, science writing from *New Scientist* and *Scientific American*, and adapted literary prose. Three MCQ patterns dominate the DU Unit B style: (1) factual / explicit — the answer is in a single sentence and you just have to find it, (2) inferential — you must read between the lines, signalled by "it can be inferred" or "the author implies", and (3) vocabulary-in-context — pick the option whose meaning matches the way the word is used in the passage. RC also feeds forward into Cloze Test and Sentence Rearrangement, which reuse the same passage-reading habits.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

**Four question types you'll meet.**

| Type | What it asks | Skill | Time per question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Factual / explicit | A direct fact stated in the passage | Locate the right sentence and match it | ~30 s |
| Inferential | Something implied but not stated | Read the surrounding sentences; don't over-interpret | ~60 s |
| Vocabulary in context | The meaning of a word *as used* in the passage, not in general | Substitute the option back into the sentence | ~45 s |
| Author's tone / purpose / main idea | Why the passage was written, how the author feels | Look at adjectives, adverbs, repeated words, conclusions | ~60 s |

**Three rules that fix most wrong answers.**

1. **The answer is in the passage.** Always. If you think you need outside knowledge, you have misread the question. RC is not a general-knowledge quiz.
2. **The wrong options are designed to look right.** "Half-right" options mix a true fact from the passage with a false claim from outside it. Re-read each option against the passage line by line.
3. **Read the question before the passage.** Skim the questions first so you know what to look for. You do not need to understand every word on first read; you need to know where to find things on second read.

**Skim strategy (90 seconds for a 300-word passage).**
- Read the first sentence (topic sentence).
- Read the last sentence (the conclusion or main claim).
- Skim the first sentence of each paragraph for structure.
- Note any bold names, dates, or numbers — these anchor factual questions.

**Scanning strategy (when answering a factual question).**
- Look for the keyword in the question.
- Find the same word or its synonym in the passage.
- Read the full sentence and the sentence before and after — sometimes the answer is implied across two sentences.

**Tone words to recognise quickly.**

| Positive tone | Neutral tone | Negative tone |
|---|---|---|
| optimistic, hopeful, enthusiastic, laudatory | objective, balanced, analytical | critical, pessimistic, cynical, dismissive |
| admiring, sympathetic | matter-of-fact | sarcastic, sceptical, disapproving |
| encouraging, supportive | descriptive | cautionary, alarmed, hostile |

**Quick self-check.**

- Can you identify the topic sentence of a paragraph in one read?
- Can you distinguish "the author states" (factual) from "the author implies" (inferential)?
- Can you substitute a vocabulary option back into the sentence and tell whether it fits?

### 🟡 Standard — Exam Prep (3d-3w)

#### The PASSAGE strategy in detail

Most Bangladesh admission RC passages run 250–400 words across 4–6 paragraphs. The model below is for a single passage with 4–5 questions. Total time budget: 5–6 minutes including reading.

**Step 1: Pre-read (15 s).** Look at the title and the source (e.g., "Adapted from an editorial in *The Daily Star*, March 2023"). The source tells you the register — a *Daily Star* editorial is opinion-leaning; a *New Scientist* excerpt is descriptive.

**Step 2: Skim the passage (60–90 s).** Read the first sentence, the last sentence, and the first sentence of each paragraph. Mentally note: what is the topic, what is the author's position, and what are the 2–3 supporting points?

**Step 3: Read the questions (45 s).** Note which questions are factual (look for "according to", "the passage states") and which are inferential (look for "it can be inferred", "the author implies", "most likely"). Note any vocabulary questions and the words they target.

**Step 4: Answer in order (3–4 min).** Do factual questions first — they are fastest and confirm you understood the passage. Save the inferential and tone questions for last, when you have full context.

**Step 5: Verify (30 s).** For each answer, find the sentence in the passage that justifies it. If you can't, the answer is probably wrong.

#### Factual questions

The question stem contains a paraphrase of a passage sentence. Your job is to find the matching sentence and confirm the paraphrase preserves meaning.

**Trap patterns in factual questions:**
- **Half-true options.** The first half of the option matches the passage, the second half adds a false claim. Read to the period.
- **Switched cause and effect.** The passage says A causes B; the option says B causes A.
- **Wrong scope.** The passage says "in the last decade"; the option says "in the 1990s". Date or number swaps.
- **Wrong referent.** The passage says "the researchers"; the option says "the government". Pronoun or noun swaps.

Example stem: *"According to the passage, what is the primary cause of the decline in vulture populations in South Asia?"* Find the paragraph that mentions vulture decline. The cause is stated in the same sentence. Match the question's paraphrase against the original sentence and tick the closest option.

#### Inferential questions

Inferential questions ask you to conclude something the passage hints at but does not directly state. The answer is *supported* by the passage, not pulled from thin air.

**Useful inference verbs in option stems:**
- "suggests", "implies", "hints at", "most likely", "can be inferred".

**Trap patterns:**
- **Too strong.** The passage says "may be"; the option says "is". Watch for "always / never / must" in options — these are wrong unless the passage explicitly says so.
- **Too weak.** The passage says "is the leading cause"; the option says "is a contributing factor". Watch for downgrading to "might" or "could".
- **Off-topic.** The inference draws on a different part of the passage than the question intends. Re-read the question's reference (paragraph, line).

**A worked inference.** Passage: *"Although the new policy has been praised for its ambition, several analysts have raised concerns about its implementation timeline."* Question: "Which of the following can be inferred about the policy?" Option A: "It has been universally accepted." — Too strong, the passage says "praised" not "universally accepted", and "concerns" contradict this. Option B: "Its execution faces challenges." — Matches "concerns about its implementation timeline". Option B is correct.

#### Vocabulary in context

The question gives you a word as used in the passage and four options. The right answer is the meaning that fits *this* sentence — sometimes an uncommon meaning of a familiar word.

**Strategy:** substitute each option back into the sentence. Whichever substitution leaves the sentence making sense is the answer. Don't pick the most common dictionary meaning if it breaks the sentence.

**Common trap.** A word with multiple meanings: e.g., "table" can mean a piece of furniture, a list, or (in British usage) to postpone discussion of something. The passage says "The committee voted to table the motion." In British English, this means postpone; in American English, it means bring forward for discussion. The right answer depends on the source of the passage. If the source is *The Daily Star* (UK English), "table" means postpone.

**Other common polysemous words:** "conduct" (lead / behaviour), "novel" (new / book), "minute" (tiny / time unit), "bank" (financial / river), "cell" (prison / biological), "court" (royal / legal / sports).

#### Main idea and tone

**Main idea** is stated or implied in the first or last paragraph. The right answer captures the passage's *central* claim, not a peripheral detail.

**Trap:** an option that summarises one paragraph but not the whole passage. The main idea covers everything, not just one section.

**Tone** is the author's attitude toward the subject. Useful signals:
- Word choice (adjectives, adverbs) — "alarming" vs "notable" vs "interesting".
- Repeated words — if "crisis" appears three times in a passage about floods, the tone is alarmed.
- Conclusions — does the author end on a hopeful note or a warning?

**Trap:** misreading sarcasm or irony. If the passage says, "Brilliant move, building a hospital on a floodplain", the tone is sarcastic, not approving. Read the surrounding sentences for context.

#### Worked example

**Passage.** *Adapted from a 2023 editorial.* Bangladesh's rivers are the spine of its agricultural economy. The delta they form, fed by the Ganges, Brahmaputra and Meghna, supports more than 130 million people and produces the rice that feeds the country. Yet the rivers are also a source of recurring disaster: flooding displaces hundreds of thousands every monsoon, and saline intrusion in the south is ruining farmland faster than new salt-tolerant varieties can compensate. The temptation, in such a setting, is to treat the rivers as an adversary to be controlled — with embankments, dredging, and concrete. This approach has failed repeatedly. A more promising path, currently being trialled in coastal districts, is to work *with* the river system: tidal river management, restoring beels and wetlands, and protecting mangrove buffers like the Sundarbans. Early results suggest that giving the rivers room to flood — rather than boxing them in — reduces both peak flood heights and long-term salinity damage.

**Q1.** According to the passage, Bangladesh's rivers support the country's agriculture primarily by:
- (A) providing irrigation water for rice paddies
- (B) forming a delta that produces much of the country's rice
- (C) preventing saline intrusion into farmland
- (D) carrying monsoon rainwater away from the coast

*Answer: B.* The passage says "The delta they form ... produces the rice that feeds the country." (A) is plausible but not stated. (C) is contradicted by the saline-intrusion complaint. (D) misstates the role.

**Q2.** It can be inferred that the author views the traditional embankment approach to rivers as:
- (A) effective but expensive
- (B) harmful in the long term despite short-term appeal
- (C) widely supported by agricultural scientists
- (D) necessary until tidal river management is fully proven

*Answer: B.* The passage calls the approach "failed repeatedly" and contrasts it with a more promising path. The "temptation" framing implies short-term appeal but long-term failure.

**Q3.** The word "buffers" in the passage most nearly means:
- (A) computer memory stores
- (B) protective barriers
- (C) chemical solutions resisting pH change
- (D) financial reserves

*Answer: B.* The context is "protecting mangrove buffers like the Sundarbans". Mangroves are protective barriers against storm surges and salinity. (A), (C), (D) are dictionary meanings of "buffer" but break the sentence.

**Q4.** The author's main purpose in the passage is to:
- (A) argue against building more embankments
- (B) describe the geography of Bangladesh's river system
- (C) advocate for a flood-management approach that works with rivers rather than against them
- (D) warn about the future of rice production in Bangladesh

*Answer: C.* (A) is too narrow — the author critiques embankments but the broader purpose is to advocate the alternative. (B) is a means, not the end. (D) is implied but secondary.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (1mo+)

#### Tone and attitude in literary passages

The DU paper occasionally uses adapted literary prose (Chekhov, Tagore, Saki, often translated). Tone is harder to read in these because the language is more figurative. Look for:
- **First-person reflections** — the narrator's emotion is in adjectives ("the cold, indifferent sky"), not in stated feelings.
- **Imagery patterns** — repeated water imagery signals a theme of cleansing, time, or change; repeated industrial imagery signals modernity and alienation.
- **Sentence rhythm** — long, flowing sentences with many subordinate clauses suggest a contemplative or melancholic tone. Short, blunt sentences suggest tension or decisiveness.

For a passage about an old man returning to his ancestral village, the tone might be **nostalgic** even if the word "nostalgic" never appears. The right tone option captures that, even though the passage uses more specific words like "remembered", "familiar", "missed".

#### Argument structure: claims, evidence, and assumptions

For opinion / editorial passages, identify the three components of an argument:
1. **Claim** — the position the author wants you to accept.
2. **Evidence** — the data, examples, or reasoning offered in support.
3. **Assumption** — the unstated belief that connects evidence to claim.

Example: claim = "Tidal river management should be expanded nationwide." Evidence = "Early results suggest reduced flood heights and salinity." Assumption = "What works in coastal districts will work in other river systems too." A "strengthen the argument" question would ask you to find an option that supports the assumption; a "weaken the argument" question would offer evidence that contradicts it.

#### Inference vs speculation

The single most common mistake in inferential questions is **over-inference**. The passage must *support* the inference; if the inference goes beyond what the passage allows, it is speculation.

Test: can you point to one or two sentences in the passage that the inference follows from? If yes, the inference is fair. If you need to draw on general knowledge or invent connecting logic, it is speculation.

Worked test. Passage: "The new vaccine showed 80% efficacy in trials." Inference A: "Most vaccinated people will not get the disease." — Supported (80% efficacy ≈ 80% protection). Inference B: "The vaccine will eliminate the disease within five years." — Speculation; the passage says nothing about long-term effects or transmission dynamics.

#### Adapting to unfamiliar vocabulary

In passages drawn from science writing, you will meet unfamiliar technical terms. Do not panic. Three strategies:
1. **Look at the surrounding sentences.** Technical terms are defined or illustrated in the same paragraph ("metabolism, the sum of all chemical reactions in a cell").
2. **Use Greek/Latin roots.** "Endotherm" = inside + heat (warm-blooded). "Photosynthesis" = light + putting together.
3. **Substitute a placeholder.** Replace the unknown word with "X" and see if the sentence still makes grammatical sense. If it does, the word's specific meaning is less important for the surrounding logic.

You rarely need to know the precise term to answer the question. The question asks about the *idea*, not the word.

#### Practice prompts

1. **Skim drill.** Take any 300-word editorial from *The Daily Star* or *Prothom Alo*. Time yourself: 60 seconds for the skim, then close the article and write down the topic, the author's position, and the two strongest supporting points. Repeat daily.
2. **Inference check.** Find a paragraph in your HSC English textbook. Cover the last sentence. Predict what it says. Reveal and compare. Trains the inference habit.
3. **Vocabulary substitution.** Take any five underlined words in a passage. For each, write four options where only one preserves the sentence's logic. Exchange with a study partner.
4. **Tone matching.** Find a paragraph you read recently. Without using any of the tone words in the table, write a one-sentence description of the author's attitude. Then compare to the standard tone vocabulary.
5. **Argument map.** Take a current editorial. Identify claim, evidence, and assumption in three bullet points. Decide whether the evidence actually supports the claim, or whether the assumption is doing the heavy lifting.

#### Connections to adjacent topics

Reading Comprehension shares habits with **Cloze Test** (englis-005) — both require reading for meaning across sentences — and with **Sentence Rearrangement** (englis-004) — both reward recognising how sentences link through pronoun reference, tense, and logical connectors. ### Common traps

- **Answering from prior knowledge instead of the passage.** RC is testing *reading*, not knowledge. If you know a lot about vultures and pick the option that matches your knowledge but contradicts the passage, you lose the mark. The passage is the only source.
- **Choosing the option that "could be true" instead of "is supported".** "Could be true" describes speculation. The right inference is *supported by the passage*. Look for the option with the tightest match to the passage's logic.
- **Skipping the title and source.** The source signals register (formal? informal? opinion? descriptive?) and the title signals topic. Skipping both means you start cold and waste reading time on figuring out what you could have known in five seconds.
- **Confusing "the author states" with "the author believes".** Statements are factual; beliefs are inferential. The question stem distinguishes them — read it carefully. "Stated" questions want the literal paraphrase; "implied" questions want the inference.
- **Picking vocabulary from dictionary memory, not context.** "Compelling" can mean "forceful" or "convincing" depending on context. "Running" can be literal (motion) or figurative (operating). Always substitute the option back into the sentence.
- **Spending too long on one question.** If a question takes more than 90 seconds, mark your best guess and move on. One RC question costs you ~1% of the paper; running out of time on later questions can cost you three times that. Move on; come back if time permits.
- **Reading the passage word-by-word.** This is the slowest and least effective strategy. Skim for structure first, then return to specific sentences to answer questions. The passage is a reference, not a novel to memorise.
