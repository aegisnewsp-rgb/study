# Work, Energy and Power

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Work** is done when a force moves its point of application through a displacement. The scalar quantity is given by **W = Fd cos θ**, where F is force in newtons, d is displacement in metres, and θ is the angle between F and the direction of motion. Work is measured in **joules (J)**.

**Energy** is the capacity to do work. Two mechanical forms dominate WASSCE: **kinetic energy (KE = ½mv²)** and **gravitational potential energy (PE = mgh)**. Their sum, **mechanical energy (ME = KE + PE)**, is conserved when only gravity or ideal springs act.

**Power** is the rate of doing work: **P = W/t**, measured in **watts (W)**. One kilowatt-hour equals **3.6 × 10⁶ J**, the commercial energy unit used in electricity bills.

- **Key fact:** W = 0 when force is perpendicular to motion (e.g., centripetal force does no work).
- **Key fact:** Doubling speed quadruples KE because KE ∝ v².
- **Key fact:** Efficiency = (useful output ÷ total input) × 100%, always below 100%.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Defining the Three Quantities

Work is a **scalar** measured by the dot product of force and displacement. When a 5 N force pushes a block 3 m along its line of action, W = 5 × 3 × cos 0° = 15 J. If the force is at 60° to the displacement, the same calculation gives W = 5 × 3 × cos 60° = 7.5 J. WASSCE Paper 2 frequently tests inclined-plane setups where a force parallel to the slope moves an object up or down, requiring cos θ to be retained or dropped depending on geometry.

Kinetic energy is energy of motion; potential energy is energy of position. The work–energy theorem states **W_net = ΔKE = KE_f − KE_i**, so a net force doing 40 J of work on a stationary 2 kg object gives it a speed v = √(2 × 40 / 2) = √40 ≈ 6.32 m/s.

#### Conservation and Loss

When friction is absent, ME stays constant: a ball dropped from height h reaches the ground with v = √(2gh). On WASSCE, **g = 10 m/s²** is the conventional choice unless the question gives 9.8 m/s².

| Quantity | Formula | SI unit | Common trap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Work | W = Fd cos θ | J (joule) | Forgetting cos θ on inclined planes |
| Kinetic energy | KE = ½mv² | J | Confusing v² with 2v |
| Potential energy | PE = mgh | J | Wrong g value |
| Power | P = W/t | W (watt) | Mixing up W (work) and W (watts) |
| Efficiency | η = output/input × 100% | % | Reporting as a decimal |

#### Power in Machines

A motor lifting a 500 kg load through 6 m in 30 s delivers P = (500 × 10 × 6)/30 = 1000 W. WASSCE efficiency problems then ask: if the motor's electrical input is 1500 W, efficiency = 1000/1500 × 100% = 66.7%.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and the Work–Energy Theorem in Depth

The theorem applies to the **net** force, not a single applied force. On a rough horizontal surface, an applied force F may do positive work while friction does negative work of equal magnitude; the net work is zero, and KE is unchanged. WASSCE examiners exploit this by giving separate values for push and friction, expecting students to compute W_net before applying ΔKE.

For springs, elastic PE = ½kx² (k in N/m, x in m) extends the conservation equation to ME = KE + PE_gravity + PE_spring. This rarely appears on WASSCE but surfaces in JAMB crossover questions, so recognising the pattern helps.

#### Numerical Example

A 2 kg ball rolls from rest down a frictionless 5 m slope inclined at 30° to the horizontal. Height lost h = 5 sin 30° = 2.5 m. By conservation, KE at the bottom = mgh = 2 × 10 × 2.5 = 50 J, giving v = √(2 × 50 / 2) = √50 ≈ 7.07 m/s. If the slope had a 4 N friction force opposing motion along its length, work done against friction = 4 × 5 = 20 J, leaving KE = 50 − 20 = 30 J and v = √30 ≈ 5.48 m/s.

#### Exam Strategy for WAEC

| Paper | Question weight | Typical format |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paper 1 (Objective) | 2–3 questions | One-line calculations: solve for KE, PE, or P |
| Paper 2 (Theory) | 8–12 marks combined | Multi-step: inclined plane + energy + power |
| Paper 3 (Alternative to Practical) | rare | Not usually assessed here |

1. Always state which value of g the question uses before plugging into PE = mgh.
2. Convert kW·h to joules by multiplying by 3.6 × 10⁶ when computing energy cost.
3. Efficiency answers must carry the **%** sign; a decimal like "0.85" loses marks.
