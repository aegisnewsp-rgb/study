# Idioms and Proverbs

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

An **idiom** is a fixed phrase whose meaning cannot be read from its individual words (e.g., *bite the bullet* = to face a tough situation bravely). A **proverb** is a short, complete traditional saying that expresses a general truth or piece of advice (e.g., *A stitch in time saves nine*). In the LAT English MCQ section, this topic routinely contributes 2–4 questions and is high-yield because idioms and proverbs are also embedded in legal-English passages.

- **Idioms** are phrases; **proverbs** are full sentences with a subject and predicate.
- LAT tests three formats: **closest meaning**, **fill-in-the-blank**, and **legal maxim interpretation**.
- Always read at the **figurative level** — the literal meaning is the usual trap.

| Term | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Idiom | *kick the bucket* | to die |
| Proverb | *Actions speak louder than words* | deeds matter more than talk |
| Legal maxim | *Ignorantia juris non excusat* | ignorance of the law is no excuse |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Meaning vs. Form

The crucial distinction LAT examiners test is structural. Idioms like *break the ice* or *hit the nail on the head* are noun phrases or verb phrases; they slot into a sentence where the surrounding grammar supplies the rest. Proverbs like *Honesty is the best policy* or *Practice makes perfect* stand alone as complete clauses with their own subject and predicate. Many candidates lose marks by treating one as the other.

#### LAT Question Formats

The English MCQ section tests idioms and proverbs in three recurring formats. The most common is **closest meaning**, where the candidate must choose the option whose overall meaning matches the expression in context. The second is **sentence completion**, where the idiom must fit the tense, subject-verb agreement, and situational sense of the blank. The third is **passage-based interpretation**, where idioms and Latin maxims appear inside legal-English reading-comprehension prompts.

#### Common Idioms Tested

| Idiom | Closest Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| bite the bullet | endure a painful situation bravely |
| let the cat out of the bag | reveal a secret accidentally |
| once in a blue moon | very rarely |
| hit the nail on the head | be exactly correct |
| burn the midnight oil | work late into the night |

#### Common Proverbs Tested

- *A stitch in time saves nine* — early action prevents bigger losses.
- *Don't count your chickens before they hatch* — do not rely on uncertain outcomes.
- *Where there is a will, there is a way* — determination finds a path.
- *Birds of a feather flock together* — similar people associate with each other.

#### Substitution Strategy

Replace the idiom or proverb with its plain meaning and read the sentence aloud in your head. If it makes sense, the option is correct. If tense shifts, register changes, or the topic drifts, eliminate it. This is faster than matching word-for-word and avoids the literal-meaning trap.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Latin Legal Maxims in LAT

Pakistani legal English draws heavily on Latin maxims, and LAT passages regularly include them. Memorising the standard ten maxims covers most of what the test reaches. They behave like idioms: fixed form, figurative-by-convention meaning, and no derivation from individual Latin words.

| Maxim | Translation | Use in legal English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Audi alteram partem | hear the other side | principle of natural justice |
| Volenti non fit injuria | to a willing person, no wrong is done | defence in tort law |
| Res ipsa loquitur | the thing speaks for itself | negligence evidence |
| Ignorantia juris non excusat | ignorance of the law is no excuse | criminal liability |
| Mens rea | guilty mind | element of a crime |

#### Common Mistakes and Traps

1. Reading *kick the bucket* literally and selecting an option about physical kicking.
2. Choosing an idiom when the prompt asks for a proverb, or the reverse — they are not interchangeable.
3. Matching **individual words** of the option to the idiom instead of its full meaning.
4. Translating Urdu idioms word-for-word; many have no direct English equivalent.
5. Treating Latin maxims as unknown vocabulary instead of learning them as standard phrases.

#### Worked Example

**Question:** *The barrister told the witness to "let the cat out of the bag" about the contract.*
Options: (A) hide the document (B) reveal the secret (C) fold the paper (D) lose the case.

Applying substitution: *The barrister told the witness to reveal the secret about the contract.* The sentence reads correctly with (B). Options (A) and (C) are meaning-inverted; (D) changes the topic entirely.

#### Exam Strategy

- Memorise 80–120 high-frequency idioms plus 10 Latin maxims; this covers the bulk of LAT questions.
- Time target: under 45 seconds per idiom/proverb MCQ.
- Within the English section, this topic carries the highest score-to-effort ratio.
- Practice PYQs from hec.gov.pk past papers to identify the LAT house style.
