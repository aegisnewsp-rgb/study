# Atoms

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

The Bohr model of the hydrogen-like atom fixes electron angular momentum in discrete units and yields closed-form expressions for orbit radius, speed, and energy. Every JEE Advanced paper on this topic tests one of three things: a Bohr calculation for a Z>1 ion, a Moseley's-law numerical on Kα X-rays, or a vector-atom-model question on angular momentum coupling.

- **Energy:** $E_n = -13.6 \dfrac{Z^2}{n^2}$ eV, with $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ and $Z$ = atomic number.
- **Radius:** $r_n = n^2 a_0 / Z$, where $a_0 = 5.29 \times 10^{-11}$ m (Bohr radius).
- **Wavelength:** $\dfrac{1}{\lambda} = R Z^2 \left(\dfrac{1}{n_1^2} - \dfrac{1}{n_2^2}\right)$, $R = 1.097 \times 10^7$ m$^{-1}$.
- **Moseley's law:** $\sqrt{\nu} = a(Z - b)$, $a \approx 4.95 \times 10^7$ Hz$^{1/2}$, $b \approx 1$ for Kα lines.
- **LASER action** requires population inversion through a metastable state followed by stimulated emission of a coherent photon.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Bohr postulates and the hydrogen-like atom

Bohr's 1913 model restricts the electron to orbits whose angular momentum is quantised: $m_e v r = n\hbar$ where $\hbar = h/(2\pi)$. The electron does not radiate while in a stationary orbit, and a photon of energy $h\nu = |E_{n_2} - E_{n_1}|$ is emitted or absorbed only when the electron jumps between orbits. Combining Coulomb's force law with centripetal force and the quantisation condition yields $r_n = n^2 a_0 / Z$ and $v_n = Z\alpha c / n$.

The Bohr model is **strictly valid only for single-electron (hydrogen-like) systems**: H, He$^+$, Li$^{2+}$, Be$^{3+}$. Applying $E_n = -13.6\,Z^2/n^2$ eV to neutral helium or lithium is the most common numerical trap in JEE Advanced.

#### Hydrogen spectral series

The wavelength relation $1/\lambda = R Z^2 (1/n_1^2 - 1/n_2^2)$ gives five named series whose $n_1$ values and spectral regions are tested by single-line MCQs.

| Series | $n_1$ | Region | $n_2$ range |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lyman | 1 | Ultraviolet | $n_2 = 2, 3, 4, \ldots$ |
| Balmer | 2 | Visible | $n_2 = 3, 4, 5, \ldots$ |
| Paschen | 3 | Infrared | $n_2 = 4, 5, 6, \ldots$ |
| Brackett | 4 | Infrared | $n_2 = 5, 6, 7, \ldots$ |
| Pfund | 5 | Far infrared | $n_2 = 6, 7, 8, \ldots$ |

#### Vector atom model

The wave-mechanical atom uses four quantum numbers $(n, l, m_l, m_s)$. Orbital angular momentum magnitude $|\vec{L}| = \sqrt{l(l+1)}\hbar$ and spin $|\vec{S}| = \sqrt{s(s+1)}\hbar$ with $s = 1/2$. The total angular momentum $\vec{J} = \vec{L} + \vec{S}$ has magnitude $\sqrt{j(j+1)}\hbar$, where $j = |l - s|, \ldots, l + s$. This L-S coupling replaces the Bohr single-orbit picture whenever fine structure or Zeeman splitting is asked.

#### Characteristic X-rays

A fast electron knocks out an inner-shell electron; an outer electron fills the vacancy and emits a photon whose frequency obeys $\sqrt{\nu} = a(Z - b)$. The Kα line corresponds to $n = 2 \to 1$ with $b \approx 1$; Kβ is $n = 3 \to 1$ with $b \approx 7.4$. The continuous Bremsstrahlung background has a minimum wavelength $\lambda_{\min} = hc / eV$ set by the accelerating voltage, not by the target material.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Worked example: Kα wavelength of molybdenum

Moseley's law gives the Kα frequency of an element with atomic number $Z$ as $\sqrt{\nu} = 4.95 \times 10^7\,(Z - 1)$ Hz$^{1/2}$. For molybdenum ($Z = 42$):

$$\sqrt{\nu} = 4.95 \times 10^7 \times 41 = 2.03 \times 10^9 \text{ Hz}^{1/2}$$

So $\nu = 4.12 \times 10^{18}$ Hz and $\lambda = c/\nu = 7.28 \times 10^{-11}$ m = 0.0728 nm. The experimental Kα line of Mo is 0.0711 nm; the small gap comes from relativistic corrections to the inner-electron energies that Bohr's formula ignores. This is the standard "Moseley numerical" pattern: plug in $Z$, use the right $b$, convert $\nu \to \lambda$ last.

#### Edge cases and adjacent links

1. **Heisenberg limit on line width.** A spectral line is not perfectly sharp; the natural linewidth is $\Delta E \cdot \Delta t \gtrsim \hbar$, with $\Delta t$ set by the excited-state lifetime ($\sim 10^{-8}$ s for allowed transitions). Doppler broadening from thermal motion of gas atoms dominates in laboratory sources.
2. **LASER three-level scheme.** Optical pumping excites atoms from ground state 1 to state 3, which decays rapidly to metastable state 2. A trigger photon of energy $E_2 - E_1$ stimulates an avalanche of identical, coherent photons; the metastable state makes population inversion sustainable.
3. **Fine structure and Zeeman effect.** Spin-orbit coupling splits each Bohr level by an amount proportional to $Z^4\alpha^2$, and an external magnetic field further splits $m_j$ sublevels — the normal Zeeman effect scales linearly with $B$, the anomalous effect involves electron spin.

#### Practice prompts

1. A Li$^{2+}$ ion makes a transition from $n = 4$ to $n = 2$. Find the wavelength, identify the series, and check whether the photon lies in the visible range. (Use $R = 1.097 \times 10^7$ m$^{-1}$ and $Z = 3$.)
2. An X-ray tube with a copper target ($Z = 29$, $b \approx 1$ for Kα) is operated at 35 kV. Compute the Kα wavelength and the minimum Bremsstrahlung wavelength. Which is shorter, and what does that imply about the continuous spectrum?
