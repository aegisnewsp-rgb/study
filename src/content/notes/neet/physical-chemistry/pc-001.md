# Some Basic Concepts

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Some Basic Concepts of Chemistry** is the quantitative backbone of NEET Physical Chemistry — roughly 3% weightage, almost always 1 question per paper, and frequently the first question of Section B because the numbers are easy. Master three ideas: the **mole** as a counting unit, **molar mass** (g mol⁻¹) as the bridge between grams and moles, and **molarity** (mol L⁻¹) as the bridge between moles and solution volume. The single must-know formula is **n = given mass / molar mass**, paired with N = n × 6.022 × 10²³. At **STP (0 °C, 1 bar)** one mole of any ideal gas occupies **22.4 L**; at **SATP (25 °C, 1 bar)** it occupies **24.8 L**. Remember: **1 amu = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g**, and the **n-factor** of an acid equals its basicity (H⁺ furnished).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Matter, Atoms, and Dalton's Theory
**Matter** is anything occupying space and possessing mass, classified as elements, compounds, or mixtures (homogeneous vs heterogeneous). **Dalton's atomic theory** (1808) proposed that matter consists of indivisible atoms; atoms of the same element are identical in mass; compounds contain atoms in fixed whole-number ratios; and chemical reactions only rearrange atoms. Two postulates were later modified — atoms are divisible (subatomic particles exist) and isotopes give different masses for the same element.

#### The Mole Concept
One **mole** contains exactly **6.02214076 × 10²³** entities (Avogadro's number, Nₐ). Conversions:

- n (mol) = given mass (g) / molar mass (g mol⁻¹)
- N (particles) = n × Nₐ
- At STP, V (L) = n × 22.4

**Molar mass** of a compound is the sum of atomic masses; for example, H₂SO₄ = 2(1) + 32 + 4(16) = 98 g mol⁻¹.

#### Concentration Terms
| Term | Formula | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Mass percent | (mass solute / mass solution) × 100 | Commercial labels |
| **Molarity (M)** | moles solute / L of solution | Titration, lab work |
| **Molality (m)** | moles solute / kg of solvent | Colligative properties |
| Mole fraction (x) | moles component / total moles | Raoult's law |

Molarity depends on temperature (volume expands); molality does not.

#### Laws of Chemical Combination
- **Conservation of mass** (Lavoisier): total mass is constant in a closed reaction.
- **Definite proportions** (Proust): a compound always has the same element ratio by mass.
- **Multiple proportions** (Dalton): when two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one combining with a fixed mass of the other are in simple whole-number ratios.
- **Gay-Lussac**: volumes of reacting gases (and gaseous products) at the same T and P are in simple integer ratios.

#### Empirical and Molecular Formulae
**Empirical formula** = simplest integer ratio (from mass-percent or combustion data). **Molecular formula** = n × empirical formula, where n = (molar mass) / (empirical formula mass).

#### Limiting Reagent
The reactant consumed first; it dictates maximum product. Compare available moles divided by stoichiometric coefficient — the smallest ratio wins.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Worked Example
Q: 5.85 g of NaCl is dissolved in water and made up to 500 mL. Calculate molarity.
Molar mass NaCl = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 g mol⁻¹; n = 5.85 / 58.5 = 0.1 mol; M = 0.1 / 0.5 L = **0.2 M**.

#### Equivalent Mass and n-Factor
**Equivalent mass = molar mass / n-factor.** For acids, n = basicity (HCl = 1, H₂SO₄ = 2, H₃PO₄ = 3 depending on reaction stoichiometry). For bases, n = acidity (NaOH = 1, Ca(OH)₂ = 2). For salts in redox, n = total electron change per formula unit. Normality = n-factor × molarity.

#### Eudiometry (Gas Stoichiometry)
Combustion reactions (hydrocarbons in excess O₂) inside a **eudiometer tube** over mercury allow volume-based stoichiometry. Apply Gay-Lussac + Avogadro: volumes are proportional to moles only at constant T and P. For gaseous reactions with water produced as vapour, subtract aqueous tension from total pressure before applying PV = nRT.

#### Significant Figures and Precision
NEET answer keys round to the least-precise measurement. Rules: non-zero digits always count; leading zeros never; trailing zeros count only if a decimal is present; exact conversion factors (e.g., 1000 mL = 1 L) are infinitely precise. Multiplication/division keeps the **fewest** sig figs; addition/subtraction keeps the fewest **decimal places**.

#### Common Pitfalls
- Confusing **molarity** (volume-based) with **molality** (mass-based); equal only for dilute aqueous solutions near 4 °C.
- Using **22.4 L** at conditions other than STP — must use PV = nRT or the SATP value 24.8 L.
- Treating **mass percent** of solute as needing density of solvent only, ignoring total solution mass.
- Misidentifying the limiting reagent by comparing masses rather than moles per coefficient.
- Forgetting that empirical formula may equal the molecular formula for many simple compounds (H₂O, HCl, NH₃).

#### Connection to Other Topics
Stoichiometry feeds directly into **redox reactions, solutions, electrolysis (Faraday's laws = equivalents in action), and chemical kinetics (rate expressions)**. Mole-fraction calculations reappear in **thermodynamics (chemical potential)** and **equilibrium constants (Kₚ ↔ K꜀ via Δn)**.

#### Practice Prompts
1. A compound contains C = 40%, H = 6.67%, O = 53.33% with molar mass 180 g mol⁻¹. Determine empirical and molecular formulae.
2. 20 mL of a gaseous hydrocarbon requires 130 mL O₂ for complete combustion, producing 80 mL CO₂ (all volumes at same T, P). Find the hydrocarbon formula.
