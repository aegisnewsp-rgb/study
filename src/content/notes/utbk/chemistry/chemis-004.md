# Colligative Properties

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your UTBK/SNPMTN Chemistry paper.

**Solutions and Equilibria** form Topic 4 in the SNPMTN chemistry grid, covering colligative properties, acid–base equilibria, buffer systems, salt hydrolysis, and solubility (Ksp). The high-yield formulas are:

- ΔTf = Kf · m (freezing-point depression) and ΔTb = Kb · m (boiling-point elevation)
- π = M · R · T (osmotic pressure, R = 0.082 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)
- pH = −log[H⁺]; for a weak acid, [H⁺] = √(Ka · Ca)
- Ksp = [M^n+]^m [X^p−]^p for the salt M_mX_p
- For electrolytes, multiply molality by the van 't Hoff factor i = 1 + α(n−1)

UTBK typically tests 1–2 numerical items from this cluster. Watch the i-factor trap with strong vs. weak electrolytes.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Colligative Properties

Colligative properties depend only on the number — not the identity — of dissolved particles. For non-electrolytes the formulas in the Lite tier apply directly. For electrolytes such as NaCl or CaCl₂, particles dissociate, so you must apply the van 't Hoff factor i. For a salt yielding n ions with degree of dissociation α, i = 1 + α(n−1).

#### Acid–Base Equilibria and pH

- **Strong acid/base:** fully dissociates, so [H⁺] equals the analytical concentration.
- **Weak acid:** partial dissociation; [H⁺] = √(Ka · Ca), valid when α < 5%.
- **Weak base:** [OH⁻] = √(Kb · Cb), then convert via Kw = 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C.

> UTBK trap: students equate pH of a 0.1 M HCl solution with that of 0.1 M CH₃COOH — the weak acid gives a higher (less acidic) pH.

#### Buffers and Hydrolysis

A **buffer** resists pH change because it contains a weak acid and its conjugate base (or weak base + conjugate acid). The Henderson–Hasselbalch form is pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]). A salt of a weak acid + strong base (e.g. CH₃COONa) hydrolyses to give pH > 7; a salt of a weak base + strong acid (e.g. NH₄Cl) hydrolyses to give pH < 7.

#### Solubility and Ksp

The solubility product links directly to molar solubility s. For CaF₂ dissolving as Ca²⁺ + 2F⁻, Ksp = [Ca²⁺][F⁻]² = (s)(2s)² = 4s³. A **common ion** (e.g. adding F⁻ from NaF) suppresses s — a classic UTBK question.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Common Mistakes

1. **i-factor vs. dilution.** α increases as concentration decreases; for very dilute strong electrolytes α → 1, so i approaches the theoretical n.
2. **Polyprotic acids.** H₃PO₄ contributes H⁺ mainly from the first dissociation; pH ≈ ½(pKa₁ − log Ca). Subsequent pKa values matter only for buffer problems.
3. **Ksp stoichiometry.** For Ca₃(PO₄)₂: Ksp = [Ca²⁺]³[PO₄³⁻]² = (3s)³(2s)² = 108 s⁵ — missing the exponents is the most common UTBK error.
4. **Le Chatelier links.** Adding a common ion shifts dissolution backward; removing an ion (by complexation or reaction) increases s — relevant when Ksp is connected to pH (e.g. Ca(OH)₂ dissolves more in acidic solution).

#### Worked Micro-Example

> Question: What is the pH of a buffer containing 0.20 mol/L CH₃COOH and 0.30 mol/L CH₃COONa, given Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵?

Using pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]) → pKa = −log(1.8 × 10⁻⁵) = 4.74. Then pH = 4.74 + log(0.30/0.20) = 4.74 + 0.18 = **4.92**. The ratio (not absolute concentrations) determines pH — a buffer principle examiners love to test.

#### Two Practice Prompts

- A solution is made by dissolving 5.85 g NaCl in 500 g water. Predict ΔTf (Kf of water = 1.86 °C·kg/mol), assuming i ≈ 2.
- For AgCl (Ksp = 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰), calculate the molar solubility in pure water and in 0.10 M NaCl. Compare.
