# Consumer Behaviour

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Consumer behaviour** studies how individuals allocate scarce money across goods to maximise **utility** subject to a budget constraint. The two core analytical tools are **Cardinal Utility** (measurable utils, governed by the **Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility**) and **Ordinal Utility** (preference ranking via **Indifference Curves** and **Budget Lines**).

- **Consumer equilibrium (cardinal):** **MUx/Px = MUy/Py**, with **MU** falling as quantity rises.
- **Consumer equilibrium (ordinal):** **MRSxy = Px/Py**, where **MRS = MUx/MUy** is the slope of the indifference curve.
- **Total Price Effect** decomposes via the **Hicks** or **Slutsky** method into a **Substitution Effect** (always non-positive) and an **Income Effect** (sign depends on whether the good is normal, inferior, or Giffen).
- For RBI Grade B Phase-II Economics, expect one 10-mark descriptive question or a 2-mark MCQ on **Engel's Law**, **Giffen paradox**, or **Prospect Theory** — keep formulas above and the **Law of Demand** exception clear.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Cardinal Utility Framework

The classical approach treats utility as measurable. **Total Utility (TU)** is the aggregate satisfaction from a bundle, while **Marginal Utility (MU = ΔTU/ΔQ)** is the addition from one more unit. The **Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility** says MU declines as Q rises. **Consumer surplus**, defined as **Willingness to Pay − Price actually paid**, follows directly from the downward-sloping MU curve.

#### Ordinal Utility Framework

Modern analysis rejects cardinal measurement and uses **Indifference Curves (ICs)**, which slope downward (more of X means less of Y for the same satisfaction), are convex to the origin (diminishing **MRS**), and never intersect. The **Budget Line** is **Px·X + Py·Y = M**, with slope −Px/Py. The consumer chooses the IC tangent to the budget line, giving the tangency condition **MRS = Px/Py** and corner solutions where an IC meets the axis.

#### Price, Income and Cross Effects

The **Income Effect** is the change in quantity demanded when real income changes (holding relative prices constant); it is positive for **normal goods** and negative for **inferior goods**. The **Substitution Effect** is the change when relative prices change (holding utility constant); it is always negative (Law of Demand). For a **Giffen good**, an inferior good whose income effect overwhelms the substitution effect, the demand curve slopes upward.

#### Demand Elasticities

- **Price elasticity of demand: Ed = (%ΔQ) / (%ΔP).**
- **Income elasticity: Em = (%ΔQ) / (%ΔM);** classifies goods as normal (>0) or inferior (<0); luxury if Em > 1, necessity if 0 < Em < 1.
- **Cross elasticity: Exy = (%ΔQx) / (%ΔPy);** substitutes Exy > 0, complements Exy < 0.

#### Intertemporal Choice

**Friedman's Permanent Income Hypothesis (PIH)** sets consumption as a function of expected lifetime wealth: **C = (W + r·A)/(1+r)**, smoothing consumption across periods. **Modigliani's Life Cycle Hypothesis (LCH)** instead distributes wealth across a finite lifespan, producing low savings during working years and dissaving in retirement. The two models predict very different responses to transitory tax cuts.

#### Typical Exam Patterns

RBI Grade B tests consumer behaviour through (i) numericals on equilibrium with given MU schedules, (ii) graphical derivations of the Slutsky/Hicks decomposition, and (iii) short notes on **Engel's Law** (as income rises, the food budget share falls) or **Veblen/Giffen paradoxes**. Watch for the trap of writing MRS as MUx/MUy without specifying the *good on the numerator in the denominator's ratio* — mark it exactly as **MUx/MUy = Px/Py**.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Revealed Preference and Advanced Demand Theory

**Samuelson's Revealed Preference Theory** infers preferences from observed choices: if a bundle A is chosen over B when both are affordable, A is revealed preferred to B; the **Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference (WARP)** then forbids choosing B when A is later affordable. **Hicksian (compensated) demand** holds utility constant and isolates the substitution effect, while **Marshallian (ordinary) demand** holds money income constant; the **Slutsky equation** links them: **∂x/∂p = (∂xh/∂p)|U=ū − x·(∂x/∂m)**. A negative compensated price derivative is the rigorous proof of the Law of Demand.

#### Behavioural Economics

Standard theory assumes a rational, expected-utility-maximising agent. **Prospect Theory (Kahneman–Tversky)** replaces this with a value function **v(x)** that is concave for gains, convex for losses, and steeper for losses (loss aversion parameter **λ > 1**), combined with **probability weighting π(p)** that overweights small probabilities and underweights large ones. Key phenomena tested by RBI:

- **Endowment Effect:** owners demand more to give up a good than they would pay to acquire it, violating **Coase's** zero-transaction-cost prediction.
- **Mental Accounting (Thaler):** consumers code, label, and segregate money non-fungibly across accounts.
- **Hyperbolic Discounting:** preferences follow **β·δ^t/(1+β·δ^t)** rather than exponential discounting, generating **time inconsistency** (e.g. preference for immediate gratification).
- **Anchoring, Framing, Status Quo Bias, Decision Fatigue, Bounded Rationality (Simon, with satisficing)** all challenge the textbook consumer.

#### Connected Topics and Edge Cases

Consumer behaviour dovetails with **producer behaviour** via the **duality** of cost and expenditure functions (Shephard's Lemma recovers input demands from the cost function, mirroring Roy's Identity recovering Marshallian demands from indirect utility). For policy questions, RBI expects awareness of the **Demonstration Effect** (Duesenberry: consumption shaped by peer-group spending), the **Bandwagon, Snob and Veblen Effects** on demand, and **Engel curves** tracing Qx against M for expenditure-pattern analysis. **Paradox of Choice** (Schwartz) warns that excessive options reduce welfare — relevant for designing digital banking interfaces.

#### Common Mistakes

1. Conflating income and substitution effects when the good is inferior; the substitution effect must be drawn separately from the total effect.
2. Writing Engel's Law as "income falls" — it actually states the **budget share** for food declines as income rises.
3. Treating **hedonic adaptation** as identical to **cognitive dissonance**; the former is utility returning to baseline, the latter is post-purchase rationalisation.

#### Practice Prompts

1. A consumer has MUx = 20 − 2X and MUy = 30 − 3Y, with Px = ₹4, Py = ₹5, M = ₹100. Find the equilibrium bundle and the maximum TU. (Hint: equate MUx/Px = MUy/Py = λ, then use the budget constraint.)
2. Using a clearly labelled diagram, separate the Slutsky income and substitution effects for an inferior good. Show explicitly why the Slutsky substitution effect is always non-positive even when the total effect is positive (Giffen case).
