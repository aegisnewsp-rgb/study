# Venn Diagrams

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A Venn diagram pictures set relationships using overlapping circles inside a rectangle that stands for the **universal set U**. Placement of an element inside or outside a circle tells you membership; the overlap holds elements common to two or more sets.

- **Union** (A ∪ B): everything inside either circle.
- **Intersection** (A ∩ B): the lens-shaped overlap.
- **Complement** (A′): the area inside U but outside A.
- **Disjoint sets**: A ∩ B = ∅, circles do not touch.

For LAT MCQs, remember the inclusion–exclusion identity: **n(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∩ B)**. For categorical syllogisms, draw the **middle term first**, then mark the third term, and read whether the conclusion is forced.

---

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Drawing the diagram

Always start with a rectangle for U. For two-set problems, two overlapping circles suffice. For three-set syllogisms, three circles A, B, C must be drawn so that each pair overlaps and a small central region holds A ∩ B ∩ C. Place the **middle term** (the term appearing in both premises) so its circle overlaps with the other premise's circle; the third term's circle is added last.

#### Marking categorical propositions

The four standard forms are encoded by either shading an excluded region or placing an **x** in an included region:

| Proposition | Mark in diagram |
| --- | --- |
| All A are B | Shade the part of A outside B |
| No A is B | Shade the entire A ∩ B region |
| Some A are B | Place x in A ∩ B |
| Some A are not B | Place x in A but outside B |

#### Validity test for a syllogism

If the two premises, once marked, already force the conclusion's region (correctly shaded or occupied by x), the syllogism is **valid**. If the conclusion's region is not forced — it could still be empty or occupied — the argument is **invalid**.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Two-circle identities and De Morgan's laws

Two-circle diagrams double as a quick check for set identities. Shading the region for A ∪ B and comparing with the region for (A′ ∩ B′)′ reveals De Morgan's law: **(A ∪ B)′ = A′ ∩ B′**. Likewise, (A ∩ B)′ = A′ ∪ B′. LAT questions occasionally ask which shaded region corresponds to a given compound expression.

#### Three-set inclusion–exclusion

For numeric problems involving three sets, the correct identity is **n(A ∪ B ∪ C) = n(A) + n(B) + n(C) − n(A∩B) − n(A∩C) − n(B∩C) + n(A∩B∩C)**. Each pairwise overlap is subtracted once, but the triple intersection was subtracted three times, so it is added back once.

#### Common traps examiners exploit

- **Wrong order of drawing**: putting the major term first makes the middle term's overlap impossible to read.
- **x-versus-shading confusion**: "Some A are B" needs an x, not shading; "All A are B" needs shading, not an x.
- **Disjoint vs. complementary**: A ∩ B = ∅ (no overlap) is not the same as A ∪ B = U (no region outside both).
- **Double-counting**: forgetting to subtract n(A ∩ B) inflates n(A ∪ B).

#### Worked micro-example

Suppose in a class of 100 students, 60 study Law (L), 50 study Accounting (A), and 20 study both. Then n(L ∪ A) = 60 + 50 − 20 = **90**, and 10 students study neither.

#### Practice prompts

1. In a three-circle diagram, "All B are C" and "Some A are B" are given. Is the conclusion "Some A are C" valid?
2. Of 70 LAT candidates, 40 passed English, 35 passed GK, and 10 passed both. How many passed at least one subject?
