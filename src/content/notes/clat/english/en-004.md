# Para Summary

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **Para Summary** question in CLAT English asks you to compress a 200–500 word passage into a single sentence (usually ≤35 words) that captures the central idea, preserves the author's tone, and discards supporting examples. The skill lives inside the Reading Comprehension section, which carries roughly **4% weight** (about 1 mark out of 30) and accompanies each long RC passage as one of its four sub-questions.

- **Topic sentence hunting**: read the first and last paragraphs first — central ideas usually anchor there.
- **Paraphrase, never copy**: lift the meaning, not the wording; verbatim phrases above ~6 words invite negative marking.
- **Tone preservation**: a critical passage demands a summary that signals criticism, not a neutral rewrite.

| Element | What to keep | What to drop |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Central idea | Author's main claim or argument | Examples, statistics, quotations |
| Tone | Critical / analytical / satirical markers | Personal asides or rhetorical flourishes |
| Voice | Third-person, neutral stance | "I think", "the passage talks about" |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### What examiners actually test

CLAT's Para Summary question is a single-option MCQ where four candidate sentences are offered and only one condenses the passage accurately. Since 2024–25, each RC set carries **4 questions** (main idea, inference, vocabulary-in-context, and one applied skill), and Para Summary ability overlaps heavily with the **main idea** item. You are rewarded for *precision*, not paraphrase fluency.

#### A four-step method

1. Skim the first and last paragraphs to locate the **topic sentence** carrying the central claim.
2. Read the middle paragraphs only to identify the author's **stance** (supportive, critical, comparative).
3. Mentally compress the passage by replacing each example with its *function* ("illustrates", "rejects", "contrasts").
4. Draft a single sentence under 35 words that names the subject, the claim, and the stance — then check word count.

#### Tone vs content

Two summaries may quote the same idea but only one survives if it preserves tone. A passage attacking populism must be summarised as *arguing against*, not merely *discussing*. **Logical connectors** (because, although, therefore) signal the argument's structure and earn marks by reflecting the author's reasoning chain.

| Common trap | Why it fails | Correct move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starts with "The passage discusses…" | Wastes 4 words on meta-framing | Lead with the subject and claim |
| Copies a full clause verbatim | Violates paraphrasing rule | Replace words with synonyms, keep grammar |
| Picks a supporting statistic as the main idea | Confuses example for thesis | Re-read the opening and closing sentences |
| Omits the author's stance | Loses half the mark | Insert a verb like "rejects", "defends", "compares" |

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge cases and high-difficulty patterns

The hardest Para Summary items hide the central idea in the **concluding paragraph** (a delayed-thesis structure common in Guardian and Indian Express opinion pieces). Train yourself to read the last paragraph first when the opening feels narrative or anecdotal. Another trap: passages with **two competing viewpoints** — the summary must name both sides and the author's leaning, not pick one.

Negative constructions trip up even strong candidates. "The author argues *against* blanket surveillance" becomes, if paraphrased carelessly, "The author argues *for* blanket surveillance" — a complete reversal. When the source verb is negative (rejects, denies, refutes), keep negation or use an antonymic verb (condemns instead of "does not support").

#### Worked micro-example

Source (paraphrased): *A 2024 study found that 60% of Indian law students rely on rote memorisation for the CLAT, yet the exam's 2024 revision shifted toward comprehension-based questions; the author warns that coaching models ignoring this shift will leave candidates underprepared.* A strong ≤35-word summary: *"The author critiques rote-heavy CLAT coaching, arguing that the exam's shift toward comprehension demands a fundamental rethink of preparation strategies."*

#### Practice prompts

1. Summarise a recent *The Hindu* editorial on judicial pendency in one sentence under 35 words, preserving its reformist tone.
2. Take a satirical *Guardian* piece, identify the target of the satire, and write a summary that signals mockery without quoting it.

#### Common mistakes catalogue

- **Fragmenting** the central idea across two sentences — examiners award zero if the box demands one sentence.
- **Exceeding** the word cap by packing in two supporting examples.
- **Neutralising** a persuasive passage into a flat statement of fact.
- **Parroting** the candidate option's wording instead of regenerating the summary from scratch.
