# Subject-Verb Agreement

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **verb** must match its **subject** in **number** (singular/plural) and **person** (first, second, third). In the present tense, a singular subject takes a verb ending in *-s/-es* (He **runs**), while a plural subject takes the base form (They **run**).

- Indefinite pronouns like *each, everyone, nobody, either, neither* are always singular and take a singular verb: *Everyone is here.*
- Ignore phrases between the subject and verb; they do not change the number: *The box of chocolates is on the table.*
- With *either…or / neither…nor*, the verb agrees with the **nearest** subject: *Neither the teacher nor the students are absent.*

| Subject type | Verb form | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Singular | Verb + *-s/-es* | The boy runs fast. |
| Plural | Base verb | The boys run fast. |
| Indefinite pronoun | Singular verb | Each of the girls is present. |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Definition and Scope
Subject-verb agreement, also called **concord**, is the rule that the verb in a clause must reflect the number and person of its subject. This rule governs all finite verbs in the present tense and is the most tested grammar skill in NCEE English error-spotting items.

#### The Core Rule
A singular subject takes a singular verb form, and a plural subject takes a plural verb form. In present-tense English, singular verbs typically end in *-s* (writes, plays, goes), while plural verbs use the base form (write, play, go). Pronouns must agree too: *I am*, *You are*, *He/She/It is*, *We/They are*.

#### Indefinite Pronouns
Words such as *each, every, anyone, everyone, somebody, nobody, either,* and *neither* are grammatically singular. Even when they precede a plural noun in a phrase (e.g., *each of the boys*), the verb remains singular: *Each of the boys **is** here.*

#### Intervening Phrases
Prepositional phrases, relative clauses, and appositives placed between the subject and verb do not alter the subject's number. The verb must agree with the true subject, not the nearest noun. Example: *The box of chocolates **is** on the table* (not *are*).

| Pattern | Rule | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Compound subject with *and* | Plural verb | Tom and Jerry are friends. |
| *or / nor* joining subjects | Verb agrees with nearest subject | Either the dog or the cats **make** noise. |
| Collective noun (unit) | Singular verb | The team **is** winning. |
| Collective noun (members acting separately) | Plural verb | The team **are** arguing among themselves. |
| Noun ending in *-s* but singular in meaning | Singular verb | The news **is** frightening. |

#### Common Traps in NCEE Sentence Items
- **Inverted sentences:** the subject comes after the verb (*Here comes the bus*). Do not let the introductory word mislead you.
- **Titles, sums, and labels:** treated as singular (*Hamlet is a tragedy; Fifty naira is too much*).
- **Subject vs. subject complement:** the verb agrees with the subject, not the complement (*The students are the cause*, not *The students is the cause*).

- Cross-check the true subject before choosing a verb.
- Watch for *-s* ending on nouns that are actually singular (*news, mathematics, physics*).
- Insert the verb into the blank mentally before deciding between *is/are* or *has/have*.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Tricky Constructions
Beyond the basic rule, NCEE items often exploit the following structures:

- **Each of / One of + plural noun:** the head is *each* or *one*, which is singular, so the verb is singular (*Each of the candidates has submitted a form*).
- **Either…or / Neither…nor:** when the two subjects differ in number, the verb matches the nearer subject (*Neither the students nor the teacher **is** absent* if teacher is nearer; *Neither the teacher nor the students **are** absent* if students is nearer).
- **Relative clauses:** pronouns like *who, which, that* must agree with their antecedents, not the nearer noun (*She is one of the teachers who **mark** strictly* — the relative clause refers to *teachers*, hence plural).
- **There is / There are:** the verb agrees with the true subject that follows (*There are five students; There is a problem*).

#### Worked Example
*Neither the principal nor the teachers (is / are) attending the meeting tomorrow.*
The verb must agree with the nearest subject, which is *teachers* (plural). The correct choice is **are**: *Neither the principal nor the teachers **are** attending the meeting tomorrow.*

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics
Subject-verb agreement links directly to **pronoun-antecedent agreement** (the same antecedent rules govern both), to **tense consistency** (concord must hold regardless of tense), and to **parallelism** in compound subjects joined by *and*, where the verb must be plural regardless of singular elements.

#### Common Mistakes
| Mistake | Correction | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The team are playing well (unit acting as one) | The team is playing well | Collective noun used as a unit takes singular verb |
| Everyone are ready | Everyone is ready | Indefinite pronoun is singular |
| The box of chocolates are here | The box of chocolates is here | The subject is *box*, not *chocolates* |
| The news are bad | The news is bad | *News* is singular in meaning despite the *-s* |

#### Practice Prompts
1. Choose the correct verb: *Each of the participants (has / have) submitted a form.*
2. Spot the error: *The committee are divided on the issue, but the chairman has the final say.*

#### Exam Strategy for NCEE English
With roughly 3% of the paper, agreement items usually appear as **1–2 sentence-completion or error-spotting questions**. Allocate under 30 seconds per item: locate the true subject, ignore intervening phrases, then apply the matching rule. Always test both singular and plural forms in your head before selecting the answer.
