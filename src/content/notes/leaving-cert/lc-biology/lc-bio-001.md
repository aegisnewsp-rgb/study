# Nature of Science — Leaving Certificate (Established) Biology Notes

Nature of Science is the unifying strand that runs through every other unit of the NCCA Leaving Certificate Biology specification. It bundles the habits of *investigating* (designing and conducting experiments with attention to controls, variables and replication), *reasoning* (interpreting data, distinguishing correlation from causation, evaluating evidence), *communicating* (drawing graphs correctly, writing lab reports, citing sources) and *engaging with the role of biology in society* (the ethical, medical, environmental and biotechnological implications of biological knowledge). The strand carries a weight rating of 3 out of 5 in the topic catalogue, but its marks are dispersed across every question on the SEC paper and across the mandatory practical assessment, so under-preparing it depresses marks in every other topic simultaneously.

At Higher level, Nature of Science is tested explicitly in dedicated short questions on Paper 1, in the experimental and graphical elements of the longer questions on both papers, and in the practical coursework components that contribute to the overall grade. At Ordinary level the same habits are tested but the mark scheme is more forgiving on the experimental-design vocabulary. The strand is also the section where the SEC examiners most reliably test historical context — the development of the microscope, the discovery of the structure of DNA, the history of vaccination — because these stories illustrate how biology actually *works* as a discipline.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Strand weight & spread**: Weight 3/5 in the topic catalogue. Marks are dispersed across every question on Paper 1 and Paper 2, plus the practical assessment — under-preparing it depresses every other unit.
- **Four pillars of the strand**: *Investigating, Reasoning, Communicating, Engaging with biology in society*. Every Nature of Science item on the SEC paper maps to one of these four pillars.
- **First move on an experimental question**: Identify the *independent variable* (what the experimenter changes), the *dependent variable* (what is measured) and the *controlled variables* (what is held constant) before reading the rest of the question. Most candidates skip this step and lose the lead-in mark.
- **Graph discipline**: Plot the independent variable on the x-axis and the dependent variable on the y-axis. Use a sharp pencil. Draw a line of best fit *only* when the relationship is linear; otherwise join the points with a smooth curve. Never extrapolate a line beyond the data without flagging it.
- **Correlation vs causation**: The mark scheme rewards the candidate who can state "the data show a correlation between X and Y, but the experiment does not establish causation". This single sentence is often the full mark on a "conclusion" question.
- **Control experiments**: A control is the experimental setup *without* the treatment being tested. If the experiment uses a new drug, the control uses a placebo. If the experiment tests light, the control is in darkness. Naming the control earns a discrete mark.
- **Historical case studies the strand reaches for**: the development of the microscope and the discovery of cells; Pasteur's experiments on spontaneous generation; the discovery of the structure of DNA (Watson, Crick, Franklin, Wilkins); the history of vaccination (Jenner, Pasteur); the discovery of penicillin (Fleming, Florey, Chain). Confirm the live case study list on curriculumonline.ie.
- **Ethics and biology in society**: The strand explicitly tests the candidate's ability to discuss the implications of biological research — genetic engineering, cloning, vaccination, antibiotic resistance, climate change, biodiversity loss. The mark scheme rewards a balanced view, not advocacy.

#### Examiner traps

- Designing an experiment without naming the variables. Naming the variables is the lead-in mark; candidates who skip it lose 20% of the experimental-design marks before designing anything.
- Drawing a graph with the axes swapped, or with non-uniform spacing on an axis. The mark scheme reads for axis discipline before it reads for the trend line.
- Confusing a control with a *controlled variable*. A control is a parallel experiment; a controlled variable is a parameter held constant. The vocabulary matters.
- Treating a historical case study as biographical detail. The strand tests whether the candidate can extract the *method* from the history — what did Pasteur *do* to disprove spontaneous generation, and why did the experiment work?
- Writing a one-sided "biology in society" answer. The mark scheme rewards the candidate who presents both sides and reaches a *considered* position; a partisan answer forfeits the engagement mark.

#### 30-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Can you identify the independent, dependent and controlled variables in an experiment in under 60 seconds?
- [ ] Can you name the four pillars of Nature of Science and give an example of each?
- [ ] Have you drawn at least one graph per week this month with correct axis labelling and units?
- [ ] Can you state one historical case study in biology and explain what *method* the scientist used?

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### Experimental design as a discrete skill

The SEC mark scheme reads experimental design as a sequence of marks, not as a single gestalt. A Higher-grade experimental-design answer walks through the marks in order:

1. **State the hypothesis** as a specific, testable claim — *not* as a question. "Increasing temperature increases the rate of enzyme activity up to the optimum" is a hypothesis; "Does temperature affect enzymes?" is a question.
2. **Name the variables** — independent, dependent, controlled. The variables list is the second mark.
3. **Describe the method** in enough detail that another student could replicate the experiment. The method mark is for *procedure*, not for equipment list.
4. **Identify the control**. The control is the experiment run *without* the treatment.
5. **State how the data will be analysed** — graph type, statistical test, expected trend. The analysis mark rewards candidates who name the analysis before the data is collected.
6. **Discuss sources of error**. The error mark rewards candidates who can name *specific* sources (parallax error, contamination, temperature fluctuation) rather than generic "human error".

Walking through these six steps in order earns the full experimental-design mark on a typical Higher-level item.

#### Graphical communication

Graph discipline is a recoverable cluster of marks. The SEC mark scheme reads graphs in a fixed order:

- **Axes**: Independent variable on x-axis, dependent on y-axis, both labelled with units. The axes mark is forfeit if either label or unit is missing.
- **Scale**: Uniform spacing on both axes, starting from zero unless the data makes that impossible. The scale mark is forfeit if the spacing is non-uniform.
- **Points**: Plotted accurately, with a sharp pencil, encircled or marked with a cross. The points mark is forfeit if the dots are smudged or off the gridlines.
- **Line of best fit**: A single straight line (for linear relationships) or a smooth curve (for non-linear relationships). The line mark is forfeit if the line is drawn dot-to-dot or zig-zagged through the points.
- **Title**: A descriptive title that names both variables. The title mark is often overlooked but is explicitly credited.

A candidate who has practised drawing graphs to this discipline for a month before the mock will pick up marks that candidates who have not practised will forfeit on every question that asks for a graph.

#### Reasoning with data

The reasoning pillar of Nature of Science tests whether a candidate can interpret a dataset, identify a trend, draw a conclusion and qualify that conclusion appropriately. The mark scheme rewards four reasoning moves:

1. **State the trend** — *what* the data show, without interpretation. "As X increases, Y increases" is a trend statement; "X causes Y" is an interpretation.
2. **Quantify the trend** — using numbers from the data where possible. "Y rises from 12 to 48 as X rises from 1 to 5" is a quantified trend.
3. **Qualify the conclusion** — by stating what the data *do not* show. "The data show a correlation, but the experiment does not establish causation" is the qualification move.
4. **Suggest a follow-up** — by naming the next experiment that would strengthen the conclusion. "Repeating the experiment with a wider range of X values would clarify the trend at the extremes" is the follow-up move.

Candidates who make all four moves on a Higher-level reasoning question reliably earn the top band on that question.

#### Biology in society

The "biology in society" pillar is the strand's distinctive mark — the bit that asks the candidate to step outside the lab and consider the *implications* of biological research. The most consistently tested topics are:

- **Genetic engineering and biotechnology**: the modification of organisms for medical, agricultural or industrial purposes; the ethical and ecological implications.
- **Vaccination and immunity**: the role of vaccines in public health; the concept of herd immunity; vaccine hesitancy as a social phenomenon.
- **Antibiotic resistance**: the evolution of resistance; the implications for human health; the role of prescribing practice.
- **Climate change and biodiversity**: the biological evidence for anthropogenic climate change; the implications for ecosystems and conservation.
- **Reproductive technologies**: IVF, cloning, genetic screening; the ethical frameworks for evaluating these technologies.

The mark scheme rewards the candidate who can present *both sides* of the issue and reach a *considered* position. A partisan answer forfeits the engagement mark.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### The mandatory practical assessment

The Leaving Certificate Biology specification includes a mandatory practical assessment that contributes to the overall grade. The exact format and weighting of the practical component should be confirmed on examinations.ie each year, as the SEC publishes the current arrangements annually. The practical assessment typically includes a laboratory component (where candidates carry out a prescribed experiment and report on it) and an aural component (where candidates may be examined on their understanding of experimental technique).

A candidate who has practised the practical component consistently through fifth and sixth year will find this section recoverable; a candidate who has neglected the practical component until the mock cycle will find it the lowest-yielding section of the paper. The preparation is straightforward: every practical lesson, write a full lab report (aim, method, results, conclusion, evaluation) in the discipline the SEC mark scheme reads for, and the practical assessment becomes a routine mark pickup rather than a feared one.

#### Historical case studies and the history of biology

The strand's reach into historical case studies is one of the most consistent features of the SEC paper. The most-tested cases:

- **The discovery of cells** (Hooke, Leeuwenhoek, Schleiden, Schwann, Virchow): illustrates how the development of the microscope transformed biology.
- **Spontaneous generation and Pasteur**: illustrates how a controlled experiment can refute a long-standing hypothesis.
- **The discovery of the structure of DNA** (Watson, Crick, Franklin, Wilkins): illustrates how model-building, X-ray crystallography and base-pairing rules converged on a structure.
- **The history of vaccination** (Jenner's cowpox vaccine, Pasteur's attenuated vaccines): illustrates how empirical observation can lead to generalisable methods.
- **The discovery of penicillin** (Fleming, Florey, Chain): illustrates how chance observation followed by systematic development produces a medical treatment.

The mark scheme reads for the *method* — what did the scientist actually *do* — and for the *implication* — what did the discovery *change*. A candidate who has rehearsed three or four of these cases with the method-and-implication structure will pick up the history marks on any SEC paper.

#### Common mistakes that depress the grade

- **The "naked variables" failure mode** — naming the variables but not the units in which they are measured. "Temperature" is incomplete; "temperature in °C" is complete.
- **The "line of best fit dot-to-dot" failure mode** — joining every data point with a straight line. The mark scheme reads for a *single* line of best fit, not a zig-zag.
- **The "control confused with controlled variable" failure mode** — naming the controlled variables in an answer that asks for the control. The vocabulary distinction is a mark.
- **The "biographical essay" failure mode** — writing a paragraph on Watson and Crick's lives instead of a paragraph on how they used model-building to deduce the DNA structure. The strand is about *method*, not biography.
- **The "one-sided society essay" failure mode** — writing a partisan answer on a biology-in-society question. The mark scheme rewards balance and qualification.
