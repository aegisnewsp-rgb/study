# Algebra — Leaving Certificate (Established) Mathematics Notes

Algebra is the largest strand in the Leaving Certificate Mathematics syllabus at Higher level by mark count, carrying weight 5 in the catalogue — the joint highest alongside Functions. It is the strand where the SEC paper's mid-section and the end-of-paper extended-response questions concentrate, and it is the strand that determines whether a candidate sits comfortably on H3 or is fighting for H4. At Ordinary level the strand is more procedural: linear equations, simultaneous equations, quadratic equations, simple sequences, and basic inequalities. At Higher level it extends to inequalities with regions, sequences and series (arithmetic and geometric), the binomial theorem, and algebraic proofs including induction.

What makes Algebra the highest-leverage strand to revise is that its skills transfer across to Functions (calculus), Statistics (regression), Geometry (coordinate geometry) and Number (financial mathematics via algebraic models). A candidate who is fluent in factorisation, in solving simultaneous equations and in handling quadratic expressions recovers marks across every other strand by extension. Conversely, a candidate whose algebraic fluency is shaky bleeds marks across the entire paper, not just in the dedicated algebra items.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Strand weight & spread**: Weight 5/5 in the catalogue — joint largest. Marks are spread across both papers at Ordinary and Higher level; expect at least two algebra items on each paper plus additional marks contributed to functions, statistics and geometry questions.
- **Levels that include each sub-topic**: Linear equations, simultaneous equations, quadratic equations, basic sequences and basic inequalities appear at all three levels. Inequalities with regions, sequences and series (sum to n terms), the binomial theorem and algebraic proofs are Higher level only.
- **First move on any algebra question**: Simplify before solving. A question that begins "solve x² − 3x + 2 = 0" can sometimes be factorised; a question that begins "solve 2x² − 6x + 4 = 0" can be divided by 2 first. The first move is the highest-yield move.
- **Quadratic formula**: x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a. Use when factorisation fails or when the question provides a, b, c directly. The discriminant b² − 4ac tells you whether the roots are real and distinct (positive), real and equal (zero), or complex (negative).
- **Factorisation**: difference of squares (a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b)); trinomials (x² + bx + c = (x + p)(x + q) where p + q = b and pq = c); common factor extraction. Each is a one-line operation that should be automatic.
- **Simultaneous equations**: substitution works when one equation is already solved for one variable; elimination works when coefficients match. Two equations, two unknowns, two solutions.
- **Inequalities (Higher)**: solve as if =, then flip the sign if multiplying/dividing by a negative number. For quadratic inequalities, sketch the parabola and read off where it is above/below the x-axis.
- **Sequences (Higher)**: arithmetic sequences have a common difference d, geometric sequences have a common ratio r. The general term and the sum to n terms formula differ for each — learn both.
- **Binomial theorem (Higher)**: (a + b)^n expands to Σ C(n,r) a^(n−r) b^r. For (1 + x)^n with x small, the first few terms give an approximation; this is tested at Higher level for the expansion and the approximation.

#### Examiner traps

- Dividing an equation by a variable without first checking that the variable is non-zero — division by zero is undefined and the resulting equation is missing a solution.
- Forgetting that |x| = 3 has *two* solutions (x = 3 and x = −3), not one. The modulus sign doubles the solution set.
- Confusing the *n*th term of a sequence with the *sum to n terms*. The former is one term; the latter is the cumulative total.
- Multiplying out (x − 3)² incorrectly as x² − 9 instead of x² − 6x + 9. The cross term is a recurring Higher-level error.
- Solving an inequality by setting one side to zero and forgetting to flip the sign on division by a negative coefficient.
- Using the binomial theorem without checking the validity of the approximation — if x is large, the higher-order terms are not negligible.

#### 30-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Can you factorise a trinomial, apply the quadratic formula, and complete the square from memory?
- [ ] Can you solve simultaneous linear equations by both substitution and elimination?
- [ ] Can you write the general term and sum-to-n formulas for both arithmetic and geometric sequences?
- [ ] Can you expand (a + b)^n using the binomial theorem for any integer n?

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### Quadratic equations and the discriminant

Quadratic equations form the spine of the Algebra strand at both Ordinary and Higher levels. Three solution routes are tested: factorisation, completing the square and the quadratic formula. Factorisation is the fastest when it works; candidates should look for the trinomial pattern first. When factorisation fails, completing the square is the route that produces the vertex form of the quadratic, which is useful for graphing. The quadratic formula is the universal route — it works in every case, including those where the roots are irrational or complex.

The discriminant b² − 4ac tells you the *nature* of the roots without solving the equation. Positive discriminant: two distinct real roots. Zero discriminant: one repeated real root. Negative discriminant: two complex conjugate roots. Higher-level questions often ask the candidate to determine the value of a parameter that makes the discriminant equal to a specified value — candidates must substitute the parameter into a, b or c, then solve the resulting equation in the parameter.

#### Simultaneous equations and their extensions

Simultaneous linear equations at Ordinary level are tested in their standard form: two equations, two unknowns, one solve. At Higher level the exam extends to *one linear and one quadratic* equation: substitute the linear expression into the quadratic, solve the resulting quadratic, then back-substitute to find the corresponding linear variable. The procedural skill is the same as for two linear equations; the navigation skill is to recognise which variable to eliminate first.

A common Higher-level extension is the word problem that produces two equations in two unknowns: a chemistry dilution problem, a cost-revenue break-even problem, an age-and-rate problem. The candidate must (a) define variables clearly, (b) translate each English sentence into an equation, (c) solve. Marks are awarded for each step; candidates who skip the variable-definition lose the lead-in mark.

#### Sequences, series and the binomial theorem (Higher)

Sequences and series at Higher level cover two families: arithmetic and geometric. Arithmetic sequences have a common difference d (each term differs from the previous by d); geometric sequences have a common ratio r (each term is r times the previous). The exam tests three operations on each family: finding the general term, finding a specific term, and finding the sum to n terms.

The arithmetic sum S_n = (n/2)(2a + (n−1)d) = (n/2)(first + last). The geometric sum S_n = a(1 − r^n)/(1 − r) for r ≠ 1. Geometric series are tested in two variants: *finite* sum and *infinite* sum. The infinite sum converges only when |r| < 1; the value is S_∞ = a/(1 − r). Candidates who attempt an infinite sum with |r| > 1 forfeit the mark and lose the interpretation credit.

The binomial theorem at Higher level is tested in two variants: full expansion and partial expansion. The full expansion of (a + b)^n uses Σ C(n,r) a^(n−r) b^r. The partial expansion of (1 + x)^n for small x retains only the first three or four terms and approximates the value of (1 + x)^n. Candidates must check the validity of the approximation before applying it — the examiner's mark depends on identifying the conditions under which the approximation is valid.

#### Algebraic proofs and induction (Higher)

Algebraic proofs at Higher level include the technique of *proof by mathematical induction*. The structure is fixed: (1) show the statement holds for n = 1; (2) assume the statement holds for n = k; (3) show that the statement holds for n = k + 1 by adding the (k + 1)th term to both sides; (4) conclude the statement holds for all positive integers n. Marks are awarded for each step, with the assumption step and the conclusion step each worth one mark.

The most common Higher-level error is conflating steps (2) and (3): the candidate writes "assume the statement holds for n = k + 1" which destroys the logic of the proof. The assumption is always for n = k; the goal is to show it for n = k + 1.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Full Mastery (1mo+)

#### Higher-level inequalities with regions

Inequalities with regions at Higher level extend the one-variable inequality to two variables. The candidate must graph a line on the coordinate plane, identify which side satisfies the inequality, and shade the region. The procedural skill is the same as for one-variable inequalities; the new skill is recognising that the line is the boundary and the shaded region is the solution set.

A typical Higher-level question presents two or three linear inequalities and asks the candidate to identify the feasible region, then find the maximum or minimum of a linear objective function. This is the foundation of linear programming; the SEC paper tests it with two or three constraints and an objective function of the form z = ax + by. Candidates who can sketch the feasible region and identify the corner points are well-prepared; the optimisation step then reduces to evaluating z at each corner.

#### Sequences, series and the binomial theorem at the highest level

At the highest level, sequences and series extend to *recurrence relations*: define u_1 and u_(n+1) = f(u_n), then find a specific term, prove by induction that a closed form exists, or investigate the long-term behaviour of the sequence. The procedural skill is to compute the first few terms and look for a pattern; the proof step requires induction.

The binomial theorem at the highest level is tested in conjunction with sequences: find the coefficient of x^k in the expansion of (1 + x)^n, given that the coefficient is a specific number. Candidates must set up the equation using the binomial coefficient formula and solve for n. The procedural skill is the same as for the standard expansion; the new skill is interpreting the coefficient as an unknown.

#### Common mistakes catalogue

1. **Quadratic formula sign error**: writing x = (b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a instead of x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a. The negative sign on b is easy to miss.
2. **Completing the square arithmetic**: dividing by the leading coefficient incorrectly or forgetting to add the constant that completes the square.
3. **Geometric sum infinite case**: applying S_∞ = a/(1 − r) when |r| ≥ 1. The series diverges and the formula does not apply.
4. **Binomial expansion signs**: writing (a − b)^n with all positive signs. The alternating signs come from the (−b)^r term; candidates who drop the alternating pattern lose the marks for each affected term.
5. **Induction assumption error**: assuming for n = k + 1 instead of for n = k. The logic of induction requires assuming the statement holds at k and proving it at k + 1.

#### Long-run mastery schedule

A four-week pre-mock plan for Algebra at Higher level:

- **Week 1**: Drill the quadratic formula, factorisation and completing the square on ten trinomials per day. Time each set to 15 minutes; the skill must be automatic.
- **Week 2**: Work every past paper's simultaneous equations question — both the standard form and the linear-plus-quadratic form. Then move to the inequalities-with-regions items.
- **Week 3**: Drill sequences and series. Cover arithmetic, geometric, finite sum and infinite sum variants. Then move to the binomial theorem: full expansion, partial expansion, and coefficient-solving problems.
- **Week 4**: Mock-exam week. Solve three past papers under timed conditions; mark against the marking scheme; write the "errors I keep making" list; re-drill those errors specifically. Confirm the live paper pattern on examinations.ie, particularly the placement of induction questions and the inclusion of recurrence relations.
