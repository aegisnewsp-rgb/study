# Politics in the TNPSC Group 1 syllabus

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Politics in the TNPSC Group 1 syllabus is the study of the **state**, **government**, **power**, and **authority**, blending Political Theory, Indian Polity, and Comparative Politics. The single most-tested idea is the distinction between **State** (an abstract, continuing political association with territory, population, government, and sovereignty) and **Government** (the temporary agency that runs the state). Weber's three types of legitimate **authority** — **traditional**, **charismatic**, and **rational-legal** — recur every cycle.

- **State** = permanent institution; **Government** = its changing agent; **Nation** = cultural community. Never use them interchangeably.
- **Sovereignty** in India rests with the **Constitution**, not Parliament alone — judicial review and Fundamental Rights bind the legislature.
- **FPTP** is the Lok Sabha system; proportional representation is NOT used for Indian state assemblies.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Conceptual Frame

Politics examines how power is acquired, exercised, and justified. Political Science is divided into Political Theory (normative questions about justice, liberty, equality), Indian Polity (institutional study of the Indian Constitution and governance), and Comparative Politics (cross-national study of regimes). Together they form the backbone of TNPSC Group 1 General Studies Paper I.

#### Power, Authority, and Legitimacy

Max Weber classified **legitimate authority** into three pure types. Each type justifies obedience differently, and examiner questions often ask you to match a historical example to the right type.

| Type of Authority | Basis of Obedience | Classic Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Traditional | Long-established customs and hereditary status | Monarchy, village headman |
| Charismatic | Personal qualities of an exceptional leader | Gandhi's mass following |
| Rational-Legal | Codified rules and impersonal institutions | Elected Parliament, civil service |

#### State vs Government vs Nation

The **State** is a sovereign political association occupying defined territory. The **Government** is the executive organ currently wielding state power. The **Nation** is a psychological community bound by shared culture, language, or history. A new government does not create a new state, but a nation may exist without statehood (e.g., the Kurdish movement).

#### Indian Polity Anchors

- The **Preamble** declares India a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic.
- **Fundamental Rights** (Part III) are justiciable; **Directive Principles** (Part IV, Art. 36–51) are non-justiciable but guide state policy.
- India is a **quasi-federal** union with a strong centre; the Constitution (not Parliament) is supreme.
- **Lok Sabha and Vidhan Sabha** elections use First-Past-the-Post, not proportional representation.

#### Common Exam Traps

Conflating rights as absolute, treating Parliament as sovereign, swapping FPTP with PR, and confusing DPSP with Fundamental Rights cost easy marks.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Sovereignty: Three Faces

Sovereignty is not monolithic. **Legal sovereignty** resides in the body whose laws are finally authoritative (in India, the Parliament acting within constitutional limits). **Political sovereignty** rests with the people exercised through elections. **Popular sovereignty** is the philosophical claim that ultimate authority belongs to the citizens. In India the supremacy of the Constitution reconciles these — Parliament cannot override Fundamental Rights, so legal sovereignty is constitutionally bounded.

#### Theories of the State

Different traditions answer "what is the state for?" differently.

| Theory | View of the State | Key Thinker |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Liberal | Limited state protecting individual rights | J.S. Mill, Locke |
| Marxist | Instrument of class domination | Karl Marx |
| Gandhian | Decentralised, village-based, non-violent | M. K. Gandhi |
| Pluralist | Arena of competing interest groups | Robert Dahl |

#### Comparative Politics at a Glance

- **Unitary vs Federal** — India is federal in structure with strong unitary features (Art. 356, single judiciary, All-India Services).
- **Parliamentary vs Presidential** — India follows the Westminster parliamentary model where the executive is drawn from the legislature; the USA uses a strict separation.
- **Electoral systems** — FPTP rewards concentrated regional majorities; PR rewards ideological breadth. India rejected PR for Lok Sabha in 1970.

#### Connections and Pitfalls

This topic feeds directly into Constitutional Framework, Public Administration, and Ethics questions in TNPSC Group 1. Linking thinkers to concepts is the most-tested micro-skill: Hobbes (Leviathan, absolute sovereignty), Locke (natural rights, limited government), Rousseau (general will, popular sovereignty), Ambedkar (drafted Part III and IV, opposed unitary bias), Kautilya (Arthashastra on statecraft).

#### Worked Distinction

> **Trap:** "Parliament is sovereign in India."
> **Correction:** Sovereignty belongs to the Constitution. Parliament is supreme only within constitutional limits — hence the basic structure doctrine (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973).

#### Practice Prompts

1. Distinguish between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles with one constitutional article each, and explain why DPSP are non-justiciable.
2. Match the following thinkers to their key concept: Hobbes, Gandhi, Ambedkar, Marx — and justify each pairing in two lines.
