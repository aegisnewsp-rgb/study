# Permutations

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Permutations** count **ordered** arrangements of *r* objects chosen from *n* distinct objects, without repetition. The workhorse formula is **nPr = n! / (n − r)!**, where *n* and *r* are dimensionless integers with r ≤ n.

When all *n* objects are arranged at once, **P(n, n) = n!**. Around a circle where only rotations are identical, divide by *n* to get **(n − 1)!**; on a necklace where reflections also coincide, divide by **2n** for n ≥ 3.

With repetition allowed (e.g., forming *r*-letter "words" from *n* letters), the count jumps to **n^r** — a different mechanism entirely.

#### High-yield CAT pointers:

- **Order matters:** swap two chosen items and the arrangement changes.
- **Identical objects:** divide *n!* by *p! q! r!* for subgroups of size *p, q, r*.
- **Circular always divides** linear counts by *n*.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Formula and Factorial Behaviour

The number of ways to arrange *r* distinct objects from a pool of *n* is
nPr = n! / (n − r)!.

Expanding, nPr = n × (n − 1) × (n − 2) × … × (n − r + 1). The formula starts with *n* and walks down *r* steps. Setting r = n gives n!; setting r = 0 gives 0! / n! = 1 (by definition **0! = 1**). Factorials explode super-exponentially — a fact CAT uses to reward clean formula substitution over brute multiplication.

#### Order vs Selection

The decisive test is swapping: if swapping two chosen items produces a different arrangement, count permutations. Forming a 3-digit code from five distinct digits is **nPr**; choosing 3 committee members from 5 is **C(n, r)**.

#### Circular Permutations

Around a round table, rotate every seating by one position and you get the "same" arrangement. So divide linear *n!* by *n*, yielding **(n − 1)!**. On a **necklace**, flipping the string also produces identity, so for n ≥ 3 the count halves again to **(n − 1)! / 2**.

#### Identical Objects

When the *n* objects split into repeated subgroups of size *p, q, r*, the count is **n! / (p! q! r! …)**. The letters of "MISSISSIPPI" — 4 S's, 4 I's, 2 P's, 1 M — yield 11! / (4! 4! 2! 1!) arrangements.

#### Exam Question Patterns

CAT typically tests permutations through:

1. Direct nPr computation (slot-1 speed check).
2. Multi-step arrangements blended into a 4-question set.
3. Circular/restricted-position word problems merged with probability in a single set.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Restricted Arrangements: Two Engines

When a constraint forces certain objects together or apart, two engines apply.

The **binding method** glues the constrained objects into a single super-object; the count becomes (n−k+1)! times k! for arranging the bound block internally. Example: arrange A, B, C, D, E so that A and B sit together. Treat {AB} as one unit → 4! arrangements of four units × 2! internal orders = 48.

The **gap method** solves "no two vowels together" by placing consonants first, then choosing slots between and at the ends for the vowels. For *c* consonants and *v* vowels, there are *c+1* gaps, giving *P(c+1, v)* ways.

#### Repetition vs Non-Repetition

| Scenario | Formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Choose *r* of *n* distinct, order matters | nPr = n!/(n−r)! | 5-digit codes from 10 digits |
| Form *r*-slot word from *n* types, repetition allowed | n^r | 4-letter strings from {A, B, C} |
| Arrange *n* with repeated subgroups | n!/(p! q! …) | Anagrams of MISSISSIPPI |
| Circle of *n* (rotation only) | (n−1)! | Round-table seating |
| Necklace of *n* (rotation + reflection) | (n−1)!/2, n ≥ 3 | Beads on a string |

#### Common Traps

- **Forgetting the divide by k!** in circular arrangements — every rotation is the same.
- **Mixing up repetition rules**: codes with repetition use n^r, not nPr.
- **Off-by-one in gaps**: with *c* consonants in a row, there are *c*+1 slots, not *c*.
- **Treating combinations as permutations** when the question says "in order" or "code/password".

#### Worked Micro-Example

How many 3-digit codes from {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} have **all distinct digits** and the **first digit even**? Even digits: {2, 4, 6}; choose first → 3 ways; remaining two positions from the 5 leftover digits, distinct → P(5, 2) = 20; total = 60.

#### Practice Prompts

1. In how many ways can 4 boys and 4 girls sit around a table so that no two girls sit together?
2. How many 5-letter "words" can be formed from the letters of BANANA if each word must start with a vowel?
