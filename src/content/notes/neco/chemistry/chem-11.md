# Organic Chemistry: Alkanes and Alkenes

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Alkanes** are *saturated* aliphatic hydrocarbons — only single C–C and C–H sigma (σ) bonds — with the general formula **CₙH₂ₙ₊₂** (methane CH₄, ethane C₂H₆, propane C₃H₈). The suffix is **-ane** and each member differs from the next by a –CH₂– unit.

**Alkenes** are *unsaturated* aliphatic hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon–carbon double bond (**C=C**), written as **CₙH₂ₙ** for monoenes (ethene C₂H₄, propene C₃H₆). Each C=C is one σ bond plus one π bond; the π bond blocks rotation, which allows **cis-trans (geometric) isomerism**.

| Property | Alkanes | Alkenes |
|---|---|---|
| Bonding | All σ bonds | One σ + one π per C=C |
| General formula | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ | CₙH₂ₙ |
| Reaction type | **Substitution** (UV light needed) | **Addition** across C=C |
| Bromine water (room temp, dark) | No reaction | Decolourises rapidly |
| Acidified KMnO₄ | No reaction | Decolourises (Baeyer's test) |

> **NECO must-know:** the alkene test with bromine in CCl₄ is the single most-tested practical distinction — it works *without* UV light.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Homologous Series & Nomenclature
Both families are **homologous series**: each consecutive member adds one –CH₂– unit. IUPAC rules use prefixes (meth-, eth-, prop-, but-, pent-, hex-) plus the suffix **-ane** (alkanes) or **-ene** (alkenes), with the locant placed immediately before the suffix (e.g. *but-2-ene*, not 2-butene, in modern NECO-marked scripts). For alkanes, *n* ≥ 1; for alkenes, *n* ≥ 2.

#### Why Alkenes React Faster
The **π bond** is weaker than a σ bond (≈ 264 kJ mol⁻¹ versus ≈ 347 kJ mol⁻¹) and its electron density sits above and below the C–C axis, attracting **electrophiles**. This is why alkenes undergo *electrophilic addition*, while alkanes — having only σ bonds and no exposed π electrons — react only via slow *free-radical substitution* under UV light.

#### Reactions You Must Be Able to Write
- **Substitution (alkanes):** CH₄ + Cl₂ ─(UV)→ CH₃Cl + HCl; further chlorination gives CH₂Cl₂, CHCl₃, CCl₄. Reactivity order: F₂ (explosive) > Cl₂ > Br₂.
- **Hydrogenation (alkenes):** CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ ─(Ni/Pt, 150 °C)→ CH₃–CH₃.
- **Hydrohalogenation:** propene + HBr → 2-bromopropane (major) + 1-bromopropane (minor).
- **Hydration:** CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O ─(H₃PO₄, 300 °C)→ CH₃CH₂OH.
- **Polymerisation:** n CH₂=CH₂ → –(CH₂–CH₂)ₙ– (polyethene).

#### Markovnikov's Rule
When HX adds to an *asymmetric* alkene, **H goes to the carbon already carrying more H atoms**, giving the more substituted carbocation. In propene + HBr → CH₃–CHBr–CH₃ (major).

> **Common trap:** candidates place H on the *more substituted* carbon. Reverse it: H goes to the carbon with **more hydrogens**, not more alkyl groups.

#### Distinguishing Test (NECO practical)
Bromine water (or Br₂ in CCl₄) decolourises with an alkene at room temperature, **without UV light**, giving a colourless dibromo product. Alkanes require UV light and only then substitute slowly. Acidified KMnO₄ (Baeyer's test) gives the same decolourisation for alkenes, with brown MnO₂ appearing.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Cis–Trans Isomerism in Alkenes
Because the C=C double bond is rigid, each doubly-bonded carbon must carry two **different** groups for geometric isomerism to exist. But-2-ene gives **cis-but-2-ene** (b.p. 3.7 °C) and **trans-but-2-ene** (b.p. 0.9 °C) — same formula C₄H₈, different physical properties. Propene and 2-methylbut-2-ene cannot show cis-trans isomerism because one carbon of the C=C holds two identical H atoms.

> **NECO trap:** cycloalkanes also have the formula CₙH₂ₙ. If the question lists "C₃H₆ — propene *or* cyclopropane", you must check the reaction with bromine water: only propene decolourises it.

#### Industrial Sources & Cracking
Crude oil is **fractionally distilled** into LPG (C₁–C₄ gases), petrol (C₅–C₁₀), kerosene (C₁₁–C₁₂), diesel (C₁₃–C₁₇), and residue above C₁₇. Long-chain fractions are cracked to meet demand for shorter alkenes used in polymers.

| Cracking type | Conditions | Product mix |
|---|---|---|
| **Thermal** | ~800 °C, no catalyst, high pressure | Mixed alkanes + alkenes, broad spread |
| **Catalytic** | ~500 °C, **zeolite** (aluminosilicate) | Branched alkanes + aromatics, more petrol |
| **Steam** | ~900 °C, steam diluent | Mostly ethene + propene (feedstock alkenes) |

#### Common Mistakes That Cost Marks
1. Writing the alkene formula as **CₙH₂ₙ₊₂** (the alkane formula). The correct one is **CₙH₂ₙ**.
2. Balancing **alkane combustion** with oxygen: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ + **(3n+1)/2** O₂ → n CO₂ + (n+1) H₂O. Burn butane: 2 C₄H₁₀ + 13 O₂ → 8 CO₂ + 10 H₂O.
3. Using **aqueous KOH** (gives substitution → alcohol) instead of **alcoholic KOH** (gives elimination → alkene) for dehydrohalogenation of haloalkanes.
4. Confusing **catalytic** and **steam** cracking — they differ in catalyst, temperature and dominant products.

#### Worked Example
Name the major product of the reaction between 2-methylbut-2-ene and HCl, and state the rule used.

The alkene is (CH₃)₂C=CH–CH₃. Adding HCl: H⁺ goes to the CH carbon (more H atoms), Cl⁻ to the C bearing two CH₃ groups, giving **2-chloro-2-methylbutane** as the major product. Apply **Markovnikov's rule** via the more stable tertiary carbocation intermediate.
