# Legal News

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Legal News in CLAT Current Affairs covers recent Indian judicial pronouncements, ordinances under Article 123, amendments to the three new criminal codes (BNS, BNSS, BSA in force from 1 July 2024), and key constitutional developments within the 12-month reference window before the exam.

#### Core must-know facts:

- The current criminal law trio: Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) replaces IPC, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) replaces CrPC, and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA) replaces the Indian Evidence Act — all enforced from 1 July 2024.
- Articles 32, 226, and 142 of the Constitution are the three most-tested writ and complete-justice provisions.
- Ratio decidendi is binding precedent; obiter dicta is persuasive observation — never treat them as the same.

#### High-yield pointers:

- Always note the bench strength, judge names, citation, and date of judgment.
- Track Supreme Court and High Court rulings reported in *The Hindu*, *Indian Express*, LiveLaw, Bar & Bench, and Supreme Court Observer.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Scope of Legal News in CLAT

The CLAT Current Affairs section carries roughly 25% weightage, of which Legal News alone accounts for about 5%. Questions typically appear as factual MCQs, passage-based comprehension, or principle-application sets anchored to recent legislation and judicial rulings. The Consortium of NLUs fixes a 12-month reference window — anything older than that is out of syllabus even if factually important.

#### Must-Do Reading List

- *The Hindu* and *Indian Express* — daily national dailies with a dedicated law page.
- LiveLaw, Bar & Bench, and Supreme Court Observer — case-specific reporting with citations.
- The official websites sci.gov.in and consortiumofnlus.ac.in for primary orders and syllabus updates.

A working student allocates at least 30 minutes daily to scan three judgments and one legislative update. Categorise every item under criminal, constitutional, commercial, environmental, family, labour, tax, or cyber law for revision-day recall.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Traps

The most-tested trap is the **IPC-to-BNS section confusion**. A question about murder after 1 July 2024 requires the BNS section (100), not the IPC section (302). Examineers exploit this by quoting case law that predates the new codes and expecting candidates to identify the correct statutory provision.

Another trap is treating **obiter dicta as binding ratio**. A Supreme Court observation may guide lower courts but does not become *law declared* under Article 141. Always separate the binding holding from persuasive commentary. Similarly, a Law Commission Report or Parliamentary Standing Committee recommendation is not enacted law until both Houses pass it and the President assents.

#### Worked Analytical Pattern

When a news item says *"Supreme Court strikes down provision X of statute Y"*, decode it as:

1. Identify the bench (Constitution bench, division bench, or single judge).
2. Note the writ invoked — Article 32, 226, or 142.
3. Extract the statute and section number.
4. Record the relief — striking down, reading down, or interim stay.
5. Note any separate concurring or dissenting opinions.

> **Exam tip:** Spend the last week before CLAT revising only Supreme Court Constitution-bench rulings, Article 142 references, and the three new criminal codes — these three buckets account for the bulk of high-scoring legal-news questions.

#### Practice Prompts

1. Differentiate between ratio decidendi and obiter dicta using any recent Supreme Court ruling of your choice, citing the judgment date and bench.
2. Map the old IPC sections 302, 376, and 420 to their corresponding BNS provisions and explain one substantive change in each.
