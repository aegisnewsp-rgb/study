# Pattern Sequences and Spatial Transformations

Section 3 of HPAT-Ireland contains no written information at all. Every item is a diagram, and every question is answered by looking. That sounds like the most accessible section and it is the one where untrained candidates lose most time, because the failure is not perception — it is failing to classify the item before trying to solve it.

## Classify first, solve second

The single highest-value habit in this section is a two-second triage at the top of every item. Section 3 contains a small number of distinct families, and each has its own procedure. Deciding which family you are in before you touch the diagram converts a puzzling image into a routine.

| What you see | Family | The procedure |
|---|---|---|
| Several panels in a row or grid, one missing or asked about | **Sequence** | Work out the rule that carries panel to panel, then extend it |
| One figure, asked about a mirror/rotation/fold of it, or which option it becomes | **Transformation** | Identify the operation, apply it once, do not combine operations |
| A complicated figure, asked which simple shape is contained in it, or which option completes it | **Search** | Decompose the complex figure into simple components |
| Two or three figures and an option set where one must share a property | **Analogy / classification** | Find the shared property, test options against it |
| A single figure with a marked point, line or region being asked about | **Attribute** | Read off count, size, shading, position, adjacency |

Two minutes of deliberate practice on classification alone converts this section more than any amount of pattern drilling.

## Sequence items

The rule is always simpler than it looks. Three habits:

- **Find the simplest rule that fits every panel, including the first two.** A rule that works only from the third panel onwards is almost certainly wrong.
- **Change one dimension at a time.** Most sequences rotate, add, remove, alternate, or combine a constant step with a constant rotation. If two things change at once, you have probably not isolated the rule.
- **Test your candidate answer against the whole sequence before selecting.** Generate the next figure yourself, compare with the options, then look for the option that matches. Candidates who jump straight to "which option looks familiar" without generating are pattern-matching noise.

Common sequence families worth recognising instantly: rotation by a fixed amount; reflection; addition or removal of one element in a fixed order; alternation between two states; growth of size or number of sides; position change along a path; and combination of two independent cycles.

## Transformation items

The discipline here is **one operation, once**.

- Identify what changes: position, orientation, reflection, scale, count, shading, fold.
- Ask whether the transformation is stated ("as seen in a mirror") or has to be inferred from an example pair.
- Apply exactly that one transformation.
- Check the result against every feature, not the first one you notice.

The classic errors are folding a mirror and a rotation together, mirroring an image whose position also moves when only the orientation changes, and losing track of an element that should be hidden rather than deleted. On a fold, an element behind the fold line is occluded — it is not removed. That distinction is a designed distractor.

## Search items

The complex figure decomposes into simple shapes. Work systematically rather than visually hunting:

1. Count the simple shapes in the question figure.
2. Count the elements in each answer option. The correct option has the same count.
3. Eliminate on count first — it is the cheapest filter and usually removes most options.
4. Then check orientation and relative position of the surviving components.
5. Then check proportion and shading.

This ordering matters. Candidates who check position first spend the time budget on options that were eliminated by a one-second count.

## What is actually being tested

Section 3 measures speed of visual search and consistency of rule application, not drawing skill. Nothing in it rewards the ability to draw well, rotate a figure mentally with high fidelity, or work in fine detail. The score comes from maintaining accuracy while the clock runs.

Practical consequences: work in a fixed order rather than choosing items by appeal; mark anything unfinished rather than sitting with it; and accept that some items are simply beyond the budget. A guessed option on a multiple-choice paper costs nothing, and a correct answer from an item you skipped in order to take two others is a net gain.

## Self-check

1. An item shows a sequence of five panels and the sixth is missing. What is the first thing you do? *(Check that your candidate rule explains panels one through five before extending it. A rule fitted to the last two panels only is almost always the wrong rule.)*
2. A fold question asks what a figure looks like folded along a line. Two elements disappear. Are they removed? *(No. They are occluded — hidden behind the fold. Treating occlusion as deletion is one of the designed distractors.)*
3. In a search item with a complex figure and five options, what is the most efficient first filter? *(Count. Count the simple components in the question figure and in each option, and eliminate on mismatch before considering position, orientation or shading.)*
4. Why is the "two-second classify" habit worth more than pattern drilling? *(Because misclassification sends the whole item down the wrong procedure. Getting the family right makes the method obvious; getting it wrong means applying a rule that cannot produce an answer regardless of how well you look.)*
5. You cannot solve an item within its budget. What is the best use of the time? *(Mark it, eliminate on the cheapest filter available, guess, and move on. A correct guess on a multiple-choice paper is worth more than the mark you recover by spending twice its budget on one item.)*
