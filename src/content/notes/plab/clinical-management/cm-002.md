# Acute Prescribing and Safe Medication in PLAB 2

PLAB 2 is an objective structured clinical examination built from 16 scenarios of 8 minutes each, and the GMC assesses three domains in every one of them: data gathering and assessment, clinical management, and interpersonal skills. Management is therefore not optional in a station — the examiner is watching what you do about the patient, not only what you diagnose.

Prescribing is the most commonly practised management skill in the format, and the most commonly failed, because the medicine is often right and the prescription is not safe.

## Write the whole prescription, every time

A station that asks for a prescription is usually not asking you to name a drug. It is assessing whether you produce a complete, legal, checkable order. That means the drug, the dose, the route, the frequency, the duration, and — where it applies — the indication, the maximum dose and the monitoring requirement.

Verb is the most commonly forgotten field and the easiest mark to lose. "Paracetamol 1g" is not an order. "Paracetamol 1g orally, now, then 1g every six hours to a maximum of 4g in 24 hours" is. If the examiner says "say what you would prescribe", say the whole thing out loud including the maximum.

## Check before you commit

Four checks convert a prescription from correct to safe, and doing them out loud signals that you are doing them.

Check the allergy status before the drug. It is on most station stems and it is the check examiners watch for, because an unsafe prescription in a station that explicitly supplied the allergy is a marked failure rather than a near miss.

Check the weight and whether the dose is weight-based. Most adult prescriptions are not, but paediatric and some adult ones are, and a fixed mg/kg that ignores the stated weight is wrong however familiar the drug.

Check renal and hepatic function before renally or hepatically cleared drugs. In a station with an abnormal creatinine, the safe dose or the contraindicated drug is the point of the scenario.

Check interactions against what is already on the medication list. The list is there to be used; a prescription that ignores it is the classic PLAB 2 hazard.

## Weight-based and high-risk drugs

Learn a small number of emergency calculations properly rather than a large number badly. Adult epinephrine in cardiac arrest, adult and paediatric fluid resuscitation, and analgesia dosing in the unwell are the ones that appear most.

Get the units right, because that is where the errors live. A weight of 70 kg is 70,000 mg, not 70 mg — the factor of a thousand between kilograms and milligrams is responsible for more medication errors than any other arithmetic slip. Write the conversion out every time: $70\,\text{kg} = 70{,}000\,\text{mg}$. And check the concentration of the ampoule before dividing, because a 1 mg/mL presentation and a 10 mg/mL presentation need very different volumes from the same nominal dose.

## Common mistakes

Naming the drug without the full order. Lose the verb, the route or the duration and the item is incomplete however good the medicine.

Not stating a maximum dose when the drug has one. The maximum is what makes the prescription safe rather than merely effective.

Forgetting to check allergies out loud. The examiner is looking for the safety habit, not only for a safe drug.

Confusing kilograms with milligrams. Write the conversion.

Prescribing a dose appropriate to the patient\'s renal function when the station did not supply a creatinine. If renal function is not given, say what you would check — the answer that acknowledges the unknown beats the answer that silently assumes normal kidneys.

Stopping at the prescription instead of saying what you would monitor and when you would review. That sentence costs five seconds and usually carries the monitoring marks.
