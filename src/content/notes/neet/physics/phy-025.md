# Dual Nature

> **Tier roadmap:** Read 🟢 Lite for the one-night-before recap, 🟡 Standard for working problems, and 🔴 Extended for the boundary cases that decide the 720+ bracket.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> The single sentence that decides whether a Dual Nature question feels obvious or mysterious: electrons behave like waves **and** light behaves like particles, on the same exam paper.

Light is quantised. Energy arrives in packets called photons, each carrying $E = h\nu$, where $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s (Planck's constant) and $\nu$ is frequency in Hz. Bump frequency up and each photon carries more energy; raise intensity and you only increase the **count** of photons hitting the metal per second. That distinction is the entire photoelectric effect.

#### Must-know formulas

| Quantity | Formula | Variables & SI units |
|---|---|---|
| Photon energy | $E = h\nu$ | $h$ in J·s, $\nu$ in Hz → energy in J |
| Einstein equation | $K_{\max} = h\nu - \phi$ | $K_{\max}$ in J, $\phi$ (work function) in J |
| Stopping potential | $V_0 = \dfrac{h\nu - \phi}{e}$ | $V_0$ in V, $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C |
| de Broglie wavelength | $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{mv}$ | $m$ in kg, $v$ in m/s → $\lambda$ in m |

> 💡 **Memory Hook — "I-F-K-S":** **I**ntensity changes photoelectric **c**urrent; **F**requency changes electron **K**inetic energy and **S**topping potential. Mix them up and the answer key mixes you up.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Photons and the photoelectric effect

Einstein's 1905 proposal: light delivers energy in discrete quanta. A single photon transfers all its energy to one bound electron. If $h\nu \geq \phi$ (the work function, the minimum energy needed to liberate an electron from the metal surface), the electron escapes with leftover energy as kinetic energy. Below the threshold frequency $\nu_0 = \phi/h$, no electrons come out no matter how bright the lamp. That single sentence already explains four of the five classical-wave failures.

The stopping potential $V_0$ is the retarding voltage that just stops the fastest electrons. Because $eV_0 = K_{\max}$, plotting $V_0$ versus $\nu$ gives a straight line with slope $h/e$ and intercept $-\phi/e$. NEET loves that graph.

#### de Broglie: matter has a wavelength

In 1924, de Broglie inverted the photon relations: if light has momentum $p = h/\lambda$, then a particle with momentum $p$ has wavelength $\lambda = h/p = h/(mv)$. Davisson and Germer confirmed this in 1927 by diffracting 54 eV electrons off a nickel crystal, reproducing the Bragg-like pattern expected for $\lambda \approx 1.67 \times 10^{-10}$ m — exactly what $h/\sqrt{2mK}$ predicts for that kinetic energy. The Davisson–Germer result is the single most-cited experimental proof examiners quote.

#### Concept matrix — commonly confused pairs

| Feature | Photon (radiation) | Electron (matter) |
|---|---|---|
| Rest mass | Zero | $9.11 \times 10^{-31}$ kg |
| Wavelength given by | $\lambda = c/\nu$ | $\lambda = h/(mv)$ |
| Energy-momentum link | $E = pc$ | $E = p^2/(2m)$ (non-relativistic) |
| Confirmed by | Photoelectric effect, Compton shift | Davisson–Germer diffraction |
| Typical NEET ask | Compute $V_0$ or $\phi$ | Compute $\lambda$ for given $V$ |

| Confused pair | Correct relationship |
|---|---|
| Threshold frequency $\nu_0$ vs threshold wavelength $\lambda_0$ | $\lambda_0 = c/\nu_0$ (inverses, not equivalents) |
| $K_{\max}$ vs total photon energy | $K_{\max} = h\nu - \phi$, not $h\nu$ |
| Intensity vs frequency | Intensity → current; frequency → $K_{\max}$ |
| de Broglie $\lambda$ formula | $\lambda = h/(mv)$, **not** $h \cdot m \cdot v$ |

#### Exam-level worked problem

**Question:** Light of wavelength 400 nm falls on a metal surface whose work function is 2.0 eV. Find (a) the maximum kinetic energy of emitted electrons in eV, (b) the stopping potential, and (c) the de Broglie wavelength of those fastest electrons. Take $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s, $c = 3 \times 10^8$ m/s, $m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}$ kg, $1\text{ eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ J.

**Solution.** Photon energy: $E = hc/\lambda = (6.626 \times 10^{-34} \times 3 \times 10^8)/(400 \times 10^{-9}) = 4.97 \times 10^{-19}$ J $= 3.10$ eV.

(a) $K_{\max} = h\nu - \phi = 3.10 - 2.00 = 1.10$ eV (or $1.76 \times 10^{-19}$ J).

(b) $V_0 = K_{\max}/e = 1.10$ V.

(c) Using $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2m_e K_{\max}}$ with $K_{\max} = 1.76 \times 10^{-19}$ J:
$\sqrt{2m_e K} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 1.76 \times 10^{-19}} = \sqrt{3.21 \times 10^{-49}} = 5.66 \times 10^{-25}$ kg·m/s.
$\lambda = 6.626 \times 10^{-34} / 5.66 \times 10^{-25} = 1.17 \times 10^{-9}$ m $\approx 1.17$ nm.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students often divide by 2 somewhere — either writing $V_0 = (h\nu - \phi)/(2e)$ or $\lambda = h/(2mv)$. Neither factor of 2 exists in the correct formulae. Re-derive from $eV_0 = K_{\max}$ and $p = \sqrt{2mK}$ before substituting.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Edge cases and the Heisenberg overlay

The non-relativistic de Broglie formula $\lambda = h/(mv)$ holds while $v \ll c$. For a 1 keV electron, $v \approx 1.9 \times 10^7$ m/s (~6% of $c$); for 100 keV electrons used in electron microscopes, $v \approx 0.55c$ and you must use $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2mK(1 + K/(2m_ec^2))}$ or the fully relativistic $p = \gamma m v$. NEET rarely crosses this line, but assertion-reason questions do.

Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, $\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \geq \hbar/2$ with $\hbar = h/2\pi$, is the mathematical reason the wave picture is not just an analogy: position and momentum cannot both be sharp. Pair it with $\Delta E \cdot \Delta t \geq \hbar/2$ and you can estimate natural linewidths and the width of photoelectric $V_0$ distributions.

#### Advanced traps and exceptions

1. **Saturation current is intensity-dependent, $K_{\max}$ is not.** Doubling intensity doubles the number of photoelectrons per second but does nothing to their speed.
2. **Stopping potential is frequency-dependent, intensity-independent.** Brighter light does not push $V_0$ higher.
3. **Threshold wavelength $\lambda_0 = c/\nu_0$** — students invert the wrong way when $\phi$ is given in eV and $\lambda$ in nm.
4. **Relativistic breakdown** above ~10 keV electron kinetic energy; the classical $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2mK}$ underestimates wavelength at high $K$.
5. **de Broglie for massive slow particles** — a 1 g ball moving at 1 m/s has $\lambda \approx 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ m, smaller than any detector can resolve. The wavelength is real but unobservable.
6. **Photoelectric equation sign convention** — $\phi$ is subtracted from $h\nu$, never added; mixing sign gives negative $K_{\max}$.
7. **Work function in J vs eV** — $1\text{ eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ J. Convert before substituting into SI expressions, or use $h$ in eV·s ($4.136 \times 10^{-15}$ eV·s) and keep everything in eV.

#### Two advanced practice prompts

1. An electron is accelerated from rest through 150 V. Compute its de Broglie wavelength and check whether a Ni crystal lattice spacing of ~0.91 Å will produce a first-order diffraction peak. (Answer: $\lambda \approx 0.10$ nm, so yes — this is essentially the Davisson–Germer geometry.)
2. Two sources emit light of wavelengths 350 nm and 500 nm on the same metal with $\phi = 1.8$ eV. Without calculating $K_{\max}$, decide which gives a higher stopping potential and by how many volts. Then verify numerically.

> 📌 **Extended Takeaway:** The exam rewards students who read graphs (slopes, intercepts, threshold cut-offs) and convert units cleanly. Memorise $h$, $e$, $m_e$, $c$, and $1\text{ eV}$ in joules — four constants unlock every Dual Nature numerical on the paper.
