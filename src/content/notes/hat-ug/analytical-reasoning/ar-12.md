# Critical Reasoning

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Critical Reasoning tests your ability to dissect a short argument, isolate its premise and conclusion, expose the unstated assumption linking them, and judge whether the conclusion follows. On HAT-UG, it appears as 1–2 MCQs inside the Analytical Reasoning block (5% of the 100-mark paper).

- A **conclusion must be true** when every truthful answer choice is forced by the passage; a **conclusion can be true** only when the passage is consistent with it but does not require it.
- **Strengthen** questions ask what makes the conclusion more likely; **Weaken** questions ask what breaks the link between premise and conclusion.
- **Assumption** questions target the unstated premise the argument cannot do without.
- Wrong answers in many papers confuse must-be-true with can-be-true, or attack an irrelevant side issue instead of the central reasoning chain.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Argument Anatomy

Every Critical Reasoning stimulus is a miniature argument: one or more **premises** (evidence), a **conclusion** (claim the author wants accepted), and an **assumption** — the unstated bridge that, if removed, collapses the reasoning. Identify the conclusion by looking for signal words (*therefore, thus, hence, so, consequently*) or by asking "what is the author trying to convince me of?"

#### The Five Question Families

| Question Type | Task | Selection Rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Must Be True | Find what the passage forces | Answer follows logically in every world consistent with the stimulus |
| Can Be True | Find what is merely possible | Answer is consistent but not required |
| Strengthen | Add evidence that helps the conclusion | Answer makes the assumption more plausible |
| Weaken | Add evidence that hurts the conclusion | Answer attacks the assumption or the premise–conclusion link |
| Assumption | Identify the unstated premise | Answer is required, not merely helpful, for the argument |

#### Common Logical Flaws

- **Hasty generalisation** — drawing a sweeping rule from one or two cases.
- **False cause** — assuming A caused B because A preceded B.
- **Ad hominem** — attacking the person rather than the argument.
- **Straw man** — refuting a distorted version of the opposing claim.
- **Either/Or (false dilemma)** — reducing many options to just two.
- **Circular reasoning** — the conclusion is restated as its own premise.

#### High-Yield Exam Pointers

- Read the question stem **before** the argument; this primes the mental filter.
- Neutralise extreme language: a passage using *in many papers, sometimes, can* cannot support a choice claiming *always, never, all*.
- Assume the argument is sincere but flawed — your job is to evaluate the reasoning, not to rewrite it.
- Pre-phrase the answer in your own words before scanning choices; this protects against seductive distractors.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Must-Be-True vs. Can-Be-True — The Decisive Split

Most wrong selections on HAT-UG Critical Reasoning come from sliding a *can-be-true* option into a *must-be-true* slot. A statement **must be true** when, in every model of the passage, the statement holds. It **can be true** when at least one consistent model permits it but the passage does not force it. Train yourself to ask: "Is there any reading of the stimulus where this statement fails?" If yes, the answer is *can-be-true*, not *must-be-true*.

#### Assumption Logic in Depth

For assumption questions, the correct answer is the **minimum** premise that, combined with the stated premises, guarantees the conclusion. Test by **negation**: if negating the answer destroys the argument, it is the assumption. If the argument still stands after negation, the answer is merely a strengthener and is therefore wrong. Common distractors are side issues the author mentions but does not rely on.

#### Worked Mini-Example

*Stimulus:* "Company X introduced flexible hours last year. Productivity rose 8%. Therefore, flexible hours boosted output."
- **Conclusion:** Flexible hours boosted output.
- **Assumption:** No other change at Company X during the same year can explain the 8% rise.
- **Strengthen:** A peer firm without flexible hours saw no productivity change in the same period.
- **Weaken:** Company X also installed new software in March, the month output jumped.

#### Traps the Examiners Plant

| Trap | What It Looks Like | Counter-Move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scope shift | Answer swaps the author's narrow claim for a broader one | Match exact qualifiers from the passage |
| Opposite direction | Question asks to weaken, answer strengthens (or vice versa) | Re-read the stem every question |
| Half-right | Answer is true but irrelevant to the reasoning chain | Check whether it touches premise → conclusion |
| Polar answer | Uses *all, never, only, none* without textual backing | Replace polar terms with *some, in many papers*; if meaning changes, reject |

#### Practice Prompts

1. A municipal report claims that the new bike lanes reduced traffic accidents "because accident rates fell 12%." Identify the conclusion, the unstated assumption, and one piece of evidence that would weaken the causal claim.
2. A nutrition columnist argues that "since people who drink green tea live longer, green tea extends lifespan." Specify the fallacy, name an alternative explanation, and rewrite the argument using a single-strengthener that would, by itself, make the conclusion more plausible.
