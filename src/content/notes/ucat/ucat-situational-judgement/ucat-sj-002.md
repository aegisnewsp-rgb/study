The Situational Judgement subtest gives you 69 items in 26 minutes, after a one-and-a-half-minute instruction section. That is roughly 22.6 seconds per item including the scenario read, the question read and the rating click. The arithmetic alone forces a pace; the subtest rewards calibration far more than deliberation, and a candidate who reads each scenario three times in search of certainty runs out of time and lands in band 3 or band 4. This note covers the per-scenario pacing that lets you answer all 69 with a clear head.

The score is a band from 1 to 4 with band 1 the highest. Each correct response that matches the published ideal answer scores full marks and a "close to ideal" answer scores partial — that is the rule the Consortium publishes. The 69-item, 26-minute shape is on the UCAT Consortium test-format page; cohort-level statistics and per-band score distributions move year to year and should be checked live on ucat.ac.uk before your sitting.

---

### 🟢 Quick revision

- **Two passes per scenario.** First read for the situation. Second read for the question and the four response options.
- **22.6 seconds per item, including the scenario share.** Plan for that pace or accept a late finish.
- **No negative marking.** Answer every question, even when the calibration is poor. A wild guess costs you zero.
- **Six questions per scenario is the published maximum.** Real scenarios cluster at two to four items.
- **End the set on time.** Last-minute half-read scenarios are how band 2 becomes band 3.

### 🟡 Standard conceptual deep dive

#### The shape of the set

The 69 items come in clusters: each scenario has between two and six questions associated with it (the upper bound six is published by the Consortium; most clusters are two to four). A typical running sequence looks like:

- Scenario 1, Question 1 — read the scenario, answer the item, move on.
- Scenario 1, Question 2 — read the scenario again from the top, answer, move on.
- Scenario 1, Question 3 — read the scenario, answer, move on.
- Scenario 2 starts here.

The shared scenario means re-reads are forced. The pacing decision is how much of the scenario to re-read on each subsequent item, and that decision decides whether you finish.

#### The two-pass rule

Read the scenario twice on the first question. Read it once on every question after that, because by then you have the situation in working memory.

Practical sequence for the first question:

1. Read the scenario title or setup sentence.
2. Read the body — who, what, at stake, at risk.
3. Read the question stem (appropriateness or importance).
4. Read the four options.
5. Pick.

Apt candidates do this in 25 to 35 seconds on the first question. The second question on the same scenario should land in 12 to 18 seconds. Items 3, 4 and 5 land in 10 to 14 seconds. That arithmetic gives a per-cluster read of about 60 to 80 seconds for a four-question scenario. With most clusters running at three items, the per-cluster budget is about 45 to 60 seconds.

#### Reading the scenario for the right question dimension

A scenario arrives, then a question stem. The first word of the stem is your biggest pacing hint.

- "How appropriate..." → four responses, rate A through D on the appropriateness scale.
- "How important..." → four considerations, rate on the importance scale.

Once you know which scale you are on, your anchor for the rating is fixed. Patient safety, dignity, consent and professional standards are the top of both scales. Confidentiality and professionalism sit just below. Personal relationships sit at the bottom of importance.

The trap is to read the scenario and assume the question without looking. Even when the format repeats, glance at the stem. The stem costs you one second and prevents the second-most-common error (importance answered as appropriateness).

#### Allocation across the 26 minutes

A working allocation for the 26-minute timer:

- **First minute:** answer two scenarios (six to eight items) at the higher-cost end. Use the reads to settle into the subtest pace.
- **Minutes 2 through 22:** maintain a per-cluster budget of 60 to 90 seconds for the common clusters and longer for the rare six-question ones.
- **Final four minutes:** bank the last two scenarios. You should have about 12 items left, at 18 to 22 seconds each, which fits comfortably.

If you reach minute 18 with fewer than 50 items answered, you are reading too deeply. Force yourself to a single read per scenario from now on.

#### When to skip and return

Skip within a cluster is rarely right. The scenario has been read once already, so re-reading it for the second question is cheap. Skipping inside the cluster costs you the re-read time twice.

Skip between clusters is the right call. If a scenario is dense (six items, long body) and the items are hard to calibrate, flag the cluster and continue. Return at minute 22 or earlier, read the scenario once, and answer all items rapidly. You lose less than if you spent 90 seconds per item for the rest of the set.

The Consortium's published scoring rule — partial marks for being "close to ideal" — means a calibrated-but-imperfect answer is worth more than a confident wrong one. If you can defend the rating in one sentence, that is enough. Pick and move on.

#### Worked example — pacing through one cluster

Scenario (read once on Question 1):

```
  A foundation-year doctor, Priya, is working in A&E. She sees a
  patient with a sprained ankle who is in obvious pain but does not
  have a life-threatening injury. The department is busy. There is
  a senior doctor free and a nurse free. The patient has asked how
  long the wait will be.
```

Question 1 (appropriateness): "How appropriate is each of the following responses?"

```
  Response A: "Tell the patient the wait time and explain that the
  team will see them as soon as possible."

    Ideal: B (Appropriate, but not ideal) — direct and clear, but
    gives an estimate that may not hold. A senior may give a more
    accurate estimate.

  Response B: "Tell the patient to wait until called and not ask
  for updates."

    Ideal: C (Inappropriate, but not awful) — dismissive, but does
    not actually harm the patient.

  Response C: "Tell the patient you will fetch the senior doctor
  immediately."

    Ideal: D (Very inappropriate) — inaccurate, disruptive and
    raises expectations that cannot be met.

  Response D: "Sit with the patient briefly, acknowledge the pain,
  and tell them you are fetching the senior for an update."

    Ideal: A (Very appropriate) — combines reassurance with action.
```

Mental read on Question 1: 30 seconds. Decision: rating of A, B, C, D mapped to R-D, R-A, R-B, R-C respectively. (That is, A very appropriate is D, etc. — check the order in the on-screen scale before you lock in. UCAT presents the four ratings as a Likert row.)

Question 2 (importance) on the same scenario:

```
  "How important are each of the following considerations in deciding
  how to respond?"

  Consideration W: "Patient comfort and acknowledgement of pain."

    Ideal: Very important.

  Consideration X: "A&E department throughput metrics."

    Ideal: Of minor importance — relevant to the team, not to
    the patient's experience right now.

  Consideration Y: "The senior doctor's availability."

    Ideal: Important, but not the highest — relevant to what
    Priya can do, but secondary to the patient's experience.

  Consideration Z: "The nurse's training level for analgesia
  advice."

    Ideal: Of minor importance — relevant if Priya escalates,
    but not the deciding factor.
```

Mental read on Question 2: 18 to 22 seconds (re-read the scenario, scan the new options, rate, move on).

Question 3 (appropriateness again, on a new angle):

```
  "How appropriate is each of the following responses if the patient's
  pain is from a deep vein thrombosis that has not yet been
  diagnosed?"

  Response A: "Reassure the patient it is a sprain and arrange
  routine follow-up."

    Ideal: Very inappropriate — misses the diagnosis.

  Response B: "Escalate to the senior doctor and ask for an urgent
  assessment."

    Ideal: Very appropriate.

  Response C: "Send the patient home with paracetamol and a
  leaflet."

    Ideal: Inappropriate, but not awful — same trap as A but
    softer wording.

  Response D: "Ask the senior to review the leg while you order
  a D-dimer test."

    Ideal: Appropriate, but not ideal — better than C, less
    complete than B because it orders a test before the senior's
    input.
```

This is the kind of question where candidates who calibrated carefully but slowly on Question 1 land at minute 7 with only one third of the subtest finished. The scenarios are timed by the cluster count, not by the question count. Practice your pacing on clusters, not on individual items.

#### Common pacing traps

- **Reading the scenario, then reading the options, then re-reading the scenario.** Each full re-read costs 8 to 12 seconds.
- **Hesitating on the mid-scale ratings (B and C).** These are the ratings where the calibration is tight, and they are exactly where the partial marks live. But over-deliberation costs the same as a wrong answer.
- **Treating the cluster as independent questions.** You have read the scenario. Use the read.
- **Failing to bank time.** The last four items are the ones candidates get to with time pressure, and they are the ones that turn a band 2 into a band 3.

#### Worked example — split-second calibration on a tricky item

```
  Scenario: A medical student is alone in a room with a patient who
  has just been told bad news. The patient is crying.

  "How appropriate is each of the following responses?"

  Response A: "Leave the room to give the patient privacy and find
  the doctor to come back."

    Ideal: Appropriate, but not ideal — privacy is reasonable, but
    leaving the patient alone in distress misses an opportunity to
    offer support right now.

  Response B: "Sit quietly with the patient and ask if they would
  like to talk."

    Ideal: Very appropriate — supports the patient without
    imposing, and does not falsely promise follow-up.

  Response C: "Reassure the patient everything will be fine."

    Ideal: Inappropriate, but not awful — false reassurance, but
    well-intentioned.

  Response D: "Use the moment to ask if the patient has any
  questions about the next steps."

    Ideal: Appropriate, but not ideal — useful, but jumping to the
    next-steps question may not match what the patient needs.
```

The mid-scale ratings — A and D — are the calibration trap. The published guidance treats B as closest to ideal. The partial marks go to A and D. Calibration between A and D, where one is privacy-first and the other is information-first, is where the band split happens. The single-sentence defence test: "would a senior clinician describe both responses as the same approach?" If yes, both are partial-credit answers.

#### Why "answer every question" matters

The Consortium publishes that there is no negative marking. A guess on a calibrated question is worth as much as a guess on a calibrated-but-flabbergasting question. The shape of the score means a missed question is more damaging than a guessed question. If you reach the final minute with three items left, do not leave them blank — the worst guess is the same as the calibrated-but-uncertain guess. Partial marks exist; they favour having chosen.

#### Anti-patterns to flag in your pacing

- **Re-reading the scenario on every question.** Costly. One full read, plus one targeted re-read, is the rule.
- **Calibrating with the next question in mind.** Each item is rated on its own merits.
- **Skipping a cluster.** Note it, return at minute 22. A blank costs you more than a guess.
- **Spending the last ten minutes on the most difficult scenario.** Spend that time on the easiest left, then return to the hard one.

### Memory anchors

- **One full read of the scenario per cluster.** Re-read selectively thereafter.
- **22.6 seconds per item.** That is the budget.
- **No negative marking.** Answer everything; the partial marks favour having chosen.
- **Bank the final four minutes.** Do not let your cluster reading run to the end.
- **Skip between clusters, not within.** The scenario has been read.

### Common traps

- Re-reading the scenario on every item. Costs 8 to 12 seconds each.
- Deciding on A or D rather than calibrating B or C. The partial-mark candidates cluster in the mid-scale ratings.
- Treating importance as appropriateness. Re-read the stem first word.
- Leaving items blank because time has run out. There is no negative marking.
- Spending too long on a six-question scenario and running out of time for the rest of the set.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Budget clusters, not items

Averaging to 22.6 seconds per item is the wrong unit, because the scenario itself is read once and then shared across its items. Model the cluster instead.

Read the scenario once at 15 seconds (fast) or 30 seconds (heavy). The first item adds about 12 seconds for its own question and four options. Every later item in the same cluster costs about 18 seconds, since only a targeted re-read is needed.

| Items in cluster | Fast-read budget | Heavy-read budget |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 27 s | 42 s |
| 2 | 45 s | 60 s |
| 3 | 63 s | 78 s |
| 4 | 81 s | 96 s |
| 6 | 117 s | 132 s |

A plausible set of 69 items is four single-item clusters, six of two, seven of three, five of four and two of six — 24 clusters. Fast reads total 1458 seconds, which is 24.3 minutes, leaving about 100 seconds of genuine slack for the heavy reads you will meet. Plan on that slack; spend it early and you are still on the clock at the end.

#### The cliff at the final three items

With twelve items left and four minutes remaining you need twenty seconds per item, which is below your own average. Either you arrive at minute twenty-two already ahead, or you cannot finish. This is the only place in the subtest where the arithmetic genuinely beats you.

The rule: when a cluster starts with two minutes left, count its items first. If the count exceeds the number you can rate in the time remaining at eighteen seconds each, answer only as many as fit, choosing the options that look most like the standard of care, and abandon the rest. There is no penalty for an unattempted item, so the cost of leaving blanks is nil, while the cost of reading half a scenario is a rating chosen with no information at all.

#### Triage a scenario before you commit your first second

Sort scenarios on sight, using cues that cost nothing to notice.

| Class | Cues | Budget |
|---|---|---|
| Fast-read | Staffing or scheduling problem, two or three options plainly wrong, no clinical detail | Bottom of the cluster table |
| Standard | A conflict between two reasonable duties, four plausible options | Middle of the table |
| Heavy | Medication, escalation, safeguarding, or a long chain of prior events | Top of the table, and the only class worth protecting |

Heavy scenarios are the ones worth spending the reserve on, and they are a small minority. Deciding this in two seconds prevents the commonest pacing failure, which is paying a heavy-read budget to a scenario that would have yielded its answer from the first line.

#### When an item is genuinely undecidable

Sometimes two options are indistinguishable and deliberation buys nothing. Deliberation also costs, and under an average of 22.6 seconds it costs more than the ambiguity is worth.

Use the publish-and-move rule: identify your leading option, move it one position toward the middle if your first instinct was extreme, commit, and move to the next item without revisiting. The middle positions collect partial marks, so a hedged mid-scale rating captures value an extreme rating would forfeit if it is wrong. Revisiting is the expensive part, and the set gives you no second pass.

#### Fast and extreme are different failure modes

| Pace | Rating behaviour | Likely result |
|---|---|---|
| Fast, calibrated | Mid-scale where unsure, decisive where the stem is clear | The target profile |
| Fast, extreme | 4s and 1s on nearly every item | Poor band; speed is not saving you |
| Slow, calibrated | Good judgement, set unfinished | Poor band; unrated items are lost marks |
| Slow and extreme | Long reads, then confident extremes | The worst combination |

Speed is not the objective by itself. A candidate who finishes with every rating at an extreme end has the pacing of a band 1 candidate and the calibration of a band 3. Measure the two separately, because fixing either one alone leaves the other untouched.

#### Three recoveries, each with a trigger

**Mid-set panic** — the trigger is a cluster where you have spent double budget. Close the scenario, take one full breath, re-read only the question stem and its lead word, rate, and resume at the next cluster. Do not attempt to recover the time inside that scenario; the loss is already sunk.

**The slow scenario** — the trigger is a heavy-read cluster that has passed its budget. Write the six-word uncertainty from the banding note, pick the option nearest the standard of care, rate it, and move. A late uncertain rating scores the same as an early uncertain rating and costs more.

**A mis-click on the scale** — the trigger is realising mid-scenario that a rating is impossible given what you have just read. Re-rate that one item only. Correcting a single rating is legitimate; re-reading the scenario to justify the correction is what breaks the budget.

#### A 26-minute timetable, eleven clusters

| Minute | Action |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Instruction screen read once, two heavy clusters banked |
| 0:30–3:00 | Four fast and standard clusters at the low end of the table |
| 3:00–9:00 | Six standard clusters, three items each, steady at about 63 seconds |
| 9:00–14:00 | Four longer clusters including both six-item scenarios |
| 14:00–20:00 | Remaining clusters, reserve the slack here rather than earlier |
| 20:00–22:00 | Deliberate consolidation: finish every cluster currently open |
| 22:00–26:00 | Rate what fits at eighteen seconds each, abandon the rest |

The timetable front-loads the heavy work and back-loads the slack, because the only predictable error is the one that happens last.

#### Build pace from measurement, not from impression

Record the time from first word of a scenario to its final rating, for every cluster, on every practice set. Then compare against the cluster table.

- Clusters running 20 per cent over budget are reading problems, not pace problems; fix by forcing a single full read plus targeted re-reads.
- Clusters running over while being read twice are the eight-to-twelve-second tax appearing in your own numbers.
- Clusters finishing early while ratings sit at the extremes mean you are rushing the decision, not the reading.

After ten sets, take the median per cluster size. That median, not your sense of speed, is the budget you sit down with. Official UCAT practice tests are the right benchmark for the published shape; cohort statistics and any year-on-year figures belong on ucat.ac.uk, read live before your sitting.

### What to do next

1. Take ten official SJT clusters. Time each cluster from first read to last rating, and write the time on the sheet. A four-item cluster should land at 60 to 90 seconds; a six-item cluster at 90 to 130 seconds.
2. Build a one-page pacing card: scenarios per minute, mid-scale calibration test, single-read rule. Read it before each mock.
3. Run a 26-minute timed mock with the on-screen calculator equivalent (timed interface only), and review how many clusters you finished. If fewer than fourteen clusters, your per-cluster pace is too slow.
4. Sit at least two full SJT mocks under timed conditions before your test date. The official UCAT practice tests are the right benchmark, and the 26-minute, 69-question shape is the published rule on ucat.ac.uk.
