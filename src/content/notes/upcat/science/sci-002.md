# Chemistry: Matter, Bonding and Reactions

---
### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Scope and study value

UP names "Science" as one of the four UPCAT subtests and publishes no syllabus, no question count and no weightage for it. So this page is organised the way an entrance syllabus would normally be, not the way UP publishes it. Treat the topic list as a study map and nothing more.

Within science, chemistry is unusually worth its hours, for a reason that has nothing to do with how many questions you get. Biology items reward recall of systems you can describe; physics items reward modelling a situation; chemistry items reward a small number of transferable rules applied consistently. If your science is weak across the board, chemistry is where marks are most reliably converted into marks.

#### Atomic structure is a trend question, not a memorisation question

The periodic table only becomes useful once you read it as a set of trends. Electronegativity rises left to right and falls down a group, so a bond between two elements on the same side of the table is more likely to be ionic or polar, and a bond between two elements far apart horizontally is more likely to be covalent. Ionisation energy rises across a period and drops down a group, with the predictable exceptions at group 2 and group 15 that you can explain from full or half-filled subshells rather than from memorising a table of exceptions.

That reasoning is the whole point. If you can predict the trend, you do not need to remember it. If you cannot, you will forget which direction it goes the week before the test.

**The four trends, and the bonding each one predicts.**

| Trend | Direction | Bonding it points toward |
|---|---|---|
| Electronegativity | Rises left to right, falls down a group | Elements far apart horizontally bond covalently; elements on the same side bond ionically or polarly |
| Ionisation energy | Rises across a period, falls down a group | Exceptions at group 2 and group 15 follow from full or half-filled subshells |
| Atomic radius | Falls across a period, grows down a group | Smaller atoms hold their outer electrons more tightly, which favours ionic bonding |
| Metallic character | Grows down a group, falls across a period | Where it is strongest, delocalised electrons give conductivity and malleability |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Bonding predicts properties

Covalent, ionic and metallic bonding are not three names to attach to three formulas. Each one produces a characteristic set of bulk properties, and an exam item usually shows you the properties and asks you to identify the bonding.

Ionic solids are brittle because shifting the lattice by a whole ion layer brings like charges together, so they shatter rather than deform. They are often soluble in water because water molecules orient around the separated ions. Their melting points are high because the electrostatic attraction is strong and acts in every direction.

Covalent networks like diamond are hard because breaking a bond requires breaking the network. Molecular covalent substances are often soft with low melting points, because the forces holding the molecules together are weaker than the covalent bonds inside them — which is why dissolving sugar in water breaks almost nothing.

Metals conduct because the delocalised electrons move; they are malleable because layers of cations slide past each other in a sea of electrons that maintains the attraction.

**Property-to-bonding, the direction the item usually works in.**

| Property you are shown | Bonding it identifies | The mechanism behind it |
|---|---|---|
| Conducts as a solid and a melt | Metallic | Delocalised electrons are free to move in both states |
| Malleable and ductile | Metallic | Cation layers slide without losing electron-mediated attraction |
| Hard but brittle, high melting point | Ionic | Strong attraction in all directions, then like charges meet on a displaced layer |
| Extremely hard, insoluble | Covalent network | Breaking a bond means breaking the extended network |
| Soft, low melting point, soluble | Molecular covalent | Only weak intermolecular forces are overcome |
| Conducts only when molten or dissolved | Ionic | Ions are fixed in the lattice until they are free to move |

#### Reactions: balance first, then think

Nearly every stoichiometry mistake is a balancing mistake. Learn the method that survives contact with ugly equations: count atoms of each element on both sides, change coefficients only, never change subscripts. Putting a coefficient on a polyatomic ion when the whole ion cancels is the shortcut that works until it does not.

Then handle equilibrium. A forward reaction favoured by low temperature and high pressure means the exothermic, fewer-moles side. Le Chatelier\'s principle predicts the shift; it does not tell you the new position, which is why an item asking about degree of dissociation needs the equilibrium constant rather than a shift argument.

#### Organic chemistry, narrowly

For an entrance test, spend your time on functional-group reactions and recognition rather than on mechanism. Know what each group does: an alcohol can be oxidised to a carbonyl, a carboxylic acid reacts with an alcohol under acid to form an ester plus water, an amine is basic and an amide is not, phenol is weakly acidic because the phenoxide resonance-stabilises the charge.

Know the difference between a primary, secondary and tertiary halide, because substitution and elimination compete and the answer depends on exactly that classification. Know that ethanol and dimethyl ether are isomers, C2H6O, with different structures and different properties — the isomer question is a staple.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Common mistakes

Changing subscripts to balance an equation. It looks right and it is chemically meaningless — CH3O is not the same substance as CH2OH. Fix coefficients.

Confusing molarity with mole fraction, or mass percent with percent by volume. The units are the warning: if the answer has no units, one of the quantities you carried forward was the wrong one.

Treating a pH change of one as a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration when it is in fact a hundredfold. The log relationship runs the other way from what instinct expects.

Saying a reaction is exothermic because it "releases energy" when energy release is what exothermic means. To answer a question about why a reaction is exothermic you need the bond-breaking and bond-forming argument, not the definition.

### 🎯 High-Yield Topics for UPCAT (UP College Admission Test)

- **Periodic trends:** Electronegativity rises left to right and falls down a group; ionisation energy rises across a period and falls down a group.
- **Ionic bonding:** Strong, nondirectional electrostatic attraction produces high melting points; lattice displacement brings like charges together and causes brittleness.
- **Covalent bonding:** Networks are hard because their bonds form an extended structure; molecular substances are often softer and have lower melting points because intermolecular forces are weaker.
- **Metallic bonding:** Delocalised electrons provide conductivity, while cation layers can slide without losing electron-mediated attraction.
- **Stoichiometry:** A balanced equation has equal atom counts for every element and is changed only through coefficients.
- **Equilibrium:** Low temperature favours the exothermic direction, while high pressure favours the side with fewer moles of gas.
- **pH:** A one-unit decrease in pH means a hundredfold increase in hydrogen ion concentration.

---
### 📝 Previous Year Question Patterns

1. **Periodic-table trend prediction:** Infer the direction of electronegativity or ionisation energy and use it to assess a likely bond type.
2. **Property-to-bonding identification:** Match conductivity, brittleness, hardness, softness, solubility, or melting point to a bonding type.
3. **Equation balancing:** Choose coefficients while preserving every chemical formula, especially when balancing would otherwise tempt a subscript change.
4. **Equilibrium interpretation:** Distinguish a Le Chatelier shift from the equilibrium position or degree of dissociation.
5. **Organic recognition:** Classify halides, match functional-group properties, or distinguish ethanol from dimethyl ether as C2H6O isomers.

---
### 💡 Pro Tips

- Count each element systematically on both sides of an equation; mark a coefficient of 1 explicitly when the term is omitted.
- Underline the property clue before naming a bond, then state the particle-level mechanism behind that property.
- Label temperature and gas-mole changes before applying Le Chatelier\'s principle.
- Convert each single unit of pH into a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration before selecting an answer.
- Classify the functional group or halide before choosing between reaction pathways.
