# Cell Biology and Cell Division

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Cell structure is the most stable high-yield block in this subject, and this is where NUMS questions concentrate. Work through the two tables and the checkpoint list below and you have covered most of the easy marks.

**What each organelle actually does:**

| Organelle | Job | Detail that earns the mark |
|---|---|---|
| Nucleus | Holds DNA, governs gene expression | The nucleolus inside it assembles ribosomal subunits |
| Rough ER | Builds proteins bound for export or membranes | Studded with ribosomes |
| Smooth ER | Lipid synthesis and detoxification | Has no ribosomes — this is the distinction candidates lose marks on |
| Ribosomes | Translation | Free or bound; the actual site of protein synthesis |
| Golgi apparatus | Modifies, sorts and packages ER output | The terminal step before secretion |
| Mitochondria | ATP via oxidative phosphorylation | Carries its own circular DNA, hence maternal inheritance |
| Lysosomes | Intracellular digestion | Hydrolytic enzymes; "suicide bag" is a nickname, not a mechanism |
| Cell membrane | Controls what enters and leaves | Phospholipid bilayer; selectivity comes from the hydrophobic core |

**Mitosis against meiosis:**

| | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome | 2 genetically identical diploid cells | 4 genetically distinct haploid gametes |
| Chromosome behaviour | Sister chromatids separate at anaphase | Homologues separate at anaphase I, chromatids at anaphase II |
| Purpose | Growth and repair | Gamete production |

**Exactly three sources of variation in meiosis** — questions routinely ask which process produced a given difference:
1. Crossing over in prophase I
2. Independent assortment of homologous pairs in metaphase I
3. Random fertilisation

A fresh combination of maternal and paternal alleles within one gamete can arise only from independent assortment or from fertilisation. Crossing over merely reshuffles alleles already sitting on the same chromosome pair.

**Counts to memorise exactly:** human germ cells 23 (n), somatic cells 46 (2n), a cell at metaphase II holding 23 chromosomes but 46 chromatids.

**Three checkpoints guard the cycle:** G1/S, then G/S, which is the DNA damage checkpoint and relies on p53, then the spindle assembly checkpoint sitting at metaphase. That last one detects aneuploidy, because it withholds anaphase until every chromosome is correctly attached.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** "**Rough ER = Ribosomes + Proteins. Smooth ER = Lipids + Detox.**" If the question says ribosomes build it, the destination is rough ER or a free ribosome — never smooth ER.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

NUMS allocates 55 of the 150 Paper I multiple-choice questions to Biology — the largest single block in the paper, more than Chemistry or Physics individually. It also states that for Biology, Chemistry and Physics, 70% of questions will be recall-level and 30% application-level. That ratio should shape how you spend your hours: most of the marks are in facts you can produce on cue, not in novel problems.

Cell biology is where that ratio is most favourable, because cell structure is the highest-yield block in the subject and the most stable across textbooks.

#### Organelles by what they actually do

The nucleus holds DNA and controls gene expression; the nucleolus inside it builds ribosomal subunits. The rough endoplasmic reticulum has ribosomes studded on it and makes proteins destined for secretion or for membranes; the smooth ER makes lipids and handles detoxification. Ribosomes, free or bound, are where translation happens. The Golgi apparatus modifies, sorts and packages what arrives from the ER.

Mitochondria produce ATP through oxidative phosphorylation and carry their own circular DNA. They are the reason you inherit your mitochondrial genome from your mother, and that inheritance pattern is a standard question. Lysosomes carry hydrolytic enzymes and are the site of intracellular digestion — the "suicide bag" description is a nickname, not a mechanism.

The cell membrane is a phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins. Its selectivity comes from the hydrophobic interior: anything polar and charged needs a transport protein to cross it. That single idea explains facilitated diffusion, active transport, co-transport and why the oxygen count in blood is mostly bound to haemoglobin rather than dissolved.

#### Mitosis and meiosis, and why the difference matters

Mitosis produces two genetically identical diploid cells: prophase (chromatin condenses, spindle forms), metaphase (chromosomes align at the equator), anaphase (sister chromatids separate to opposite poles), telophase and cytokinesis. Its purpose is growth and repair.

Meiosis produces four genetically distinct haploid gametes. The sources of variation are exactly three, and questions love to ask which process produced a given difference: crossing over in prophase I, independent assortment of homologous pairs in metaphase I, and random fertilisation. A new combination of maternal and paternal alleles in a single gamete can only come from independent assortment or fertilisation — never from crossing over, which only reshuffles alleles already on the same chromosome pair.

The chromosome counts in the classic questions are worth memorising precisely: human germ cells are 23 chromosomes (n) and somatic cells are 46 (2n); a cell at metaphase II has 23 chromosomes but 46 chromatids.

#### The cell cycle and its checkpoints

Interphase (G1, S, G2) occupies most of the cycle, DNA replicates in S phase, and mitosis follows. Three checkpoints control it — G1/S, G/S (the DNA damage checkpoint, which uses p53), and the spindle assembly checkpoint at metaphase. The first two prevent a damaged cell from replicating or dividing; the third prevents an anaphase until every chromosome is correctly attached. That is why the spindle checkpoint is the one that detects aneuploidy.

Cancer is what happens when those checkpoints fail, and the modern framing is that it is not a single disease of uncontrolled division but a set of failures in exactly these controls — p53 function, cell-cycle regulation, apoptosis and DNA repair.

#### Common mistakes

Saying a chromosome has two chromatids. A replicated chromosome does; an unreplicated one has a single chromatid. Questions about anaphase and centromere division depend on this distinction.

Confusing mitochondrial inheritance with nuclear inheritance. Nuclear genes come from both parents; mitochondrial DNA is effectively maternal only.

Treating the smooth ER as a protein factory. Proteins are made on ribosomes; the smooth ER makes lipids, metabolises glycogen and detoxifies.

Assuming a substance crosses the membrane by diffusion because it is small. Size alone is not the criterion — polarity and charge are, and glucose is small and still needs a transporter.

Saying crossing over happens in prophase of mitosis. It happens in prophase I, between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### The five errors this chapter exists to prevent

| Wrong statement | Why it fails | What to say instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Every chromosome carries two chromatids" | True only after replication | An unreplicated chromosome carries a single chromatid. Anaphase and centromere-division questions turn on exactly this |
| Mitochondrial and nuclear inheritance behave alike | Nuclear genes arrive from both parents | The mitochondrial genome is effectively maternal only |
| Smooth ER is a protein factory | Proteins require ribosomes | Smooth ER makes lipids, handles glycogen and detoxifies |
| Small solutes cross by diffusion | Size is not the deciding property | Polarity and charge decide — glucose is small and still needs a carrier |
| Crossing over occurs in prophase of mitosis | Wrong division and wrong chromatids | It occurs in prophase I, between non-sister chromatids of homologues |

#### One idea that generates four membrane questions

The hydrophobic core of the bilayer forces anything polar or charged to use a transport protein. That single constraint explains the whole family at once:

- **Facilitated diffusion** — passive and down-gradient, but protein-mediated because the solute cannot cross the core unaided.
- **Active transport** — against the gradient, powered by the protein.
- **Co-transport** — one gradient supplies the energy for a second solute, which is why a single carrier defect produces several symptoms together.
- **Oxygen carriage** — blood oxygen rides mostly bound to haemoglobin rather than free in plasma, because plasma is aqueous.

#### Phase-recognition drill

| The question says | The answer carries |
|---|---|
| Chromatin condenses | Prophase |
| Chromosomes line up at the equator | Metaphase |
| Chromatids move to opposite poles | Anaphase |
| Two identical daughter cells | Mitosis, 2n → 2n |
| Four distinct daughter cells | Meiosis, 2n → n |
| Anaphase begins too early | Spindle assembly checkpoint has failed |

#### Why the checkpoint framework explains cancer

Malignancy is not one disease of unchecked division but a cluster of failures in p53 function, cell-cycle regulation, apoptosis and DNA repair. G1/S and G/S stop a damaged cell before it replicates or divides; the spindle checkpoint withholds anaphase until attachment is verified. Remove any one of the three and the cells that should have arrested continue instead — which is exactly what aneuploidy measures.

#### Advanced practice prompts

1. A cell arrested at metaphase releases sister chromatids anyway. Name the checkpoint that failed and the chromosomal condition that follows.
2. Explain why the mitochondrial genome passes from the mother while the nuclear genome does not, naming the organelle feature responsible.
3. Evaluate the claim that small molecules cross the membrane by diffusion. Identify which property actually decides the answer, using glucose as your counter-example.
