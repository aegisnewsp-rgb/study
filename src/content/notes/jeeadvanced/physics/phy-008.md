# Mechanical Properties

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Mechanical properties describe how solids and fluids respond to applied forces — through **stress** (force per unit area) and **strain** (fractional deformation). Within the elastic limit, stress is proportional to strain (Hooke's law), and the constant of proportionality is the relevant **modulus**: Young's modulus (Y) for length, shear modulus (G) for shape, bulk modulus (B) for volume. Fluids add **viscosity**, **surface tension**, and **capillarity** to the picture.

| Quantity | Definition | Symbol / Formula | SI Unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tensile stress | Restoring force per area | σ = F/A | Pa (N/m²) |
| Longitudinal strain | Fractional change in length | ε = ΔL/L | dimensionless |
| Young's modulus | Stress / longitudinal strain | Y = (F/A)/(ΔL/L) | Pa |
| Shear modulus | Tangential stress / shear angle | G = (F/A)/θ | Pa |
| Bulk modulus | −ΔP / (ΔV/V) | B = −ΔP/(ΔV/V) | Pa |
| Coefficient of viscosity | Shear stress / velocity gradient | η = F·d/(A·v) | Pa·s |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **YGB = Young·Geometry·Bulk** — Young's handles stretching, Geometry (shear) handles shape distortion, Bulk handles squeezing. For excess pressure: **"Drop has 2, Bubble has 4"** (ΔP = 2S/R vs 4S/R) because a bubble has two surfaces.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Elastic Constants and Their Interrelation

For a homogeneous isotropic solid, three moduli describe elastic behaviour and three dimensionless ratios describe cross-effects. The most-tested cross-effect is **Poisson's ratio** σₚ = −(lateral strain / longitudinal strain), bounded between −1 and 0.5 for stable materials. The moduli are not independent:

$$\frac{1}{B} = \frac{3}{Y}(1 - 2\sigma_p) \quad ; \quad Y = 2G(1 + \sigma_p)$$

This is why JEE Advanced can give two moduli and ask for the third, or give Y and σₚ and ask for B. The bulk modulus B enters **compressibility** κ = 1/B, relevant to hydraulic systems.

#### Stress-Strain Curve Interpretation

A typical ductile material's curve traverses: **proportional limit** (Hookean, σ = Yε), **elastic limit** (recovery still complete), **yield point** (onset of permanent set), **plastic region** (work hardening, possibly necking), **ultimate tensile strength** (max σ), and **fracture point**. A **brittle** material like cast iron breaks before yielding visibly; a **ductile** material like copper shows long plastic flow. JEE Advanced frequently asks you to identify regions from a labelled diagram.

#### Fluid Flow: Poiseuille, Stokes and Bernoulli

For **laminar** viscous flow through a cylindrical tube:

$$Q = \frac{\pi r^4 \Delta P}{8 \eta L}$$

The fourth-power dependence on radius r is the most-tested fact — doubling the bore increases flow sixteenfold, which is why a slight narrowing in an artery disproportionately raises blood pressure.

For a sphere falling under gravity through a viscous medium, the viscous drag follows **Stokes' law** F = 6πηrv, valid only at low Reynolds number. At **terminal velocity**, weight = buoyancy + drag:

$$v_t = \frac{2 r^2 (\rho - \sigma) g}{9 \eta}$$

JEE students often forget the (ρ − σ) buoyancy correction. Bernoulli's equation (P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant) is the energy-conservation form for ideal inviscid flow, frequently combined with continuity A·v = const.

#### Surface Tension and Capillarity

The **excess pressure** across a curved liquid interface is ΔP = 2S/R for a liquid drop (one surface) and ΔP = 4S/R for a soap bubble (two surfaces). In a capillary of radius r, the liquid rises (or depresses) by:

$$h = \frac{2 S \cos\theta}{\rho g r}$$

where θ is the angle of contact. Water (θ < 90°) climbs; mercury (θ > 90°) is depressed.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Feature | Solid (elastic) | Fluid (viscous) |
|---|---|---|
| Governing law | Hooke's law (σ = Yε) | Newton's law (τ = η dv/dx) |
| Key modulus/ratio | Y, G, B, σₚ | η (viscosity) |
| Reversibility | Reversible below elastic limit | Reversible only in inviscid ideal fluids |
| Typical test | Stress-strain graph | Poiseuille / Stokes problem |
| Excess pressure | Not applicable | ΔP = 2S/R (drop), 4S/R (bubble) |

#### Key Steps to Remember

1. Compute **stress first** as F/A in Pa, then divide by the right **strain** to get the modulus.
2. Use the elastic-constants relations to convert between Y, G, B and σₚ.
3. For flow problems, check the **Reynolds number regime** before applying Stokes' or Poiseuille.
4. For surface-tension problems, count surfaces — one for a drop, two for a bubble.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** A glass capillary of radius 0.5 mm is dipped in water of surface tension 0.072 N/m and density 1000 kg/m³. Assuming the angle of contact is 0°, find the height of water rise. (g = 9.8 m/s²)

#### Solution:

$$h = \frac{2 S \cos\theta}{\rho g r}$$

Substitute S = 0.072 N/m, cos 0° = 1, ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 9.8 m/s², r = 0.5 × 10⁻³ m:

$$h = \frac{2 \times 0.072 \times 1}{1000 \times 9.8 \times 0.5 \times 10^{-3}}$$

$$h = \frac{0.144}{4.9} \approx 2.94 \times 10^{-2}\,\text{m} \approx 2.94\,\text{cm}$$

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Many students write r = 0.5 mm as 0.5 m, off by a factor of 1000. Also, the formula uses the **radius**, not the diameter — using d in place of r underestimates rise by half and is a classic distractor on JEE Advanced.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Validity Regimes

Stokes' law and Poiseuille's flow are both **laminar** (low Reynolds number) approximations. The Reynolds number for pipe flow is Re = ρvd/η; the transition to turbulence typically begins near Re ≈ 2300. A "sphere in air" problem solved with Stokes' formula is technically invalid — air is so thin that inertia dominates and drag follows a different (quadratic-in-v) law. Equally, applying Poiseuille to a non-circular cross-section requires the **hydraulic diameter** correction.

For very small deformations, Hooke's law holds and the spring constant of a wire is:

$$k = \frac{Y A}{L} = \frac{Y \pi r^2}{L}$$

This connects mechanical properties to the simple harmonic motion syllabus — JEE often combines them in a single problem (a wire acting as a spring-mass oscillator).

#### Bulk Modulus and Adiabatic Processes

The **adiabatic bulk modulus** Bₐ = γP (where γ = Cp/Cv) governs sound-wave speed in fluids:

$$v_{\text{sound}} = \sqrt{\frac{B_a}{\rho}}$$

Using isothermal B = P instead of adiabatic Bₐ = γP changes Newton's famous miscalculation of sound speed by exactly a factor of √γ ≈ 1.16 — a connection JEE Advanced has historically tested.

#### Surface Tension: Energy Interpretation

Surface tension S can equivalently be defined as **work done per unit area** (J/m² = N/m) when creating new surface. Excess pressure derivation:

- Liquid drop of radius R, radius decreases by dR → surface area decreases by 8πR dR.
- Work done by excess pressure = ΔP · 4πR² · dR.
- Equating to surface energy change S · 8πR dR gives **ΔP = 2S/R**.

For a bubble the surface area change is 16πR dR (two surfaces), giving **ΔP = 4S/R**.

#### Advanced Pitfalls and Traps

1. Writing Hooke's law as F = kx (correct) but then reading x as extension *of the spring* — not extension per unit length — in a wire problem.
2. Treating surface tension as a force (it is force per unit length, N/m), so its dimensional formula is [M T⁻²], not [M L T⁻²].
3. Confusing **angle of contact** θ with **meniscus curvature angle** — θ is measured inside the liquid between the solid and the tangent to the liquid surface.
4. For a composite (series/parallel) bar under load, the effective Young's modulus is *not* a simple average — stress and strain redistribute through the interface.
5. Capillary rise with a conical tube: the formula changes because radius is not constant — use h·r = constant (Jurin's law).
6. Excess pressure ΔP = 2S/R uses R as the radius of curvature in 3D; for a 2D meniscus between parallel plates it is ΔP = S/R.

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics

- **Simple Harmonic Motion:** A vertical wire of stiffness k = YA/L supporting a mass m oscillates with ω = √(k/m) = √(YA/(mL)).
- **Wave motion:** Speed of transverse waves on a string, v = √(T/μ), uses the same tension/stress-strain framework as Young's modulus.
- **Thermodynamics:** Bulk modulus (especially adiabatic) controls sound speed and isothermal/adiabatic compression cycles.
- **Fluid dynamics:** Bernoulli + Poiseuille together describe pressure drop in real pipes.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. Two wires of identical dimensions but different Young's moduli Y₁ and Y₂ are joined in series and stretched by a load F. Find the effective Young's modulus and the position of the interface at extension ΔL.
2. A spherical raindrop of radius 0.3 mm falls through air with η = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ Pa·s. Verify the Reynolds number, decide whether Stokes' law is valid, and compute the terminal velocity. (ρ_water = 1000 kg/m³, ρ_air ≈ 1.2 kg/m³)
