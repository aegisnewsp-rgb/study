# Reading Comprehension — GRE Verbal Reasoning Notes

Reading Comprehension is the largest single block of GRE Verbal Reasoning. In a typical administration roughly half of each Verbal section's 20 questions are passage-based, and passages run from about 150 to 460 words. They are drawn from four content areas — natural science, social science, humanities and business — and you are never required to bring outside knowledge to them. Every correct answer is derivable from the text. What is actually being tested is whether you can reconstruct an author's argument and then locate the one sentence that settles a question.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

**Format facts worth memorising**

- Verbal Reasoning is **two sections, 20 questions each, 27 minutes per section**. A section holds roughly 10 Reading Comprehension questions, about 6 Text Completion and about 4 Sentence Equivalence.
- RC questions come in three formats: **multiple choice with one answer**, **multiple choice with one or more answers** (select all that apply), and **select-in-passage** (click a sentence). The last two carry **no partial credit** — all-or-nothing.
- Short passages are one paragraph (~150 words) and carry one or two questions. Long passages are four or five paragraphs (~460 words) and carry three or four.

**The one habit that raises scores fastest**

Read for **structure**, not for detail. Before you answer anything you should be able to say in one sentence what the author is claiming, and what each paragraph is doing. Detail questions can always be re-found by scanning; a wrong mental model of the argument cannot be repaired by re-reading.

**The pivot-word list**

Arguments turn on connectives. Circle them mentally as you read:

- **Contrast**: although, though, despite, however, yet, nonetheless, whereas, while, on the other hand
- **Cause**: because, since, therefore, thus, consequently, hence, as a result
- **Addition**: moreover, furthermore, in addition, also
- **Concession**: admittedly, it is true that, granted, certainly

A sentence shaped "Although X, Y" means the author's position is **Y**, not X. That single rule resolves a large share of inference and attitude questions.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

**The six question families and what each one actually asks**

1. **Main idea / primary purpose.** What is the passage as a whole doing? The answer must cover the whole passage, so any option describing only one paragraph is wrong even when it is factually true.
2. **Detail.** What does the passage state? The answer paraphrases a specific sentence. Wrong options are usually true statements that appear elsewhere in the passage, or near-misses that change one word.
3. **Inference.** What must be true given the passage? The correct answer is a small forced step — one logical consequence — not a leap. If an option needs information from outside the passage, it is wrong.
4. **Author's attitude / tone.** How does the author regard a claim? Answers are graded words: *qualified approval*, *cautious scepticism*, *unreserved endorsement*, *measured criticism*. Extreme words (contemptuous, ecstatic, indifferent) are almost always wrong, because academic prose is rarely extreme.
5. **Function / purpose of a sentence.** Why is that sentence there? Answer with a verb — it *qualifies*, *illustrates*, *concedes*, *refutes*, *provides evidence for*, *anticipates an objection to*.
6. **Strengthen / weaken / evaluate.** What new information would affect the argument? Identify the conclusion and the evidence, then name the assumption bridging them. A weakener attacks that assumption.

**A worked example of the structure-first method**

Suppose a passage opens: "Historians have long assumed that the decline of the guild system followed the rise of factory production. Recent work on Flemish weaving towns suggests the reverse." Everything after that is elaboration. Two facts are now fixed: the traditional view is *guilds declined because factories rose*; the author's view is *factories rose because guilds declined*. A later sentence — "The first mechanised looms appeared in towns where guild restrictions had already collapsed" — is *evidence for* the author's claim, not a *concession to* the traditional view. A question asking "The author mentions the first mechanised looms primarily to…" is answered by that role, and the option naming the role beats any option that merely restates the fact.

**Timing**

Budget about **1.5 minutes per RC question**, reading included. That leaves roughly 6–7 minutes for a long passage with four questions and under 3 minutes for a short passage with one. If a question resists after 90 seconds, mark it and move on — an unanswered question and a wrong question cost exactly the same.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

**Where strong readers lose points**

- **The "true but not the answer" trap.** On main-idea and purpose questions every option may be a true statement about the passage. Only one describes the *whole*. Test each option by asking whether it covers paragraph 1 *and* the last paragraph.
- **Scope inflation.** Options that swap "some" for "all", "may" for "must", "suggests" for "proves", or "in the twentieth century" for "historically" are wrong by scope, not by content.
- **Select-in-passage precision.** Click the sentence that *does the job*, not the sentence that *contains the topic*. Asked for the author's conclusion, click the sentence stating it, not the one setting it up.
- **Multiple-answer arithmetic.** "Select all that apply" typically has two or three correct choices out of five. Because there is no partial credit, verify each choice independently against the passage rather than picking the two that feel strongest.
- **Comparative passages.** Some administrations pair two short passages on one topic and ask how they relate. That question is almost always about **agreement and disagreement between claims**, not about which is better written.

**A three-week drill schedule aimed at the real weakness**

- **Week 1 — accuracy, untimed.** Take ten passages with no clock. For every wrong answer write one line naming the *trap* (scope, true-but-not-answer, outside knowledge). The error log is the deliverable, not the score.
- **Week 2 — timed sets.** Two passages per sitting at 1.5 minutes per question. Record where the time went: reading, deciding, or second-guessing.
- **Week 3 — mixed section practice.** Full 27-minute Verbal sections under exam conditions, so RC, Text Completion and Sentence Equivalence compete for time the way they will on test day.

**What not to do**

Do not memorise vocabulary hoping it fixes RC. GRE passages deliberately keep question vocabulary plain; the difficulty is structural. Do not practise only science passages because they feel hardest — humanities passages carry the most attitude and function questions, and that is where marks actually leak.
