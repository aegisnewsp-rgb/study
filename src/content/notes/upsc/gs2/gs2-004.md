# Polity

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Polity in UPSC CSE GS2 is the paper that tests your working knowledge of the Indian Constitution, the institutions it creates, and the everyday governance mechanisms that flow from it. The syllabus explicitly covers the political system, Panchayati Raj, public policy, and rights issues — so questions are constitutional at the core but governance-flavoured in the Mains.

- **Constitution of India** — supreme law, adopted 26 Nov 1949, came into force 26 Jan 1950; blend of rigidity (special majorities for amendments) and flexibility (simple majority for ordinary laws).
- **Parliamentary System** — executive drawn from and responsible to the legislature; the Lok Sabha can defeat the Council of Ministers through a no-confidence motion.
- **Federalism with unitary bias** — three lists (Union, State, Concurrent); Article 356 allows Central rule in a state on breakdown of constitutional machinery.
- **Fundamental Rights (Part III)** — justiciable, enforceable through writs under **Article 32** (Supreme Court) and **Article 226** (High Court).
- **Directive Principles (Part IV)** — non-justiciable; guide the State in policy-making.
- **Panchayati Raj** — constitutionalised by the **73rd Amendment (1992)** and the **74th Amendment (1992)** for municipalities.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"FR-J, DPSP-NJ"** — **F**undamental **R**ights are **J**usticiable, **D**irective **P**rinciples are **N**ot **J**usticiable. For writs: **"32 = SC, 226 = HC"** — Article 32 is only for the Supreme Court, Article 226 is only for High Courts.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Conceptual Pillars of the Indian Constitution

The Constitution rests on four pillars you must separate cleanly in the exam hall: the **Preamble** (which declares India a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic), **Fundamental Rights** (negative restrictions on the State), **Directive Principles** (positive obligations of the State), and **Fundamental Duties** (added by the 42nd Amendment, 1976, on citizens).

| Feature | Fundamental Rights (Part III) | Directive Principles (Part IV) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Justiciable — enforceable by courts | Non-justiciable — guide policy only |
| Source of inspiration | USA (Bill of Rights) | Ireland (Directive Principles) |
| Who it binds | State, not private persons | State — in governance and law-making |
| Remedy | Writs — Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto | No court remedy; pressure through public opinion |
| Conflict resolution | In a clash, FRs prevail (per **Minerva Mills, 1980**); DPSP must conform to FR | Subordinate to FRs but harmonious construction preferred |

#### Key Steps for a Polity Answer in Mains

1. **Quote the article or amendment first** — write "Art. 32" or "73rd Amendment" in the opening line.
2. **State the institutional mechanism** — who acts, who appoints, who checks.
3. **Link to current affairs** — connect the provision to a recent Supreme Court ruling or government scheme.
4. **Mention a case law or committee** — adds marks instantly (e.g., Kesavananda Bharati, 1973; Sarkaria Commission on Centre-State relations).
5. **Close with a limitation or reform suggestion** — shows critical thinking, which UPSC rewards.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** "The Directive Principles of State Policy are the conscience of the Constitution, while Fundamental Rights are its spine." Critically examine, citing relevant constitutional provisions and judicial pronouncements.

#### Solution:
- **Step 1 — Quote provisions.** Fundamental Rights are codified in **Part III (Articles 12–35)** and are **justiciable** under **Article 32 (Supreme Court writ jurisdiction)** and **Article 226 (High Court writ jurisdiction)**. Directive Principles are in **Part IV (Articles 36–51)** and are **non-justiciable**.
- **Step 2 — Frame the doctrine.** In **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)**, the Supreme Court held that the **basic structure** of the Constitution cannot be abrogated even by a constitutional amendment — both FRs and DPSP are part of this basic structure.
- **Step 3 — Apply the conflict rule.** In **Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)**, the Court ruled that the harmony between FRs and DPSP is part of the basic structure, and the **42nd Amendment's clause subordinating FRs to DPSP was struck down**.
- **Step 4 — Apply to governance.** DPSP Articles 39(b)–(c) guide land and wealth distribution; Article 48A mandates environmental protection. FR Article 21 (right to life) has been **expanded through judicial interpretation** to include clean air, education, and livelihood.
- **Step 5 — Conclude critically.** Both parts must be read as complementary — FRs check State overreach, DPSP push the State towards welfare goals; one without the other leaves the Constitution lopsided.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Do **not** answer "DPSP overrides FR" or vice versa in absolute terms. The correct line is: FRs have legal primacy, DPSP guide policy, and **harmonious construction is the rule** (Champakam Dorairajan, 1951 — FR prevails; but post-Minerva Mills, neither can destroy the other).

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Doctrinal Traps

1. **Basic Structure Doctrine — exact origin.** Cited in **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)**, not Minerva Mills (1980). Minerva Mills *applied* and *expanded* it. Confusing these two cases costs marks repeatedly in Prelims.
2. **Schedules confusion.** The **9th Schedule** shields land-related laws from judicial review (added by the 1st Amendment, 1951), but the Supreme Court ruled in **I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007)** that even 9th Schedule laws are open to review if placed after 24 April 1973 (date of Kesavananda). The **12th Schedule** deals with **municipalities** (74th Amendment), not panchayats — the **11th Schedule** covers panchayats.
3. **Article 356 misuse.** Imposition of President's Rule is reviewed by Parliament (both Houses) within two months. Misuse has been flagged repeatedly by the **Sarkaria Commission (1988)** and the **Punchhi Commission (2010)**, which recommended that it be used only as a last resort.
4. **Writ jurisdiction scope.** The Supreme Court under Article 32 can issue writs only for **enforcement of Fundamental Rights**; High Courts under Article 226 can issue writs for **fundamental rights AND any other purpose**, making Article 226 wider in scope.
5. **Anti-Defection Law.** The **10th Schedule** (added by the 52nd Amendment, 1985) disqualifies members who defect voluntarily. The Speaker of the House is the arbiter — though **Kihoto Hollohan (1992)** upheld the Speaker's power, the Court has noted that the Speaker acts as a "tribunal" subject to judicial review.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. **Trace the evolution of the basic structure doctrine** from **Sankari Prasad (1951)** through **Golaknath (1967)** to **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)** and **Minerva Mills (1980)**. Explain how each case redefined Parliament's amending power under **Article 368**.
2. **"Indian federalism is a bargain, not a blueprint."** Discuss with reference to the **7th Schedule**, **Article 249**, **Article 312**, and the role of the **Inter-State Council** (Art. 263) in resolving Centre-State disputes.

#### Common Mistakes

- Calling India a "federal state" without the qualifier **"with unitary features"** (a phrase examiners expect).
- Confusing the **5th Schedule** (tribal areas) with the **6th Schedule** (tribal areas in the Northeast).
- Assuming the **Preamble is a source of power** — it is **not a part of the Constitution's basic structure debate**, but the **Berubari Union (1960)** said it is not a law, while **Kesavananda (1973)** held it is part of the Constitution.
- Forgetting that **Article 370** stands abrogated since 2019, and that the **Jammu & Kashmir Reorganisation Act** carved out two Union Territories.
