# Simplification and Approximation: the arithmetic that survives SBI's 20-minute clock

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> What the official advertisement actually fixes, and why that decides your method.

SBI's Junior Associate (Clerk) detailed advertisement fixes the quantitative papers precisely. There is no ambiguity here — this is the pattern straight from the bank's own recruitment notice:

| Phase | Test | Questions | Marks | Time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Prelims (Phase-I) | Numerical Ability | 35 | 35 | 20 min |
| Prelims (Phase-I) | Reasoning Ability | 35 | 35 | 20 min |
| Prelims (Phase-I) | English Language | 30 | 30 | 20 min |
| **Prelims total** | | **100** | **100** | **1 hour** |
| Mains (Phase-II) | General / Financial Awareness | 50 | 50 | 35 min |
| Mains (Phase-II) | General English | 40 | 40 | 35 min |
| Mains (Phase-II) | **Quantitative Aptitude** | 50 | 50 | 45 min |
| Mains (Phase-II) | Reasoning Ability & Computer Aptitude | 50 | 60 | 45 min |
| **Mains total** | | **190** | **200** | **2 hr 40 min** |

Three consequences that decide how you study:

1. **35 questions in 20 minutes is 34 seconds a question.** That is the arithmetic budget. A method that takes 90 seconds is not a method, it is a donation.
2. **Each test is timed separately.** You do not carry leftover time from Reasoning into Numerical Ability. The clock restarts.
3. **Negative marking is one quarter of the mark assigned for the question**, on wrong answers, in both phases.

The last point is the arithmetic that governs every decision. At 34 seconds per question, the temptation is to guess everything you cannot solve. Do the arithmetic first: a quarter-mark penalty per wrong answer means **four wrong answers cancel one correct answer**. In a 35-question paper, guessing all 35 earns you 35 marks minus 35/4 — a net 26.25 marks from pure guessing. Guessing has to beat that number, not feel better than leaving blanks.

| Strategy | Expected net |
| --- | --- |
| Blank on every unsolved question | 0 from the unsolved |
| Pure guess on every unsolved question | −8.75 marks |
| Guess with 1-in-3 odds | ≈ +2.9 marks over leaving blank |

Only guess when your odds are better than one in four. That is the whole rule, and it applies to every question in both phases.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> The four techniques that fit inside 34 seconds.

#### Technique 1: BODMAS, but only as a speed rule

Operations in order — brackets, then exponents, then division and multiplication left to right, then addition and subtraction left to right. Most candidates know this and most still lose time on it, because they evaluate left to right out of habit.

The speed version is a **pre-scan, not a re-think**. Read the expression once and identify the outermost operation before you compute anything. In `(15 × 4) ÷ 3 + 8 × 2`, two candidates do different things: one multiplies everything in reading order and gets it wrong; one sees the brackets first, gets 20, then divides to 20/3, then computes the independent 16, then adds. Pre-scanning takes two seconds and prevents the error.

#### Technique 2: approximation to eliminate options

Approximation is not a fallback for when you cannot be exact. It is the primary method for MCQ papers, because MCQs reward a close answer and punish only a wrong one.

- **Round to one significant figure** for anything involving percentages, ratios or large numbers. If the true answer is 1,847, an approximation of 1,800 identifies the right option every time, because MCQ options are spaced.
- **Keep the approximation inside the option gap.** Round to the coarsest level that still lands between two options. Coarser is faster.
- **Approximate one term at a time, not the whole expression.** `(1.02)³` → approximate as `1 + 3(0.02) = 1.06`, which is far faster than multiplying three decimals.

The classic that rewards practice: for small *x*, `(1 + x)ⁿ ≈ 1 + nx`. One line, no calculator, and correct to within a percent for *x* under about 0.1. That single shortcut handles a recurring family of approximation questions.

#### Technique 3: digit-sum and divisibility as a ten-second filter

Before computing anything, test the options:

- **Digit sum for 3 and 9.** 20% of 3,450 is 690 — digit sum 15, divisible by 3. Eliminate the options that are not.
- **Last two digits for 4, 25, 50, 100.** If an answer must end in 00, half the options die instantly.
- **Digit sum for 11.** Alternating sum of digits.

These are not tricks for their own sake. In a 34-second paper, a ten-second filter that removes three of four options converts a two-minute computation into a fifteen-second selection.

#### Technique 4: substitute a friendly number

For percentage change, ratio and mixture problems, replace the awkward value with a round one, compute, then correct back proportionally.

A 37.5% rise looks hostile. It is not: 37.5% is 3/8, so on a base of 160 the rise is 60 — a mental multiplication. Pick the base *you* want by scaling the given value to a number whose fraction is obvious, then scale the answer back.

#### What the advertisement does not tell you

The bank's advertisement fixes the **paper** — number of questions, marks, duration, negative marking, medium — not a topic-wise syllabus. So use the topic list as a study map rather than as a prediction. The advert's own statement is that minimum qualifying marks on aggregate are "as decided by the bank" and that "no minimum qualifying marks for individual subject are prescribed".

That last fact has a direct strategic consequence and candidates regularly get it backwards. With **no per-subject minimum**, a candidate cannot be disqualified by one weak section. With negative marking, however, a weak section can cost aggregate marks that the others cannot recover. A single strong section does not carry the paper; aggregate is what counts, so a section you score zero in caps you at the level of your other sections.

#### Drill design for 34 seconds

Timing practice matters more than question volume. A weekly set of 35 questions in 20 minutes, on the clock, with no calculator, is worth more than 200 questions done leisurely over a week. Write down the number of questions you attempted, the number wrong, and the number you *should* have guessed on — three numbers, tracked every week.

Watch the third number especially. Candidates who leave blanks lose the same 8.75 marks as candidates who guess badly, but feel better about it.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Optimising the score rather than the syllabus.

#### Minimising the cost of the unknown

Under one-quarter negative marking, the optimal guessing threshold is one in four. Work out what that means in practice for a paper where you expect to solve, say, 22 of 35 questions cleanly:

- You have 13 unsolved.
- Blank on all 13: net contribution 0.
- Guess all 13: −3.25 marks.
- Guess only where you have real partial information — an option you can eliminate to two, a unit-order argument, an estimate that rules out two options: with odds above 1 in 4 across roughly half of them, you turn 13 blanks into a small gain.

The practical rule: **never guess on a four-option question with no information, and always guess on a question where you can eliminate two options.** Elimination is the whole game. That is why the filter techniques above are not optional polish — they exist to convert knowledge into eliminations.

#### Prelims and Mains are different problems

The 35 Numerical Ability questions in 20 minutes and the 50 Quantitative Aptitude questions in 45 minutes are not the same paper at two speeds. In prelims you are triaging — 34 seconds means you attempt what is instantly recognisable and abandon the rest. In mains you have 54 seconds per question and the syllabus widens toward data interpretation.

That difference has a preparation consequence: prelims rewards breadth of pattern recognition, mains rewards depth on the heavier topics. Candidates who prepare only for mains-level DI underperform in prelims on the arithmetic that looked trivial until the clock started.

Also note what the advertisement says about how the final list is built: the marks from Phase-I "will not be added for the selection and only the aggregate marks obtained in Main Examination (Phase-II) will be considered". Prelims is a filter that earns you the mains call, not points on the merit list. That is why a safe prelims strategy is rational — clearing the call is the objective, not maximising prelims marks.

#### The state-wise dimension

The advertisement sets the medium of examination state-wise, and the test is applied for the state a candidate applies to. Two practical notes:

- Numerics are language-neutral, but reading the question carefully in a second language costs seconds you do not have at 34 seconds a question. If your application state offers a second medium, the arithmetic cost is real even though the content is identical.
- The mains test carries separate state-wise and centre-allotment provisions. The centre is chosen by the candidate at prelims, and the bank reserves the right to re-allot. Do not build a study plan around travelling for the exam.

#### Building a personal error taxonomy

Keep an error log with four columns: question type, the mistake, the root cause, and the time cost. Three months in, the log will show that almost all lost marks come from three or four causes — BODMAS misorder, percentage-of-percentage confusion, and mis-set equations from ratio problems.

Those three, not twenty topics, are the marginal return on a quarter-mark penalty. Fix the top three causes and the same 35 questions yield more marks with no extra knowledge.

#### Common mistakes and exam strategy

1. **Carrying time across tests.** Each test is separately timed. Unused Reasoning time does not follow you into Numerical Ability.
2. **Guessing on four-option questions with no elimination.** That is a guaranteed negative under quarter marking.
3. **Leaving blanks and assuming it is free.** It is exactly as costly as guessing at one-in-four odds; the difference is only that blanking feels safer.
4. **Carrying an exactness habit into an approximation question.** If two options are 1,800 and 2,000, computing to the decimal place is wasted time.
5. **Assuming a weak section disqualifies you.** No per-subject minimum is prescribed. A weak section costs aggregate marks, not eligibility.

> **Exam strategy:** in prelims, run a triage pass — solve only what is instantly recognisable, mark everything else, and move. Come back only with time left and only where you can eliminate options. The paper is won by the questions you attempt in the first ten minutes, not by the ones you agonise over at minute eighteen.

#### Practice prompts

1. *"Under one-quarter negative marking, when should a candidate guess?"* — Derive the one-in-four threshold, then apply it to a four-option question, a three-option question after one elimination, and a two-option question after two eliminations.
2. *"Explain why `(1 + x)ⁿ ≈ 1 + nx` is the highest-value approximation shortcut for an objective quantitative paper."* — Cover the error term, the range of validity, and how it converts a computation into a one-line answer.
3. *"Phase-I marks are not added to the merit list. How does that change your prelims strategy relative to your mains strategy?"* — Address triage versus depth, and the aggregate-only merit calculation.
