# Chemical Equilibrium

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **reversible reaction** reaches **chemical equilibrium** when the forward and reverse reaction rates become equal, so macroscopic concentrations stop changing even though both reactions continue at the molecular level (dynamic equilibrium).

- The **equilibrium constant** measures the position of equilibrium: **Kc** uses concentrations (mol/dm³); **Kp** uses partial pressures.
- **Le Chatelier's principle**: changing concentration, pressure, or temperature shifts the equilibrium to oppose the change; only temperature alters the value of K.
- **Reaction quotient Q** has the same form as Kc — compare Q with Kc to predict direction of shift (Q < K → forward; Q > K → reverse).

| Quantity | Expression | Units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kc | [products]^coeff / [reactants]^coeff | (mol/dm³)^Δn |
| Kp | Kc(RT)^Δn | atm^Δn |
| Q | Same form as Kc, plugged with current values | (mol/dm³)^Δn |

Remember: **catalysts do not shift equilibrium** — they only speed up the rate at which it is reached.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Defining Kc and Kp
For the generalised reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, the equilibrium constant in terms of concentration is **Kc = [C]^c[D]^d / [A]^a[B]^b**. Only species in the gas phase or solution appear in the expression; **pure solids and pure liquids are excluded** because their "concentrations" are constant. For gas systems, **Kp** uses partial pressures (P^exponent), and the two constants are linked by **Kp = Kc(RT)^Δn**, where Δn = (moles of gaseous products) − (moles of gaseous reactants), R = 8.314 J/(mol·K), and T is in kelvin.

#### Predicting the direction of shift
The **reaction quotient Q** is calculated using current (non-equilibrium) concentrations in the Kc expression. Compare Q with Kc:

- If **Q < Kc**, the reaction proceeds forward to produce more products.
- If **Q > Kc**, the reaction proceeds in reverse to produce more reactants.
- If **Q = Kc**, the system is already at equilibrium.

#### Le Chatelier's principle
This principle states that a system at equilibrium responds to a stress by shifting in the direction that counteracts it:

- **Concentration**: adding reactant or removing product drives the forward reaction.
- **Pressure**: increasing pressure favours the side with fewer moles of gas.
- **Temperature**: raising temperature favours the endothermic direction (this is the only stress that changes K itself).
- **Catalyst**: lowers activation energy for both directions equally — equilibrium is reached faster but the position is unchanged.

#### Degree of dissociation
For reactions such as PCl₅ ⇌ PCl₃ + Cl₂, the **degree of dissociation α** = (moles dissociated) / (initial moles). It is found experimentally from vapour density: **D (theoretical) / d (observed) = 1 + α**, where α is dimensionless between 0 and 1.

| Stress applied | Effect on K | Shift direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Add reactant | No change | Forward |
| Increase pressure (Δn ≠ 0) | No change | Side with fewer gas moles |
| Increase temperature | K changes | Endothermic direction |
| Add catalyst | No change | None (faster attainment) |

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Worked calculation — Kc from initial data
For the reaction N₂O₄(g) ⇌ 2NO₂(g), 1.00 mol of N₂O₄ is placed in a 2.00 dm³ flask at 25 °C. At equilibrium, 0.40 mol of N₂O₄ has dissociated.

- Initial [N₂O₄] = 1.00 / 2.00 = 0.500 mol/dm³
- Change: −0.40/2.00 = −0.200 mol/dm³ N₂O₄; +2(0.200) = +0.400 mol/dm³ NO₂
- Equilibrium: [N₂O₄] = 0.300 mol/dm³; [NO₂] = 0.400 mol/dm³
- Kc = (0.400)² / (0.300) = **0.533 mol/dm³** (units carry Δn = +1 here)

#### Ionic and solubility equilibria
For a weak acid HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, **Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA]** (mol/dm³). For a sparingly soluble salt AB ⇌ A⁺ + B⁻, **Ksp = [A⁺][B⁻]** (mol²/dm⁶). A precipitate forms only when the **ionic product Qsp exceeds Ksp**.

#### Common mistakes examiners exploit
- Including the concentration of a solid or liquid in Kc/Kp — these are constant and must be omitted.
- Writing Δn as the *total* moles of all products rather than only gaseous moles on each side.
- Assuming a catalyst shifts the equilibrium position (it does not — it only shortens the time to reach it).
- Adding an inert gas at constant volume: no shift because partial pressures are unchanged; at constant pressure, the gas expands and partial pressures drop, sometimes shifting the equilibrium.
- Confusing Kc with reaction rate — K is a concentration ratio, not a speed.

#### NABTEB exam strategy
Chemistry contributes roughly 4% of NABTEB questions, and Chemical Equilibrium typically yields **one theory item plus one 3–5 mark calculation** in the Objective/Structured sections. The theory question almost always tests Le Chatelier's principle (stress → direction). The calculation asks for Kc, Kp, or α from a data table — set up an ICE (Initial/Change/Equilibrium) table and substitute carefully into the expression.
