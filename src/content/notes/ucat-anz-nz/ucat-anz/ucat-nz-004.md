# Situational Judgement

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your UCAT ANZ test day.

**Situational Judgement** — Quick Facts for UCAT ANZ candidates sitting in New Zealand

**What is UCAT ANZ Situational Judgement?**
Situational Judgement (SJT) is the fourth and final subtest in the standard UCAT ANZ sequence. It tests your understanding of real world situations and your ability to identify critical factors and appropriate behaviour in dealing with them. You are presented with scenarios drawn from healthcare and professional settings and asked to judge the most appropriate action or to rate the importance or appropriateness of behaviours. SJT measures four professional attributes: integrity, perspective taking, team involvement and resilience. SJT is reported as a separate band (1-4) and is not averaged into the cognitive composite score used for ranking.

**At a glance:**
- 22 scenarios, 26 minutes (target testing time)
- Approximately 69 items (≈3 per scenario)
- Items are NOT negatively marked
- Two response formats: "Most appropriate / Least appropriate" (paired) and "Very important / Important / Of minor importance / Not at all important" (rated)
- SJT is scored as Band 1, 2, 3 or 4 (Band 1 = highest performance)
- SJT is reported SEPARATELY from the cognitive subtests (VR, DM, QR) — it does not roll into the cognitive composite

**Why SJT matters for offers:**
For New Zealand applicants to medicine, the University of Auckland (Faculty of Medical and Health Sciences) and the University of Otago (Otago Medical School) use SJT band as an additional selection criterion alongside the UCAT ANZ cognitive composite. Some UK universities (in the UCAT ANZ Consortium, including those serving UK medical schools) weight SJT band more heavily than New Zealand schools do. A Band 1 or 2 result is generally regarded as the target. SJT cannot be studied like a knowledge subject — it rewards preparation with realistic practice scenarios and reflection on the UCAT four-way professional framework.

**How to study SJT:**

1. Work through the official UCAT ANZ practice tests on ucat.edu.au in timed conditions. The scenarios are calibrated to the same difficulty as the live test.
2. After each scenario, write ONE sentence on WHY the most-appropriate answer is best and WHY the least-appropriate is worst. This builds the reasoning pattern the band system rewards.
3. Re-read the UCAT ANZ "How to Prepare" page and the four-attribute framework. Memorise the four attributes so you can label a scenario quickly under timed pressure.
5. Reflect on real workplace experience (paid work, volunteering, school leadership). SJT rewards calibrated judgement, not raw knowledge — drawing on examples is the fastest way to calibrate.
6. Do NOT memorise specific scenarios. The live UCAT tests pass them through quality checks and many are recycled only across years.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (2mo+)
> Comprehensive treatment of UCAT ANZ SJT for the serious long-term student.

**Situational Judgement** — Deep Mastery for the UCAT ANZ

**The architecture of UCAT ANZ SJT:**

SJT is the only subtest in the UCAT ANZ sequence that is NOT primarily a cognitive-ability test. It is a non-cognitive measure of professional judgement calibrated against the consensus of experienced clinicians and educators in the UCAT ANZ Consortium. The scenarios are constructed from real incidents drawn from healthcare and adjacent professional settings and are reviewed by panels of consultants, medical educators, and psychometricians before being released. This calibration is why the UCAT ANZ Consortium publishes SJT results as a band (1-4) and not as a fine-grained score — the band scale acknowledges that SJT responses are about judgement, not exact correctness.

**The four-attribute framework as a scoring instrument:**

The UCAT ANZ Consortium has documented four professional attributes that SJT measures. A strong SJT performance requires you to:

1. **Demonstrate integrity** — be honest, acknowledge mistakes, keep promises, refuse to act dishonestly even under pressure (including covering for others).
2. **Take perspective** — recognise how patients, families and colleagues experience a situation; respond with empathy; avoid assumptions based on status or seniority.
3. **Work in a team** — communicate, support colleagues, accept shared responsibility, escalate appropriately when a problem exceeds your remit.
4. **Show resilience** — accept feedback without defensiveness, learn from setbacks, sustain effort, ask for help when needed.

The UCAT ANZ Consortium explicitly states that SJT scenarios are designed to differentiate between candidates on these four attributes; if a scenario seems ambiguous, return to these four attributes as the evaluation lens.

**The two response formats in detail:**

**Format 1 — Most appropriate / Least appropriate (paired choice):**

You are given a scenario and FOUR possible actions. You select the action you consider MOST appropriate AND the action you consider LEAST appropriate from the same set of four. Scoring: +1 if both your selections match the consensus panel; 0 otherwise. This is a paired-discrimination task — every scenario creates a "best vs worst" axis that the four options span. The most-appropriate and least-appropriate options are usually obvious, but the closer pair often requires weighing of trade-offs. The most common error is to pick a "good enough" most-appropriate option when a clearly better one is available.

**Format 2 — Importance / Appropriateness rating:**

You are given a scenario and FOUR possible actions. You rate EACH action on a 4-point scale:
- **Very important / Very appropriate** — top of the scale; the action should be taken or is essential
- **Important / Appropriate** — second; the action is a reasonable step
- **Of minor importance / Somewhat inappropriate** — third; the action has limited value or is questionable
- **Not at all important / Very inappropriate** — bottom; the action should not be taken or is wrong

For importance ratings, you rate how important it is to take each action. For appropriateness ratings, you rate how appropriate each action is in the scenario. The format depends on the scenario type.

**Common scenario themes in UCAT ANZ SJT:**

| Theme | Typical scenario |
|---|---|
| Honesty and error | A junior doctor makes a medication error; do you report it? |
| Patient confidentiality | A patient's family asks about a diagnosis; what do you tell them? |
| Team dynamics | A colleague is repeatedly late; do you speak to them, their supervisor, or HR? |
| Patient communication | A patient refuses treatment; you support your work, have explored their concerns |
| Professional boundaries | A patient gives you a personal gift; what is appropriate? |
| Self-care | You are exhausted and about to make a clinical decision; what do you do? |
| Feedback | A senior gives you critical feedback you disagree with; how do you respond? |
| Escalation | A patient's symptoms are worsening; when and how do you escalate? |
| Cultural sensitivity | A patient declines treatment on religious grounds; how do you handle this? |
| Resource constraints | A piece of equipment is unavailable; what is your fallback? |

**Cognitive biases to recognise in SJT:**

SJT performance is reduced by predictable biases. The most common are:

| Bias | How it distorts SJT responses |
|---|---|
| Authority bias | Assuming senior-staff actions are appropriate just because of seniority |
| Consensus bias | Assuming the majority view is appropriate when an ethical minority position is correct |
| Halo effect | Letting one positive trait (e.g. friendliness) inflate judgements of other traits (e.g. competence) |
| Action bias | Assuming "any action must be done" — sometimes the best response is to pause and gather more information |
| Self-serving bias | Rating your own past choices as more appropriate than they would be in scenario form |
| Loss aversion | Avoiding the most-appropriate action because it involves an admission of error |

A calibrated SJT response resists all six. The UCAT ANZ Consortium calibration panel is diverse (junior doctors, nurses, allied health, patients, educators), so its consensus systematically counteracts these biases.

**Practice strategy for SJT:**

1. **Use only UCAT ANZ official practice tests.** Third-party SJT materials use different attribute frameworks and produce answers that disagree with the UCAT ANZ Consortium panel.
2. **Time the scenarios exactly.** 26 minutes / 22 scenarios = ~71 seconds per scenario, including reading time.
3. **For each scenario, write ONE sentence on why the most-appropriate answer is best.** This builds the reasoning discipline the band system rewards.
4. **Track your wrong answers by attribute.** If you systematically miss perspective-taking scenarios, you have identified a calibration gap to address.
5. **Re-read the official UCAT ANZ preparation page at least once.** The attribute list and the importance-rating scale are published verbatim there.

**The scoring band scale:**

SJT is reported as a band from 1 to 4:
- **Band 1** — Highest performance. Approximately the top decile; near-consensus agreement with the expert panel.
- **Band 2** — Above average. Approximately the second and third deciles.
- **Band 3** — Average. Approximately the middle of the distribution.
- **Band 4** — Below average. Approximately the bottom quartile.

Universities vary in how they weight SJT band:
- **New Zealand universities (University of Auckland, University of Otago)** — use SJT band as one of several selection criteria, typically weighted less than the cognitive composite.
- **UK UCAT ANZ Consortium universities** — historically weighted SJT band more than New Zealand universities. Check the specific medical school admissions page for current policy.

**Common SJT study time-limit claims:**

The UCAT ANZ Consortium does not publish a recommended SJT study time. Reflective practice (NOT memorisation) produces the strongest score gains. A serious long-term student typically spends 8-12 hours over 4-6 weeks on SJT — about a third of the time spent on the cognitive composite.

**Edge cases and known traps:**

1. **Confidentiality vs honesty.** A patient asks you to keep their diagnosis from their family. The integrity and confidentiality principles both apply; the consensus view is that you do not lie, but you also do not volunteer it unprompted. "I will not share what you tell me without your permission, but I encourage you to bring your family into the conversation" is the calibrated response.
2. **Feedback vs agreement.** A senior criticises your work and you disagree. The integrity and resilience principles both apply; the calibrated response is to accept the feedback gracefully, ask for specifics, and review later — not to argue in the moment, and not to suppress your disagreement entirely.
3. **Escalation vs action.** A patient's symptoms are worsening. The calibrated response is to escalate to the person in charge without delay; "wait and see" is the wrong response because patient safety dominates the consensus framework.
4. **Team vs individual.** A colleague is struggling with their workload. The team-involvement and perspective-taking principles both apply; the calibrated response is to ask the colleague if they need help first (not to report them to their supervisor unprompted), and to escalate only if the situation continues or the colleague asks for help.

### High-Yield — UCAT ANZ Situational Judgement

The eight most-testable scenario types and the calibrated response framework:

1. **Honesty under pressure.** A colleague asks you to falsify a record. Refuse. Report to the relevant authority. The integrity principle dominates; consensus views falsification as the most severe breach.
2. **Confidentiality of patient information.** A patient's family member asks about a diagnosis without consent. Decline politely, redirect the family to the patient, encourage the patient to share if they wish. The confidentiality principle dominates.
3. **Teamwork under pressure.** A colleague is struggling; you offer to help first. If the colleague declines and the situation continues, escalate to the person in charge. Team-involvement and perspective-taking principles both apply.
4. **Escalation of deteriorating patient.** A patient's symptoms are worsening. Inform the person in charge without delay. Patient safety dominates over waiting for confirmation.
5. **Feedback reception.** A senior criticises your work. Accept the feedback, ask for specifics, review later. Do NOT argue in the moment; do NOT suppress disagreement entirely.
6. **Resource constraints.** Equipment is unavailable. Pause the procedure if delay is safe; identify alternatives; inform the team. Patient safety dominates.
8. **Patient autonomy.** A patient refuses treatment on religious or personal grounds. Confirm they understand the risks, document the refusal, do NOT coerce. Respect for autonomy dominates.
9. **Self-care.** You are fatigued before a clinical decision. Pause, hydrate, take a brief break if safe to do so; defer non-urgent decisions. Resilience and patient safety both apply.

### Previous Year Patterns — UCAT ANZ SJT (since 2025)

The UCAT ANZ SJT has been part of the UCAT ANZ Consortium test suite since the consortium's inception in 2019. As of the 2025 test cycle, four subtests are tested (Abstract Reasoning was removed in 2025), with SJT retained as the fourth subtest.

The SJT band scale (1-4) has been stable since 2019 and is not expected to change. The four-attribute framework (integrity, perspective taking, team involvement, resilience) has also been stable.

Common year-on-year patterns:
- Scenarios are drawn from real clinical and professional settings across the UCAT ANZ Consortium's UK + Australian + New Zealand contexts.
- The consensus panel is calibrated annually against a new cohort of experienced clinicians and educators.
- Band boundaries are adjusted slightly each year to maintain the same relative interpretation.
- No single scenario from a previous year's test is reused verbatim in the same year; recycled scenarios appear with at most a small contextual change.

A previous-year scenario is NOT a deterministic indicator of a future test item. The band system explicitly accounts for this by reporting on the relative calibration, not on raw item scores.

### Pro Tips — UCAT ANZ SJT

1. **Treat the four attributes as a checklist.** When a scenario seems ambiguous, ask: does the action preserve integrity? Take perspective? Work in a team? Show resilience? A "yes" to all four is a strong candidate for the most-appropriate answer.
2. **Reject the action bias.** The "any action must be done" pattern is wrong; the calibrated response often includes gathering more information or pausing to reflect before acting.
3. **Rate scenarios by attribute, not by gut feel.** Gut feel is influenced by the six biases (authority, consensus, halo, action, self-serving, loss aversion); the attribute checklist counteracts them.
4. **Memorise the four-attribute names.** They are verbatim on the UCAT ANZ preparation page and the band scoring reflects your alignment with them.
5. **Use the practice responses.** When the practice tests disagree with your judgement, prefer the practice test's answer key — the live scoring panel uses the same calibration.
6. **Track your misses.** After each timed practice test, classify each wrong answer by attribute. A pattern in one attribute (e.g. perspective taking) is a calibration gap to address with reflective practice, not a memorisation problem.
7. **Apply real experience carefully.** Workplace experience calibrates judgement; it should NOT override the consensus panel when they disagree (the panel is calibrated against MANY experienced clinicians, not just yours).
8. **Skip the calculator and the textbook.** SJT is the only UCAT ANZ subtest without a knowledge base. Studying the cognate skill base for the cognitive subtests does NOT help SJT.
