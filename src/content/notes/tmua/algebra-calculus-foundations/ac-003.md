# MM6 Differentiation

The official TMUA content specification lists eight pure-mathematics families under Section 1 Part 1, and they are MM1 Algebra and functions, MM2 Sequences and series, MM3 Coordinate geometry in the (x, y)-plane, MM4 Trigonometry, MM5 Exponentials and logarithms, MM6 Differentiation, MM7 Integration and MM8 Graphs of functions.

Differentiation and integration are two of the eight, and they are the two that behave most like different subjects. Almost every other family is a list of results to apply; these two are a single idea you have to be able to construct, because the rules are only the four operations plus the chain rule and the product rule. Everything else is technique.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> MM6 asks one question in three costumes: the gradient of a tangent, a rate of change, or the equation of that tangent. All of it is four rules.

| The expression you are handed | What it actually is | The part that gets dropped |
|---|---|---|
| $6x^5$ | A single power | The coefficient — the answer is $30x^4$ |
| $x^2\sin x$ | A product | One of the two surviving terms |
| $\dfrac{\sin x}{x}$ | A quotient | The $g^2$ under the bar |
| $(3x+1)^5$ | A composite | The $3$ from the inside |
| $x^2 + y^2 = 9$ | An implicit relation | That $y$ is itself a function of $x$ |
| $x = 3t$, $y = t^2$ | A parametric curve | Which ratio goes on top |

**Why this family deserves the time you give it.** It is two of the eight pure-mathematics families on the paper, and it is the only one where the answer is something you build rather than something you look up. A candidate who can name the outer function, the inner function and the coefficient sitting in front of the power rule has already settled most of the family.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

> The working picture: decide which of the situations you are in, because the situation dictates the setup and the setup is the part that goes wrong.

| Situation | Set this up first | Then |
|---|---|---|
| Composite | $u$ = the bracket, written out | Differentiate w.r.t. $u$, differentiate w.r.t. $x$, multiply |
| Implicit | Differentiate both sides w.r.t. $x$ | Carry the $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ factor through every $y$ |
| Parametric | Nothing to prepare | $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}$ |
| Inverse | Write the defining relation, e.g. $x = \dfrac{1}{y}$ | Read the derivative straight off it |
| Turning point | Nothing to prepare | Set the derivative to zero, then prove it is a maximum |
| Tangent | Nothing to prepare | Differentiate, then substitute the $x$ you were given |

**The order that actually saves time.** Classify the expression before you touch the algebra. Writing $u = 3x + 1$ costs three seconds and retires the single most common error in the family; discovering afterwards that you needed it costs you the item.

### Four rules cover almost every question

The power rule $\dfrac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1}$. The product rule $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$. The quotient rule $\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)' = \dfrac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}$. The chain rule, which is the product rule applied to a composite.

Everything in TMUA differentiation is one of these, applied twice if necessary. If you find yourself writing something else, you have either missed a rule or missed a substitution.

### The chain rule is the one that fails under time pressure

For $y = (3x + 1)^5$, the derivative is $5(3x + 1)^4 \cdot 3$, not $5(3x+1)^4$. The missing factor of 3 is the most common single error in the family, because the outer function looks complete and the inner variable never crosses your mind.

Two habits prevent it. Before differentiating, rewrite the composite as $u = 3x + 1$ so that $\dfrac{dy}{du}$ and $\dfrac{du}{dx}$ are visibly separate. And after differentiating, check the units — if the function is a length in metres and time is in seconds, the derivative must be a velocity, and a missing factor usually shows up as an impossible unit.

### A derivative has two meanings and questions use both

Geometrically it is the gradient of the tangent. Physically it is the rate of change with respect to whatever the independent variable is.

The second meaning is where the marks are. If $V$ is volume in cubic centimetres and $t$ is time in seconds, then $\dfrac{dV}{dt}$ is a rate of change of volume. If the question says "the volume is proportional to the square of the radius", you are given a relation and asked for a derivative of it — differentiate the relation, do not solve it first.

Likewise a solid of revolution, a curve and its tangent, and an optimisation question all come back to the same operation. Recognising which of the three you are looking at saves time.

### Implicit, parametric and the sign

Not all of MM6 is explicit differentiation. When $y$ appears more than once, differentiate both sides with respect to $x$ and treat $y$ as a function of $x$, so $\dfrac{dy^2}{dx} = 2y\dfrac{dy}{dx}$. The $y$ is not a constant, and treating it as one is the standard error in implicit questions.

For parametric curves, the chain rule gives $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy}{dt} \div \dfrac{dx}{dt}$, which is easier to remember than it looks once you see it as the chain rule applied to $y$ as a function of $x$ through the parameter $t$.

For the second derivative of an inverse function, differentiate the defining relation rather than solving for the inverse — for $y = \dfrac{1}{x}$ the inverse is $x = \dfrac{1}{y}$, and $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ follows directly.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (3mo+)

#### The four rules are one rule
The product rule, the quotient rule and the chain rule are not three separate facts. The chain rule is the product rule applied to two functions of a shared variable. The quotient rule is the product rule applied to $f$ and $g^{-1}$, using $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(g^{-1}\right) = -g^{-2}g'$, which produces the numerator $f'g - fg'$ over $g^2$ without ever needing to be stated separately. The family therefore rests on a single instruction: differentiate the composition, and let every factor you pass through keep travelling. When an answer looks wrong, the cause is almost always a factor that stopped somewhere on the way.

#### Where the routine patterns run out
Three situations break them. A parametric curve with $\dfrac{dx}{dt} = 0$ has no finite gradient, because the tangent is vertical. An implicit curve has the same problem when the implicit derivative has a zero denominator, which is the moment the gradient is infinite rather than undefined — a distinction an item will use. And a function with a corner has no derivative at all, so finding where the derivative is zero and finding where it fails to exist are separate questions that candidates routinely answer as one.

#### When not to differentiate
The paper is short enough that a calculus step is not always the cheapest route. Where a quantity is a product of two factors held at a fixed sum, AM–GM settles the maximum without a derivative. Recognising that an item does not need the tool you have been drilling is worth more in a short paper than one further example of the tool itself.

### Common mistakes

Dropping the inner derivative in the chain rule.

Differentiating $y$ as a constant in an implicit equation.

Forgetting that $\ln(\ln x)$ is a composite, so it needs the chain rule twice.

Writing the derivative but not the equation of the tangent when the question asked for the tangent.

Setting the derivative to zero to find a maximum without checking it is a maximum. Check the sign of the second derivative or the behaviour of the function either side.

Losing a factor of 10 in the power rule by dropping the coefficient before differentiating.
