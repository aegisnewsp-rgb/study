# Urinary Catheterisation & Bladder Management: Techniques, Sizing, CAUTI Prevention & Clinical Emergencies

Urinary catheterisation is one of the most frequently performed invasive clinical procedures across inpatient, emergency, and surgical units in the United Arab Emirates. For medical practitioners, registered nurses, and allied healthcare professionals preparing for the Department of Health Abu Dhabi (DOH), Dubai Health Authority (DHA), and Ministry of Health and Prevention (MOHAP) Pearson VUE licensing examinations, catheterisation protocols represent a core patient safety domain. Licensing exams consistently test clinical indications, French sizing selection, catheter materials, aseptic insertion technique, troubleshooting obstructed flow, management of autonomic dysreflexia, and rigorous implementation of the Catheter-Associated Urinary Tract Infection (CAUTI) bundle.

## Complete 3-Tier Study Guide

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Revision (1h–1d sprint)

For high-yield recall before examination morning, memorize these essential clinical parameters, sizing conventions, and safety rules:

- **Indications for Catheterisation (CDC / DOH Guidelines)**:
  - *Appropriate Indications*: Acute urinary retention or bladder outlet obstruction; strict hourly urine output monitoring in critically ill intensive care patients; perioperative use for specific urological or gynecological procedures; stage 3 or 4 sacral/perineal pressure injuries in incontinent patients; end-of-life comfort care.
  - *Inappropriate Indications*: Routine management of incontinence without skin breakdown; obtaining urine samples when the patient can void voluntarily; prolonged postoperative use without specific clinical justification; staff convenience.
- **Sizing Conventions (French Gauge)**:
  - The French gauge (Fr or Ch) measures the **external circumference** in millimeters:
    $$\text{External Diameter (mm)} = \frac{\text{Fr}}{3}$$
    For example, a $12 \text{ Fr}$ catheter has an external diameter of $4.0 \text{ mm}$, and an $18 \text{ Fr}$ catheter is $6.0 \text{ mm}$.
  - Standard adult sizing:
    - Females: $12 \text{ to } 14 \text{ Fr}$.
    - Males: $14 \text{ to } 16 \text{ Fr}$.
    - Gross hematuria / postoperative clot irrigation: $20 \text{ to } 24 \text{ Fr}$ (3-way).
  - Retention balloon volume: Standard adult balloon requires **10 mL of sterile water** (never normal saline, which crystallizes inside the micro-valve and blocks deflation).
- **Critical Safety Landmarks**:
  - **Never inflate the balloon until urine flow is visualized AND the catheter is advanced to the bifurcation hub** (in males) or advanced an additional $3 \text{ to } 5 \text{ cm}$ past urine return (in females). Inflating a balloon inside the urethra causes severe mucosal rupture, hemorrhage, and chronic urethral stricture.
  - **Urethral Trauma Triad**: Blood at the external urethral meatus, perineal hematoma/bruising, and high-riding prostate on digital rectal examination represent **absolute contraindications** to urethral catheterisation (indicative of urethral disruption following pelvic fracture). Obtain urgent urological consult for suprapubic cystostomy.
- **Core CAUTI Prevention Directives**:
  - Maintain an unbroken **closed drainage system**; never disconnect catheter and drainage bag tubing unless irrigation is clinically mandatory.
  - Keep the drainage bag **below the level of the bladder at all times** to prevent retrograde flow of colonized urine; never allow the bag to touch the floor.
  - Review daily necessity; catheter days represent the single greatest modifiable risk factor for hospital-acquired bacteriuria (infection risk compounds at 3% to 7% per day of catheterization).

### 🟡 Standard — Core Exam Concepts (2d–2mo preparation)

In this standard preparation tier, we examine catheter materials, anatomical insertion protocols for male and female patients, and the evidence-based CAUTI bundle.

#### 1. Catheter Types and Material Selection

Selecting the correct catheter material and design directly impacts dwell longevity, tissue biocompatibility, and encrustation resistance:

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                         URINARY CATHETER TAXONOMY & APPLICATIONS                        |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 1. TWO-WAY FOLEY (Indwelling):                                                          |
|    • Channel 1: Urinary drainage channel connected to closed drainage bag.              |
|    • Channel 2: Balloon inflation channel fitted with a one-way Luer-lock valve.        |
| 2. THREE-WAY FOLEY (Continuous Bladder Irrigation - CBI):                               |
|    • Channel 1: Large drainage lumen to evacuate blood clots and tissue debris.         |
|    • Channel 2: Balloon inflation lumen (often 30 mL capacity for hemostatic tamponade).|
|    • Channel 3: Continuous inflow irrigation channel for sterile normal saline.         |
| 3. COUDÉ-TIP CATHETER (Curved Tip):                                                     |
|    • Features a fixed 40° to 45° anterior curve at the tip with a positioning notch on   |
|      the drainage funnel indicating tip orientation.                                    |
|    • Indication: Navigating past enlarged median prostatic lobes (BPH) or strictures.   |
| 4. INTERMITTENT NELATON CATHETER (Straight Single-Use):                                 |
|    • Unballooned, single-lumen catheter; inserted to empty bladder and removed immediately.|
|    • Indication: Clean Intermittent Self-Catheterisation (CISC) in neurogenic bladders. |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

1. **Catheter Materials**:
   - **Latex**: Flexible and economical, but carries significant risks of type I latex hypersensitivity. Prone to rapid bacterial biofilm formation and encrustation. Restricted strictly to short-term usage ($< 14 \text{ days}$).
   - **100% Silicone**: Highly biocompatible and chemically inert; features a wider internal drainage lumen than latex for the same external French gauge. Highly resistant to encrustation; approved for long-term dwell up to **12 weeks (84 days)**. Ideal for patients with known or suspected latex allergy.
   - **Hydrogel-Coated Latex**: Hydrophilic coating absorbs water, forming a slippery protective layer that reduces urethral friction upon insertion. Approved for medium-term use (up to 4–6 weeks).

#### 2. Clinical Insertion Protocol: Male vs. Female

Strict adherence to Aseptic Non-Touch Technique (ANTT) is mandatory under UAE hospital accreditation standards:

- **Female Catheterisation**:
  1. Position the patient in the dorsal recumbent position with knees flexed and hips abducted.
  2. Don sterile gloves. Drape the perineum maintaining a sterile field.
  3. Gently separate the labia minora using the non-dominant hand; **this hand is now contaminated** and must remain in place throughout the procedure until insertion is complete.
  4. Clean the urethral meatus with sterile saline or water-based antiseptic: wipe from anterior (clitoris) to posterior (anus) using a single downward stroke per sterile swab.
  5. Lubricate the catheter with sterile water-soluble lubricant.
  6. Gently advance the catheter into the urethral meatus (approximately $5 \text{ to } 7.5 \text{ cm}$) until a flash of urine appears in the tubing.
  7. **Advance an additional $2.5 \text{ to } 5 \text{ cm}$** to ensure the retention balloon clears the bladder neck completely.
  8. Slowly inflate the balloon with $10 \text{ mL}$ of sterile water; observe patient for grimacing or pain (which indicates urethral balloon positioning).
  9. Gently pull back until slight resistance is felt at the bladder neck; secure catheter to the anterior thigh with an engineered catheter stabilization device to eliminate traction.

- **Male Catheterisation**:
  1. Position the patient supine with thighs slightly abducted.
  2. Retract the prepuce (foreskin) if uncircumcised using the non-dominant hand.
  3. Clean the glans in a circular motion starting at the urethral meatus moving outward towards the coronal sulcus using sterile swabs.
  4. Grasp the penile shaft and hold it **upright, perpendicular ($90^\circ$) to the lower abdomen**; this straightens the anterior urethral curvature.
  5. Instil $10 \text{ to } 12 \text{ mL}$ of sterile **2% lidocaine jelly** directly into the urethra using a pre-filled sterile syringe; apply gentle compression at the meatus and wait **3 to 5 minutes** for mucosal anaesthesia and lubrication of the membranous urethra.
  6. Insert the catheter smoothly; as the tip encounters the external urethral sphincter (resistance at the bulbar urethra), ask the patient to take deep, slow breaths and relax pelvic muscles. Lower the penis to $45^\circ$ towards the feet and advance.
  7. **Advance the catheter completely to the "Y" bifurcation junction (hub)** before inflating the balloon. Urine flow alone does not confirm that the balloon is entirely inside the bladder, as the side drainage eyelets can drain urine while the balloon still straddles the prostatic urethra!
  8. Inflate the balloon with $10 \text{ mL}$ sterile water. Gently retract until resistance is felt.
  9. **CRITICAL STEP**: Always **reposition the foreskin over the glans** in uncircumcised men to prevent **paraphimosis** (arterial strangulation and venous engorgement of the glans).

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Analytical Study (3mo+ mastery)

In this advanced tier, we examine the management of clinical emergencies, catheter encrustation by urease-producing bacteria, and the neurogenic bladder crisis of Autonomic Dysreflexia.

#### 1. Autonomic Dysreflexia: A Medical Emergency

In patients with spinal cord injuries at or above the **T6 thoracic vertebral level**, acute urinary retention, a kinked catheter, or an overdistended bladder triggers an uninhibited, life-threatening sympathetic discharge:

```
                      TRIGGER: Bladder Distension / Blocked Catheter
                                          │
                                          ▼
                      Massive Sympathetic Surge Below Level of Injury (T6)
                                          │
                                          ▼
                      Arteriolar Vasoconstriction -> Severe Malignant Hypertension
                                          │          (Systolic > 200 mmHg)
                                          │
                   ┌──────────────────────┴──────────────────────┐
                   ▼                                             ▼
          Baroreceptor Reflex                            Spinal Cord Block
         (Vagus Nerve Activation)                  (Inhibitory Signals Blocked at T6)
                   │                                             │
                   ▼                                             ▼
          Severe Bradycardia                          Profuse Sweating & Flushing
          Pounding Headache, Blurred Vision           ABOVE T6; Pale, Cold Skin BELOW T6
```

- **Emergency Action Protocol**:
  1. **Immediately sit the patient upright ($90^\circ$) with legs dangling** over the edge of the bed; this induces orthostatic pooling of venous blood and rapidly reduces intracranial pressure.
  2. Loosen all constrictive clothing, abdominal binders, and anti-embolism stockings.
  3. **Check the urinary catheter immediately**: untwist any kinks, empty a full drainage bag, or gently flush with 10–15 mL sterile saline if occluded. If the catheter is blocked and does not flush freely, remove it immediately and re-catheterise using copious lidocaine gel.
  4. If systolic blood pressure remains $> 150 \text{ mmHg}$ despite relieving bladder distension, administer rapid-acting antihypertensives (e.g., sublingual nifedipine or topical nitroglycerin paste above the lesion).

#### 2. Bacterial Biofilms & Encrustation Mechanics

Long-term indwelling catheters inevitably develop microbial biofilms on their luminal surfaces:
- **Urease-Producing Pathogens**: Primarily *Proteus mirabilis*, *Morganella morganii*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, and *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*.
- **Chemical Mechanism**:
  - The enzyme **urease** hydrolyzes urea into ammonia and carbon dioxide:
    $$\text{CO(NH}_2)_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{urease}} 2\text{NH}_4^+ + \text{CO}_3^{2-}$$
  - The release of ammonia raises the urinary pH into the alkaline range ($	ext{pH} > 7.5$).
  - In an alkaline environment, calcium and magnesium ions precipitate out of solution, forming mineral deposits of **struvite** ($	ext{MgNH}_4\text{PO}_4\cdot 6\text{H}_2\text{O}$) and **calcium phosphate / apatite** [$\text{Ca}_{10}(\text{PO}_4)_6(\text{OH})_2$].
  - These mineral crystals form a concrete-like crust that obstructs the drainage eyelets and lumen, leading to urine bypassing around the catheter, acute retention, and ascending pyelonephritis.
- **Clinical Management**:
  - Do **not** prescribe routine prophylactic antibiotics for asymptomatic bacteriuria; it selects for multi-drug-resistant strains.
  - For frequent encrusters, replace with 100% silicone catheters and consider acidic catheter maintenance solutions (e.g., citric acid / Suby G) only under medical guidance.

#### 3. Management of Balloon Deflation Failure

If a Foley balloon fails to deflate during removal:
1. **Never cut the catheter tubing indiscriminately**: Cutting the catheter near the meatus can allow the remaining cut shaft to retract into the bladder or collapse the inflation micro-conduit, trapping the balloon inside.
2. **First step**: Re-attach a Luer-lock syringe without the plunger and allow passive drainage; do not pull hard on the syringe plunger (which collapses the elastic inflation channel walls).
3. **Second step**: Advance the catheter slightly into the bladder to reposition a balloon that might be wedged against the bladder neck.
4. **Third step**: Cut the inflation arm *distal* to the main bifurcation valve.
5. **Urological intervention**: If conservative steps fail, a urologist must puncture the balloon under transabdominal ultrasound guidance or via transurethral cystoscopy.

## Worked DOH Clinical Examination Vignettes

### Vignette 1 (DOH Registered Nurse / General Practitioner Exam)

**Case:** A 68-year-old male with a history of benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is admitted to the medical ward with acute urinary retention. The registered nurse attempts to insert a 14 Fr Foley catheter using standard technique and sterile lubrication. Upon advancing the catheter approximately 15 cm, firm resistance is felt, and the patient reports discomfort. The catheter will not advance further, and no urine is observed. Which of the following is the most appropriate next clinical step?
- (A) Apply firm, steady manual pressure on the catheter to force it past the prostatic obstruction.
- (B) Remove the catheter, instil 10 mL of 2% lidocaine gel into the urethra, wait 3 minutes, and attempt insertion with a Coudé-tip catheter.
- (C) Upsize immediately to a 20 Fr three-way Foley catheter to provide greater structural rigidity.
- (D) Inflate the 10 mL balloon immediately to dilate the narrowed prostatic urethra.

**Detailed Solution:**
- **Option A is incorrect and dangerous**: Forcing a catheter against resistance can cause false passage creation, urethral laceration, and massive hematuria.
- **Option B is correct**: When resistance is encountered at the level of the prostate in a male patient with BPH, the appropriate action is to ensure adequate urethral anaesthesia and muscle relaxation by instilling 10–12 mL of 2% lidocaine jelly into the urethra, allowing 3–5 minutes for action. Subsequently, a **Coudé-tip (curved tip) catheter** should be selected. The curved tip (directed anteriorly toward 12 o'clock) slides smoothly over the elevated median prostatic lobe without traumatizing tissue.
- **Option C is incorrect**: Upsizing to a large 20 Fr catheter in an obstructed patient will worsen tissue trauma.
- **Option D is catastrophic**: Inflating a balloon when the catheter is not fully inside the bladder causes complete urethral rupture.
- *Correct Answer:* **(B)**.

### Vignette 2 (CAUTI Infection Control & Patient Safety)

**Case:** A 74-year-old female patient in the surgical intensive care unit has had an indwelling urinary catheter in place for 6 days following abdominal surgery. The nurse notices that the urine in the drainage tubing is slightly cloudy. The patient is afebrile (36.8°C), hemodynamically stable, has no suprapubic tenderness or flank pain, and the white blood cell count is normal. A routine urinalysis shows $10^5 \text{ CFU/mL}$ of *Escherichia coli*. What is the most appropriate management according to DOH UAE infection control protocols?
- (A) Start intravenous broad-spectrum carbapenem therapy immediately for catheter-associated UTI.
- (B) Perform daily antiseptic bladder washouts with chlorhexidine solution.
- (C) Recognize this as Asymptomatic Bacteriuria (ASB), avoid antibiotic therapy, and assess whether the catheter can be safely removed.
- (D) Replace the catheter immediately and send repeat daily urine cultures.

**Detailed Solution:**
- **Option C is correct**: This patient has **Asymptomatic Bacteriuria (ASB)**, defined as significant bacteriuria ($\ge 10^5 \text{ CFU/mL}$) in the absence of local or systemic signs of infection (no fever, leukocytosis, altered mental status, flank pain, or hemodynamic instability).
- Under international (IDSA / CDC) and UAE DOH guidelines, **antibiotic treatment is NOT indicated for asymptomatic bacteriuria** in catheterized patients (with rare exceptions such as pregnant women or patients undergoing invasive urological procedures). Treating ASB promotes multi-drug-resistant superinfections without improving clinical outcomes.
- The single most effective action is to evaluate the clinical necessity of the catheter and **remove it promptly** if no longer strictly required.
- *Correct Answer:* **(C)**.

## Common Clinical Traps for DOH Candidates

- **Trap 1: Upsizing Balloon Volume for Urine Leakage**: When urine leaks around a catheter, it is almost always caused by **bladder spasms** or luminal obstruction, not an undersized balloon. Inflating a larger balloon (e.g., 30 mL) exacerbates bladder spasms and increases trigone irritation.
- **Trap 2: Normal Saline in Balloon**: Never instill normal saline into a retention balloon; sodium chloride crystals precipitate inside the inflation lumen, blocking the channel and preventing deflation during catheter removal. Always use **sterile water**.
- **Trap 3: Uncircumcised Male Paraphimosis**: Forgetting to pull the prepuce back down over the glans after catheterisation in an uncircumcised male is a critical safety violation. It causes arterial constriction, glans necrosis, and surgical emergency.
- **Trap 4: Urine Bag Placement During Wheelchair Transport**: Never hang the urine drainage bag on the wheelchair backrest or armrest above seat level; it must be suspended from the under-seat frame below the patient's bladder.
