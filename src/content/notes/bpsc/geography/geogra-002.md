# Climate and Weather Systems

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your BPSC Prelims.

A BPSC candidate who confuses **weather** (today's atmospheric state) with **climate** (30-year statistical average) loses a free mark every year. The climate system runs on five reservoirs — atmosphere, hydrosphere, cryosphere, lithosphere, biosphere — and is driven by an imbalance between incoming **insolation** (≈342 W·m⁻² at the top of the atmosphere) and outgoing longwave radiation, redistributed from the energy-surplus tropics to the deficit-prone poles by **winds** and **ocean currents**.

- **Specific heat equation:** Q = m·c·ΔT — where Q is heat in joules (J), m is mass in kg, c is specific heat capacity in J·kg⁻¹·K⁻¹ (water = 4186), ΔT is temperature change in K or °C.
- **Coriolis acceleration:** a_c = 2·Ω·v·sin(φ), with Ω = 7.29 × 10⁻⁵ rad·s⁻¹, v in m·s⁻¹, φ in degrees.
- **Vertical lapse rate:** ≈ 6.5 °C·km⁻¹ through the troposphere (0–12 km).

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"TP-MAW"** for the five climate reservoirs — **T**roposphere, hydrosphere, **A**tmosphere-cryosphere, lithosphere, **W**orld-biosphere. Pair with the wind mnemonic **"Three-Two-One"** — Trade winds (0°–30°), Westerlies (30°–60°), Polar Easterlies (60°–90°), read low-to-high latitude to track planetary circulation.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Working understanding for candidates two weeks to two months from Prelims.

#### Pressure Belts and Wind Systems

Earth's rotating surface and uneven insolation produce seven pressure belts that migrate seasonally with the sun's declination. The equatorial low shifts into the summer hemisphere; subtropical highs sit near 30° N/S; subpolar lows near 60°; polar highs over the caps.

| Belt | Latitude | Surface Wind Leaving It | Direction (NH) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equatorial Low (Doldrums) | 0° | — | Calm, ITCZ |
| Subtropical High | 30° | Trade Winds | NE Trades (toward equator) |
| Subpolar Low | 60° | Westerlies | SW (toward pole) |
| Polar High | 90° | Polar Easterlies | NE (away from pole) |

> 📌 **Formula Check:** Geostrophic wind v ≈ (1/(ρ·f))·(ΔP/Δn), with f = 2Ω sin φ in s⁻¹. Coriolis force (an apparent force in Earth's rotating frame) balances the pressure-gradient force — students often wrongly label it the "real" cause of wind direction.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix: Cyclones That Hit Bihar vs. the Bay of Bengal

| Feature | Tropical Cyclone (Bay of Bengal / Indian Ocean) | Temperate (Extra-tropical) Cyclone |
|---|---|---|
| Formation latitude | 5°–30° N (cannot form within 5° of equator) | 35°–65° N |
| Energy source | Latent heat of condensation from warm ocean (≥26–27 °C SST) | Temperature contrast along polar front |
| Frontal structure | Non-frontal, warm-core | Frontal (warm + cold sectors), cold-core |
| Movement | Initially westward, then recurves northward | West-to-east with the Westerlies |
| Naming convention | "Cyclones" in Indian Ocean; hurricanes (Atlantic), typhoons (W Pacific) | Not regionally named |
| Diameter | 150–800 km | 800–3000 km |
| Bihar impact | Heavy rain, flooding (e.g., 2019 Fani, 2023 Michaung remnant) | Winter rain in north Bihar (Dec–Feb) |

#### Indian Monsoon Mechanism

The Indian monsoon is not "just" differential heating. The working model combines:

1. **Land–sea thermal contrast** — Asian landmass heats faster than Indian Ocean in summer.
2. **ITCZ migration** to ~25° N over the Gangetic plain by July.
4. **Somali low-level jet** crossing the equator and turning right (NH Coriolis) to feed moisture into the Arabian Sea branch.
5. **Tibetan Plateau heat pump** — elevated surface heats the upper troposphere, drawing moist air upward.
6. **Tropical Easterly Jet** (TEJ) at 100 hPa over peninsular India during summer.
7. **ENSO modulation** — El Niño (warm SOI negative) suppresses monsoon rainfall; La Niña enhances it.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students routinely answer "differential heating" and stop. BPSC has asked multi-part mains questions where one mark goes to the Somali jet, one to the Tibetan plateau, one to ENSO — a single-mechanism answer loses 60% of the marks on monsoon.

#### Worked Problem

A BPSC Prelims 2022-style MCQ read: *"During an El Niño year, the Indian summer monsoon is most likely to be (a) stronger than normal, (b) weaker than normal, (c) unaffected, (d) delayed by two months."* The correct choice is **(b)**.

Reasoning: El Niño warms central/eastern Pacific sea-surface temperatures; the Walker Circulation weakens, suppressing the westward pressure gradient that drives the Indian Ocean branch of the monsoon. The Southern Oscillation Index (SOI) — Tahiti minus Darwin sea-level pressure — turns strongly negative. Statistical correlation across 1950–2020 shows below-normal June–September rainfall over India during El Niño years. So El Niño → weak monsoon, drought risk in Bihar; La Niña → strong monsoon, Bihar flood risk.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Edge cases, numerical limits, and the connections that separate a 90% from a 99% candidate.

#### Atmospheric Energy Budget — Where the Numbers Are Tight

Incoming shortwave at the top of the atmosphere averages ≈ 342 W·m⁻². Of this, ≈ 30% is reflected back (planetary albedo ≈ 0.30), ≈ 20% is absorbed by clouds and atmosphere, ≈ 50% reaches the surface and is re-radiated as longwave. The 30% albedo is composed roughly of clouds (≈ 17%), surface (≈ 11%), and atmospheric scattering (≈ 2%). If a BPSC question asks which component contributes most to planetary albedo, the answer is **clouds**, not snow or ice — a common error because candidates conflate surface albedo with planetary albedo.

#### Numerical Edge Cases Worth Memorising

- **Coriolis force vanishes at the equator** (sin φ = 0). Tropical cyclones therefore cannot form within ~5° latitude — the eye needs at least a weak Coriolis component for closed circulation.
- **Tropical-cyclone genesis threshold** is sea-surface temperature of ≥ 26–27 °C sustained through ≈ 50 m depth, with low vertical wind shear (< 10 m·s⁻¹ between 850 and 200 hPa).
- **Trade-wind inversion** sits between 1.5 and 4 km over the eastern tropical oceans; it caps convection and is the reason trade-wind cumuli rarely grow into thunderheads.
- **Jet stream core speeds** reach 150–200 knots (77–103 m·s⁻¹) in winter, weakening to 60–80 knots in summer. The subtropical westerly jet (≈ 30° N, 200 hPa) sits over north India in winter and feeds Western Disturbances.
- **Koppen letter codes for India** — Aw (Tropical savanna, most of peninsular India), Am (Tropical monsoon, west coast), BSh (semi-arid, Deccan), BWh (arid, Thar), Cwa (humid subtropical with dry winter, Indo-Gangetic plain), Cwb (highland variant of Cwa, Nilgiris), Dfb (warm-summer continental, parts of Kashmir).

#### Common Mistakes BPSC Candidates Lose Marks On

1. Treating Coriolis as a "real" force on air parcels — it is fictitious in an Earth-fixed frame, real only in an inertial frame.
2. Citing differential heating alone for the monsoon — must also reference Somali jet, Tibetan plateau, ITCZ, and ENSO for full marks.
3. Conflating El Niño with La Niña on Indian rainfall — El Niño = weak monsoon, La Niña = strong monsoon; reversing this loses 2 marks routinely.
4. Believing temperate cyclones bring rain to Bihar only in winter — Western Disturbances and cyclonic storms in March–May also cause pre-monsoon hailstorms and dust storms.
5. Writing that the troposphere ends at "10 km" — average is 12 km in mid-latitudes, 17 km at the equator, 8 km at poles; examiners reward the latitude-dependent value.

#### Two Advanced Practice Prompts

1. **Assertion–Reason style:** "Assertion (A): The Trade Winds are deflected to the right in the Northern Hemisphere. Reason (R): The Coriolis force deflects moving air to the right of its motion in the Northern Hemisphere." Mark each true/false and justify using the geostrophic balance equation.
2. **Map-based:** Given a weather chart over north India showing closed isobars of 996 hPa centred over Bihar with cyclonic curvature, identify (i) the system type, (ii) the season most consistent with the configuration, (iii) the most likely impact zone inside Bihar, and (iv) one mitigation measure drawn from Bihar State Disaster Management Authority practice.
