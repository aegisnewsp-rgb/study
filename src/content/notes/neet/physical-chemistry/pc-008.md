# Redox

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Oxidation and reduction happen together. One species loses electrons, the other gains them.

- **Oxidation:** loss of electrons; oxidation number rises (e.g., Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻).
- **Reduction:** gain of electrons; oxidation number drops (e.g., Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu).
- **Oxidising agent** is the species that *gets reduced*; **reducing agent** is the species that *gets oxidised*.

Equivalent mass in redox uses a single identity:

$$E = \frac{M}{n}$$

where $M$ is the molar mass in g mol⁻¹, and $n$ is the number of electrons lost or gained per formula unit (dimensionless). SI unit: kg eq⁻¹, though g eq⁻¹ is far more common in NEET numericals.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **OIL RIG** = Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons). For the agents, flip it: the **R**educing agent **O**xidises itself — **ROA** — and the **O**xidising agent **R**educes itself — **ORA**. This trio settles four of the most frequently flipped MCQ options in a single breath.

Three half-cell facts worth memorising cold: SHE has $E^\circ = 0.00$ V; $\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ_{\text{cell}}$; Nernst at 298 K reads $E_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cell}} - \dfrac{0.0591}{n}\log Q$.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Working understanding for steady semester-level study.

#### Assigning Oxidation Numbers

Every redox problem begins by tagging atoms with oxidation numbers (O.N.). The rules stack — apply them in order until the unknown O.N. is forced.

| Species / context | Oxidation number rule |
|---|---|
| Free element (Fe, O₂, Cl₂) | 0 |
| Hydrogen in most compounds | +1 (except metal hydrides: NaH, CaH₂ → −1) |
| Oxygen in most compounds | −2 (except peroxides H₂O₂: −1; superoxides KO₂: −½; OF₂: +2) |
| Fluorine in all compounds | −1 |
| Neutral molecule (sum of all O.N.) | 0 |
| Polyatomic ion (sum of all O.N.) | Charge on the ion |

> 📌 **Formula Check:** In KMnO₄ (neutral), K is +1 and each O is −2, so Mn = +7. Always solve for the unknown by algebraic sum; do not memorise the Mn state — the pH changes it.

#### Balancing Redox Equations

Two methods are accepted at NEET. The **oxidation-number method** tracks total electron loss and gain; the **ion-electron (half-reaction) method** balances H and O with H₂O, then H⁺ (acidic) or OH⁻ (basic) plus H₂O. Both must give identical balanced coefficients.

#### Identifying Oxidising and Reducing Agents

The trick that defeats most students: the named agent is the *reactant*, and it undergoes the *opposite* process. KMnO₄ is added as an oxidising agent, but Mn itself is **reduced** from +7 to +2 (acidic), +4 (neutral), or +6 (strong base). The substance being oxidised is the reducing agent.

| n-factor in KMnO₄ | Medium | Reduction half-reaction |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | Strongly acidic | MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O |
| 3 | Neutral / weakly basic | MnO₄⁻ + 2H₂O + 3e⁻ → MnO₂ + 4OH⁻ |
| 1 | Strongly alkaline | MnO₄⁻ + e⁻ → MnO₄²⁻ |

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

In an acidic medium, 25 mL of 0.1 M KMnO₄ is titrated against oxalic acid solution. The reaction proceeds as:

$$2\,\text{MnO}_4^- + 5\,\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-} + 16\,\text{H}^+ \rightarrow 2\,\text{Mn}^{2+} + 10\,\text{CO}_2 + 8\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

How many mL of 0.5 M oxalic acid is required to completely reduce the permanganate, and what is the equivalent mass of oxalic acid if its molar mass is 90 g mol⁻¹?

**Step 1 — Equivalents of oxidant.** n-factor of KMnO₄ in acid = 5.
Equivalents of KMnO₄ = M × n-factor × volume (L) = 0.1 × 5 × 0.025 = 0.0125 equivalents.

**Step 2 — Equivalents of reductant.** Oxalic acid (H₂C₂O₄ → 2CO₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ per molecule). Equivalents of oxalic acid = 0.5 × 2 × V(L) = 1.0 × V(L).

**Step 3 — Equate equivalents.** 0.0125 = 1.0 × V(L) → V = 0.0125 L = **12.5 mL**.

**Step 4 — Equivalent mass of oxalic acid.** $E = M/n = 90/2 =$ **45 g eq⁻¹**.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** A frequent wrong move is using n = 2 for *each* C₂O₄²⁻ ion instead of per oxalic acid molecule — the values coincide here, but the question's wording differs in titration numericals involving Na₂C₂O₄ or KHC₂O₄. Always write the half-reaction first; count electrons from it.

#### Common Mistakes at This Level

- Swapping anode/cathode between galvanic and electrolytic cells. Anode = oxidation in *both*, but anode is the **negative** terminal in a galvanic cell and the **positive** terminal in an electrolytic cell.
- Putting log where ln belongs in the Nernst equation. The 0.0591 factor at 298 K already encodes the conversion $2.303 RT/F$; substituting $\ln Q$ in that formula gives a numerical error.
- Forgetting that $n$ in $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ is the **total** electrons in the balanced overall cell reaction, not per half-cell.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Edge cases, mathematical limits, and connections worth chasing once basics are airtight.

#### The Nernst Equation Under Real Conditions

At 298 K the working form is $E_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cell}} - \dfrac{0.0591}{n}\log Q$. At any other temperature the full form holds: $E_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cell}} - \dfrac{RT}{nF}\ln Q$, where $R = 8.314$ J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, $T$ in K, $F = 96500$ C mol⁻¹, and $n$ dimensionless. $E$ carries dimensions of $[M L^2 T^{-3} A^{-1}]$, the volt. The numerical constant 0.0591 comes from $\dfrac{2.303 \times 8.314 \times 298}{96500} \approx 0.0591$, so any deviation in T breaks the constant.

A useful boundary: when $Q = 1$, $E_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$; when $Q = K$, $E_{\text{cell}} = 0$ at equilibrium. For a concentration cell where both half-cells are the same redox couple, $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = 0$ and $E_{\text{cell}}$ depends purely on the concentration ratio. NEET occasionally asks the slope interpretation — doubling the concentration ratio changes $E_{\text{cell}}$ by $\dfrac{0.0591}{n}$ volts per tenfold shift.

#### Disproportionation and Comproportionation

Disproportionation: a single species simultaneously oxidises and reduces itself. Classic examples are 2Cu⁺ → Cu²⁺ + Cu in aqueous solution and 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. Comproportionation is the reverse direction. Both are signposts of an unstable intermediate oxidation state.

| Reaction type | Oxidation state shift | NEET exemplar |
|---|---|---|
| Disproportionation | One element → two different states | 2Cu⁺ → Cu²⁺ + Cu |
| Comproportionation | Two states → one common state | Cu + Cu²⁺ → 2Cu⁺ (rare, requires specific ligands) |
| Redox in same element | Different atoms, different states | MnO₂ + 4HCl → MnCl₂ + Cl₂ + 2H₂O |

#### Edge Cases in Oxidation-Number Rules

- **Oxygen in OF₂:** +2, not −2. Fluorine is more electronegative, so it pulls bonding electrons from oxygen.
- **Superoxide ion O₂⁻:** Each oxygen sits at −½ — fractional O.N.s are legitimate in polyatomic ions, not in neutral atoms.
- **Peroxides in organic chemistry:** Benzoyl peroxide (C₆H₅COO)₂ carries O at −1 within the O–O linkage but −2 in the carbonyl oxygens — O.N. rules apply atom by atom, not to functional groups wholesale.

#### Two Advanced Practice Prompts

1. A Daniel cell at 298 K starts with [Zn²⁺] = 1.0 M and [Cu²⁺] = 0.01 M. Compute $E_{\text{cell}}$ given $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = 1.10$ V. *Hint:* $n = 2$; $Q = [\text{Zn}^{2+}]/[\text{Cu}^{2+}] = 100$; $\log Q = 2$; answer rounds to ≈ 1.04 V.
2. K₂Cr₂O₇ (n = 6 in acid) reacts with FeSO₄. For 25 mL of 0.05 M K₂Cr₂O₇, find (a) equivalents of oxidant, (b) mass of Fe²⁺ consumed if FeSO₄ is 0.3 M, and (c) volume of FeSO₄ used. *Hint:* equivalents = M × n × V; equate with Fe²⁺ (n = 1); mass = equivalents × 56 g eq⁻¹.
