# Nuclei

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Fastest path through Nuclei before the CUET UG paper — read this twice the night before.

The atomic nucleus packs almost all of an atom's mass into a radius that obeys R = R₀·A^(1/3), with R₀ = 1.2 fm. Because volume grows with A while mass grows with A, density stays pinned near 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³, the single most counter-intuitive fact in this chapter.

#### Two formulas that matter

| Relation | Variables | Quick unit check |
|---|---|---|
| BE = Δm · c² | Δm = mass defect (kg), c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s | 1 u = 931.5 MeV/c² |
| N(t) = N₀ · e^(−λt) | λ = decay constant (s⁻¹), t in s | T₁/₂ = 0.693 / λ |

The binding-energy-per-nucleon curve peaks at roughly 8.5 MeV near A ≈ 56 (Fe region). Heavier nuclei release energy by splitting; lighter ones release energy by joining.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** "**Alpha = A−4, Z−2. Beta = Z±1, A same. Gamma = neither changes.**" — recite this in the hall before ticking the option on decay identity.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> The working layer: mechanics, derivations, and one fully solved exam-style numerical.

#### Composition and size of the nucleus

A nucleus of atomic number Z and mass number A contains Z protons and (A − Z) neutrons. Two nuclei sharing Z but differing in A are **isotopes**; sharing A but differing in Z makes them **isobars**; sharing neutron count (A − Z) makes them **isotones**.

The empirical radius formula, R = R₀·A^(1/3) with R₀ ≈ 1.2 fm, leads directly to nuclear density:

> ρ = 3·m_nucl / (4·π·R₀³) ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³

This number barely moves from A = 12 to A = 238, a useful invariant that lets CUET questions be solved without a calculator.

#### Mass defect and binding energy

Free nucleons weigh more than the bound nucleus by the **mass defect** Δm:

> Δm = [Z·m_p + (A − Z)·m_n − m_nucleus]

BE = Δm · c². Dividing BE by A gives binding energy per nucleon, which peaks around 8–8.5 MeV at A ≈ 56.

| Region of curve | What happens energetically | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Light (A < 20) | Curve rises steeply | Fusion releases energy |
| Middle (A ≈ 56) | Maximum stability | ⁵⁶Fe |
| Heavy (A > 200) | Curve falls gently | ²³⁵U fission releases energy |

#### Radioactive decay

Decay follows N(t) = N₀·e^(−λt), where λ is the decay constant in s⁻¹ and t is measured in seconds. The half-life is T₁/₂ = ln 2 / λ ≈ 0.693/λ. The activity A_act = λN gives decays per second, measured in becquerels (Bq) — a common multiple-choice trap asks students to confuse activity (decays/s) with λ (per s).

The three decay modes change A and Z in distinguishable ways, captured in the Lite-tier hook and reinforced here:

| Mode | Change in A | Change in Z | Identity of emitted particle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha (α) | −4 | −2 | ⁴₂He nucleus |
| Beta-minus (β⁻) | 0 | +1 | Electron + antineutrino |
| Beta-plus (β⁺) | 0 | −1 | Positron + neutrino |
| Gamma (γ) | 0 | 0 | Photon, no charge |

#### Worked problem — binding energy from mass defect

**Question.** ⁴He has a nuclear mass of 4.0026 u. Given m_p = 1.0073 u and m_n = 1.0087 u, compute the binding energy per nucleon in MeV.

#### Step 1 — mass of constituents.
Z·m_p + (A − Z)·m_n = 2(1.0073) + 2(1.0087) = 2.0146 + 2.0174 = 4.0320 u

#### Step 2 — mass defect.
Δm = 4.0320 − 4.0026 = 0.0294 u

#### Step 3 — convert using 1 u = 931.5 MeV/c².
BE = 0.0294 × 931.5 ≈ 27.39 MeV

#### Step 4 — per nucleon.
BE/A = 27.39 / 4 ≈ 6.85 MeV per nucleon, consistent with ⁴He sitting on the rising left shoulder of the curve.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students often write BE = Δm (in u) instead of multiplying by 931.5. If the answer choices are in MeV and your computation is in atomic mass units, the missing factor of ~932 is almost always why your option is missing.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Edge cases, limits, and two advanced prompts to push mastery past the CUET baseline.

#### Why the density stays flat

Because R ∝ A^(1/3), the volume V ∝ A. Since the nucleon mass also scales linearly with A, ρ = m/V becomes effectively A-independent. The σ(error) on ρ across the periodic table is below 5%, a stronger constancy than any other macroscopic density in nature. Slight deviations at the nuclear skin (a diffuse outer layer of ~0.5–1 fm) are explored in heavier research-level problems but are out of CUET scope.

#### Q-value, threshold, and direction of energy release

For any nuclear reaction, Q = (m_initial − m_final)·c². Q > 0 means kinetic energy is released; Q < 0 means kinetic energy must be supplied. Fission of ²³⁵U releases ~200 MeV per event; fusion of D + T → ⁴He + n releases 17.6 MeV. The huge energy per unit mass is the direct reason a 1 kg fusion reaction could, in principle, outproduce millions of kg of coal.

#### Mean life versus half-life

Mean life τ = 1/λ, while T₁/₂ = ln 2 / λ ≈ 0.693/λ. Hence τ ≈ 1.443·T₁/₂. Numerical traps disguise one as the other; always check the unit of the given constant before plugging into N(t).

#### Critical mass and chain reaction

A self-sustaining fission chain in ²³⁵U requires at least one neutron from each fission to induce another. The **critical mass** is the minimum mass for which the neutron multiplication factor k ≥ 1; moderators like graphite or heavy water thermalise neutrons to raise fission probability. CUET typically asks only the conceptual link between critical mass, moderator, and control rods — leave the precise mass figure to official NTA notifications, since exact reactor-dependent numbers vary.

> ⚠️ **Extended Trap:** Students write "binding energy = energy to form the nucleus." It is the opposite: BE is the energy required to **separate** a nucleus into its free nucleons. The wording flips the sign of intuition and is a recurring distractor.

#### Advanced practice prompts

1. A sample has N₀ = 4 × 10¹⁰ atoms and activity 6.4 × 10⁸ s⁻¹. Find T₁/₂ and the remaining count after 12 hours.
2. For the fusion ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n, with masses 2.0141 u, 3.0160 u, 4.0026 u, and 1.0087 u respectively, compute Q in MeV and identify whether the reaction is exothermic.
