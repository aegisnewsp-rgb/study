# Coordinate Geometry

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Coordinate geometry** links algebra and geometry by placing points as ordered pairs (x, y) on a flat **Cartesian plane** with a horizontal **x-axis**, a vertical **y-axis**, and an **origin** O(0, 0). The four **quadrants** are signed regions: (+,+) top right, (−,+) top left, (−,−) bottom left, (+,−) bottom right. The four must-know tools are **distance** d = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²), **midpoint** M = ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2), **gradient** m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁), and the **equation of a line** y = mx + c. Two lines are **parallel** when m₁ = m₂ and **perpendicular** when m₁·m₂ = −1. A **circle** centred (a, b) with radius r has equation (x − a)² + (y − b)² = r². NABTEB typically awards 10–20 marks here; expect at least one question on circle equation plus a line/gradient problem.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Distance, Midpoint and Gradient

For two distinct points P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂):
- **Distance** |PQ| = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²), which is the Pythagorean rule applied to the right triangle formed by the horizontal and vertical differences.
- **Midpoint** M = ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2); average the x-coordinates and the y-coordinates separately.
- **Gradient** m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁), the ratio of vertical change to horizontal change. If x₁ = x₂, the line is vertical and the gradient is undefined.

#### Equation of a Straight Line

A straight line in slope-intercept form is **y = mx + c**, where **m** is the gradient and **c** is the **y-intercept** (the point (0, c) where the line crosses the y-axis). To find **c** from a graph, read where the line cuts the y-axis. The **x-intercept** is found by setting y = 0, giving x = −c/m. A line through a known point (x₁, y₁) with gradient m uses the point-slope form **y − y₁ = m(x − x₁)**. Given two points, first compute m, then substitute either point to find c.

#### Parallel and Perpendicular Lines

Two lines are **parallel** when their gradients are equal: m₁ = m₂. Two lines are **perpendicular** when the product of their gradients is −1: m₁·m₂ = −1. If m₁ is, say, 2/3, then the perpendicular gradient is −3/2 (flip and negate).

#### Equation of a Circle

A circle is the **locus** of points equidistant from a fixed centre (a, b). With radius r, the equation is **(x − a)² + (y − b)² = r²**. If the centre is at the origin, it simplifies to x² + y² = r². Given three points on a circle, you can find the centre by solving the perpendicular bisectors of any two chords, then r from the distance formula.

#### Exam Question Patterns

NABTEB questions usually fall into these types: (i) calculate distance or midpoint from two coordinates; (ii) find the gradient of a line from two points, or the equation through a point with a stated gradient; (iii) test whether two lines are parallel, perpendicular, or neither; (iv) write the equation of a circle given centre and radius, or read centre and radius from an expanded equation; (v) solve two line equations simultaneously to find their intersection. Graph questions ask candidates to plot at least three points to draw a line or circle and read off intersections.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Deriving the Distance Formula

Start with P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂). Draw a horizontal line from P and a vertical line from Q; they meet at R(x₂, y₁), forming right triangle PRQ. The horizontal leg |PR| = x₂ − x₁ and the vertical leg |RQ| = y₂ − y₁. By Pythagoras, |PQ|² = (x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)², hence |PQ| = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²). Always use **absolute value** implicitly by squaring, so order of subtraction does not matter.

#### General Form of a Line and Circle

A line can also be written as **ax + by + c = 0**, with gradient m = −a/b and y-intercept = −c/b. A circle equation can be expanded: x² + y² − 2ax − 2by + (a² + b² − r²) = 0. To read off the centre and radius, complete the square for x and y separately, e.g. x² − 6x = (x − 3)² − 9.

#### Common Mistakes and Traps

- Inverting the gradient formula to (x₂ − x₁)/(y₂ − y₁) — remember the y-difference goes on top.
- Forgetting the square root in the distance formula.
- Writing the circle as (x + a)² + (y + b)² = r² when the centre is (a, b); the signs inside the brackets are **always minus** the centre's coordinates.
- Treating "both gradients negative" as proof of perpendicularity — check the product, not the signs.
- Confusing the y-intercept c with the x-intercept.

#### Worked Micro-Example

Find the equation of the perpendicular bisector of AB where A(2, 3) and B(8, 9). Gradient of AB = (9 − 3)/(8 − 2) = 1, so perpendicular gradient = −1. Midpoint of AB = ((2+8)/2, (3+9)/2) = (5, 6). Using y − 6 = −1(x − 5), we get y = −x + 11, or **x + y − 11 = 0**.

#### Practice Prompts

1. A circle has centre (−3, 4) and passes through the origin. Write its equation.
2. Line L₁ has equation 2y = 4x − 6 and L₂ passes through (1, 5) perpendicular to L₁. Find L₂ and the point where it meets the x-axis.
