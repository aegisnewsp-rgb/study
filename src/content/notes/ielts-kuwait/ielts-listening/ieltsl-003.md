# Part 3 — Academic Discussion — IELTS Listening Notes

Part 3 puts you in an academic discussion and asks you to hold an argument in your head while two people talk over each other about it. IELTS describes the section exactly this way: Parts 3 and 4 deal with educational and training situations, and in Part 3 there is a conversation between two main speakers, for example two university students in discussion, perhaps guided by a tutor (ielts.org, Listening test format). The other recorded fact worth knowing is structural: the test has four parts of 10 questions each, and the questions run in the same order as the information in the recording (ielts.org). So questions 31–40 arrive in sequence, and an item you miss does not push the next one out of reach.

That ordering guarantee is the reason Part 3 rewards preparation more than any other section. Parts 1 and 2 test whether you can catch a fact. Part 3 tests whether you can hold two positions, recognise which one you are tracking, and notice when the speakers change their minds — while a tutor interrupts and both students hedge. Most candidates who lose marks here do not have a vocabulary problem. They have a tracking problem.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Position:** third of four parts, so questions 31–40 in a standard set.
- **Questions:** 10. The count of 10 per part is fixed; what varies is which question types appear (ielts.org lists the number of questions for each type as "Variable").
- **Speakers:** two main speakers, "for example, two university students in discussion, perhaps guided by a tutor" (ielts.org, Listening test format). A third voice is common but is not one of the two main speakers.
- **Question types:** multiple choice, matching, plan/map/diagram labelling, form/note/table/flow-chart/summary completion, and sentence completion are the set IELTS uses across the Listening test. Part 3 draws mainly on multiple choice and matching.
- **Marking:** each correct answer receives 1 mark; there is no negative marking, so a blank never costs you (ielts.org, Listening test format).
- **Delivery in Kuwait:** both British Council Kuwait and IDP Education Salmiya run computer-delivered IELTS. British Council advertises daily computer-delivered sessions across multiple Kuwaiti venues (britishcouncil.com.kw). On computer you type answers as you listen, with no separate transfer time.

#### The five traps that cost marks in Part 3

1. **Writing the first opinion instead of the settled one.** A speaker opens with "I suppose we could...", argues a position, then says "actually, no — what I mean is...". The answer is the revised position.
2. **Attributing an opinion to the wrong speaker.** The recording alternates. Match the view to the person the question stem names, not to whoever spoke most recently before the answer landed.
3. **Confusing the example with the opinion.** Academic discussions are full of illustration. A tutor asks "what's wrong with the field data?" and the student answers with an anecdote about the coastline survey. The question asked for the problem, not the coastline.
4. **Missing the answer because it was said quickly.** The relevant clause lands in a subordinate position and the speaker moves on. This is a speed problem, not a comprehension problem.
5. **Overrunning the word limit.** IELTS states plainly: you lose the mark for writing more than the word limit (ielts.org). Hyphenated words such as "check-in" count as single words; contracted words such as "they're" are not tested.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### The four question families you will meet in Part 3

**Multiple choice.** IELTS describes it as "a question with three possible answers or the first half of a sentence with three possible sentence endings" (ielts.org, Listening test format). You are picking the one correct answer. In Part 3 the options paraphrase the speakers' claims, so the correct option rarely uses the recording's own wording.

**Matching.** You "match a list of items from the recording to a list of options, then choose the correct letter" (ielts.org). IELTS states this tests three things: listening for detailed information, following a conversation between two people, and "recognise how facts in the recording are connected to each other". That third skill is the one candidates under-train. In a matching set the options are a grid, and one option may be used more than once, used not at all, or both.

**Sentence completion and summary completion.** These share one rule set with form, note, table and flow-chart completion: fill a gap using the recording's words, "You do not have to change the words in the recording in any way" — and equally, you do not have to copy them. Both forms exist; the difference is that sentence completion gives you the sentence and you supply the missing part.

**Short answer.** IELTS: "read a question and write a short answer using information from the recording" (ielts.org). You are listening for facts — places, prices, times.

#### Speaker attribution is the whole game

This is the highest-leverage sub-skill in Part 3, and it is trainable on its own.

1. **Read the stem before the audio.** Write the speaker label you need at the top of each item: Student A, Student B, tutor, or whichever the question names.
2. **Catch attribution cues.** "I think", "the way I see it", "to be honest", "from my perspective" open a position. "Exactly", "Right", "I'd go along with that" confirm someone else's. "I'm not convinced", "that's not really true", "well, it depends" reopen it.
3. **Tag as you listen.** When a position arrives that matches a stem you flagged, note it against that speaker's label immediately. Do not hold ten opinions in memory.
4. **Wait for the turn to close.** A student who opens a clause and then modifies it has not yet made a claim. Move on only when the speaker lands.
5. **Answer the question asked.** If the stem asks what the tutor objects to, the students' agreement is context, not answer.

#### Kuwait-context academic scenarios

Practise the discussion shape with material drawn from how higher education and professional training actually work in Kuwait. The vocabulary will be familiar, which frees attention for the argument.

- Two students and a tutor revising a final-year project proposal at Kuwait University.
- Two students at a Public Authority for Applied Education and Training polytechnic comparing report formats for a technical report.
- Two candidates comparing research options ahead of a Kuwait Foundation for Advanced Studies postgraduate programme.
- Two students at Kuwait University of Science and Technology debating lab work versus fieldwork for a final project.
- Two trainees at an American University of Kuwait seminar comparing group assessment with individual assessment.
- Two students discussing a placement with a supervisor from an oil and gas or water and electricity operating company.
- A study group comparing revision methods, and arguing about whether group work actually helps.

None of these scripts recur on the day. What recurs is the shape: two positions, a reason each, an example, and usually partial agreement.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Why Part 3 separates candidates

Parts 2 and 4 are linear. One speaker, one thread, and a missed clause is simply lost. Part 3 adds three simultaneous loads — turn-taking, hedge, and revision — on top of academic vocabulary at native pace. A candidate can have every relevant word and still lose marks by writing Speaker B's view when the question asked for Speaker A's.

The Cambridge public band descriptors for Listening at band 7 require the ability to "follow extended speech on a wide range of topics, including some unfamiliar topics" and to "follow detailed reasoning and argument". Part 3 is where that descriptor is actually tested. Band 6 asks the candidate to follow extended speech; band 7 asks them to follow the reasoning inside it.

#### Diagnosing which sub-skill is failing

Score a full practice test part-by-part before choosing an intervention. The pattern in your answers tells you which drill to run.

- **Right idea, wrong speaker** — attribution problem. Run named-speaker interview drills: five minutes of audio, write one bullet per opinion attributed to the person who said it.
- **Right speaker, wrong version of the opinion** — revision-tracking problem. Drill with recordings where speakers concede and reverse. Mark each reversal in the transcript, then replay just those ten seconds.
- **Right answer, over the word limit** — instruction-reading problem. Before every item, say the limit out loud. Count hyphenated words as one.
- **Answers cluster at the end of each exchange** — you are listening to the whole utterance instead of scanning for the noun. Practise skipping the clause you can predict.
- **Blank answers throughout a matching set** — you lost the grid early. Treat a matching set as a single item worth five marks, not five separate items.

#### A six-week drill sequence

**Week 1 — one-minute grids.** Take a one-minute two-speaker clip. Build a grid of speakers against positions. Stop after each minute to write it. Accuracy over speed.

**Week 2 — timed Part 3 sets.** Take three full official practice tests. Do the Part 3 section under real timing. Mark it separately from the other three parts so the trend is visible.

**Week 3 — reversal hunting.** Work only on recordings containing self-correction. For every reversal, note the trigger word that announced it.

**Week 4 — paraphrase matching.** Write the options before listening. Then confirm whether the recording said the option's words or a paraphrase of them. You are building tolerance for paraphrase.

**Week 5 — Kuwait-context simulation.** Record your own three-minute discussion in a polytechnic or university setting with a classmate, with deliberate hedging and one reversal per speaker. Swap recordings and answer questions over it.

**Week 6 — full-length mock under test conditions.** Computer-delivered format, no pausing, answers typed as heard.

#### Edge cases worth rehearsing

1. **Speaker labels are not names.** If the stem says "the female speaker", write what she said, not her first name.
2. **Partial agreement is not agreement.** If a stem asks which view the speakers agree on and they agree only on the method, not the conclusion, the item is wrong.
3. **The tutor is not the answer machine.** Tutors ask and probe. Students carry the content.
4. **Numbers and units.** IELTS counts "30%" as one word and "30 percent" as two. Decide before you write.
5. **Blank beats wrong on nothing.** There is no negative marking, so never write a guess where you are genuinely uncertain and the stem has three plausible options.

### High-Yield — IELTS Listening Part 3

Seven scenarios that carry most of the Part 3 marks, with the response each one usually demands.

1. **Research project critique.** Two students present fieldwork to a tutor who questions sample size. The answer is the weakness the tutor names, not the limitation the students volunteer first.
2. **Study method comparison.** One student defends group work, the other defends solo revision. Matching captures which benefit each attributes to which method.
3. **Course or specialisation choice.** Two students weigh engineering, business and medical tracks. The decision often resolves on one stated reason — cost, length, or entry requirements.
4. **Fieldwork versus laboratory work.** A standard debate. Listen for the moment one speaker concedes a point about practicality rather than principle.
5. **Assessment format.** Individual versus group assessment, continuous versus end-of-term. Common in training-college contexts.
6. **Technology in teaching.** Recorded lectures, blended delivery, online tutorials. Expect hedging from both sides and a partial agreement at the end.
7. **Placement and industry readiness.** Two students compare what an employer values in a graduate. The distinguishing detail is usually a specific behaviour, not an adjective.

### Previous Year Patterns — IELTS Listening Part 3

The four-part structure is not a yearly choice. IELTS publishes it as the standard Listening format — four parts, 10 questions each, 40 questions in total, approximately 30 minutes — and it has survived every revision since the Academic and General Training split. A student planning a revision timetable can bank on Part 3 being the third academic-style section.

What does move is the question mix. IELTS lists the number of questions for each question type as "Variable" (ielts.org), so no candidate should assume a fixed count of multiple-choice items, or that matching always appears. Treat every published practice set as a sample of the types, not as a template.

Three patterns have been stable enough to plan around:

- The conversation is two main speakers, and a guiding tutor is described as the typical third voice.
- Answers appear in recording order, so preparation transfers across papers.
- Hedging and self-correction are structural features of academic discussion, which means the traps in this note recur too.

A past paper tells you the shape of the task. It does not tell you which word will carry the mark, and British Council and IDP refresh their question banks rather than recycling recordings verbatim.

### Pro Tips — IELTS Listening Part 3

1. **Label before you listen.** Write the speaker each item needs in the margin. Thirty seconds of setup removes the most common Part 3 error.
2. **Answer the closing position.** When a speaker revises, the revision is the answer. The original was a distractor the recording planted on purpose.
3. **Read matching options first.** Build the mental grid before the audio so you are classifying, not searching.
4. **Track connections, not just facts.** IELTS names this skill explicitly for matching: recognising how facts in the recording connect to each other.
5. **Count words.** IELTS states you lose the mark for exceeding the limit, and that hyphenated words such as "check-in" count as single words.
6. **Predict the grammatical slot.** A gap after "the main advantage of X is ___" wants a noun phrase. Predicting the shape narrows what you listen for.
7. **Type as you hear on computer delivery.** There is no transfer window to tidy up afterwards, so slow careful typing costs you the following item.
8. **Re-read your Part 3 answers in the gap before Part 4.** Part 4 starts with a longer instruction block; use the first of it on Part 3 corrections.
9. **Practise with several accents.** British Council's guide to IELTS states that different accents, "including British, Australian, New Zealand, and North American", are used.
10. **Do not revisit a lost item.** The order is guaranteed. Item 34's answer is ahead of item 33's, and chasing backwards costs you two marks to recover none.
