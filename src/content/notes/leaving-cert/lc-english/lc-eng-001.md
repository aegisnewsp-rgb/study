# Exploring — Leaving Certificate (Established) English Notes

Exploring is the engagement strand of the NCCA Leaving Certificate English specification: the habit of reading, viewing and listening carefully across a wide range of texts — spoken, written, visual and multimedia — and asking how each one constructs meaning through language, form and context. At Higher level the strand demands that a candidate move beyond personal response and into sustained critical reading: spotting how a writer's choices in a single line shape the reader's response, and tracing that choice back to genre, period or cultural setting. At Ordinary level the same engagement is required but the analytical vocabulary is less demanding, and the mark scheme rewards clear personal response backed by textual reference over dense theoretical argument.

The strand carries a weight rating of 4 out of 5 in the topic catalogue because it underpins almost every other component of the SEC paper. The exploration of texts is what gets tested in Paper 1 (the comprehension and language question), in Paper 2 (Section I — the single text, and Section II — the comparative), and in the leaving certificate oral (which is reported separately but draws on the same engagement habits). A candidate who cannot read a poem or a short prose extract and articulate what it is doing in plain English will lose marks across the entire paper, not just one section. The marks attached to this strand are dispersed, not bundled, so under-preparing it depresses the rest of the script at the same time.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Strand weight & spread**: Weight 4/5 in the topic catalogue. The marks attached to this strand are spread across Paper 1 (comprehension), Paper 2 (single text and comparative), and the leaving certificate oral — under-preparing it depresses every section.
- **Three text types, one habit**: Spoken (drama transcripts, podcasts), written (prose, poetry, articles), and visual/multimedia (film stills, advertisements, photographs) all call for the same response: identify the *what is being done*, the *how it is being done*, and the *why the choices matter in context*.
- **First move on an unseen text**: Read once for gist, once for the question, then a third time with a pen in hand marking the moment that *answers the question*. Most candidates never reach the third reading.
- **PETER principle for poetry**: *Process, Example, Technique, Effect, Response*. When you annotate a line, name the technique, anchor it to a word or phrase, then state the effect. Skipping the example is the single most common mark-loss on Higher level.
- **Film and visual text**: Treat a single frame or a sequence of shots the same way you'd treat a paragraph — note the composition, the lighting, the framing, the cut. The vocabulary is partly technical (shot types, mise-en-scène) and partly interpretive.
- **Context is not summary**: "Set in 19th-century Ireland" is background, not context. Context explains *why* the writer chose a particular form, register or image at this moment in the text. Mark scheme rewards contextual inference over biographical detail.
- **Comparative texts on the new spec**: At Higher level the comparative mode asks for *three* texts studied together for comparison, with a fourth text studied individually — confirm the live comparative brief on curriculumonline.ie and examinations.ie each year.
- **Spoken language markers**: Hesitations ("um", "like"), fillers, sentence fragments, and contractions are *features*, not errors, when transcribing or analysing spoken English. Mark scheme credit depends on treating them analytically.

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing personal response ("I enjoyed this") with critical reading ("the writer uses a fragmented sentence here to convey hesitation, which mirrors the speaker's uncertainty in line 12"). Personal response alone rarely earns more than a low tariff.
- Summarising the plot of a studied text instead of analysing it — the mark is for *how* the writer does it, not what happens next.
- Quoting a long passage (six lines or more) without picking out the *operative* phrase. Examiners reward precise, short quotation; long quotes suggest the candidate cannot locate the relevant words.
- Treating visual texts as illustrations of the written text. A film adaptation or a poster is a *separate* text with its own choices; the analysis applies the same habits, not the same answers.
- Ignoring register shifts inside a single text. Most marks on Paper 2 sit on the line where the writer changes tone or voice — mark scheme rewards noticing the shift and explaining its purpose.

#### 30-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Can you write one sentence on each of the following in under 30 seconds: imagery, tone, register, structure, diction?
- [ ] Have you practiced annotating an unseen poem for *technique + effect* in under five minutes?
- [ ] Can you identify three features of a single film still that an examiner would credit?
- [ ] Do you have one prepared paragraph on context that you can adapt, not copy, for any studied text?

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### The engagement habit and how it is examined

Engagement with a wide range of texts is, at its core, a habit rather than a knowledge base. The examiner is testing whether the candidate can apply the same five-step reading cycle — *notice, name, anchor, explain, evaluate* — to a poem they have never seen, a film still they have never watched, or a speech they have never heard. The studied texts on the SEC paper test a *different* skill: recall plus sustained argument across a longer arc. Both skills are required, but the unseen-reading skill is what separates a strong H4 from an H1, because every year roughly a third of the marks sit on texts the candidate has not met before.

The most efficient way to train the engagement habit is timed annotation drills. Take any unseen poem, time yourself for four minutes, and force yourself to mark three techniques and one effect. Do this three times a week for a month and the speed becomes automatic; the mark scheme's vocabulary stops feeling foreign.

#### Spoken, written and visual modes

Each mode demands a slightly different vocabulary but the same underlying discipline.

- **Spoken mode**: Transcribe pauses, fillers and overlaps as features. Note who has the floor, who interrupts, and what the silence does. The leaving certificate oral assesses a related skill — sustained, responsive engagement with a teacher — but the same critical vocabulary applies in the aural component of the paper if one is set.
- **Written mode**: Diction (word choice), syntax (sentence structure), imagery, tone, voice and structure are the six core handles. Pick the one that *best* answers the question on the paper — don't list them all.
- **Visual / multimedia mode**: Composition, framing, lighting, colour, focus and shot type for still images. For sequences, cuts, transitions, sound and duration. The vocabulary overlaps with film studies but the mark scheme does not require technical fluency — only accurate observation and inference.

#### Context, culture and time

Context is the mark scheme's quiet differentiator. A candidate who can locate a writer in a period, a movement, or a cultural moment — and explain *why* the writer's choices make sense in that location — outscores a candidate with a wider technical vocabulary but no contextual grounding. The trap is biographical detail: knowing when an author was born does not earn marks; knowing what literary movement they belonged to and how it shaped their *choices in this passage* does.

The new specification (in force from 2025 onwards) emphasises cross-cultural and cross-temporal engagement. A study plan that reads one Irish poet, one contemporary global poet and one pre-20th-century writer will cover the comparative breadth the syllabus is asking for. Confirm the live prescribed text list on examinations.ie before each academic year, as the SEC rotates the comparative texts.

#### Building an exploration routine

A weekly routine that converts the strand into recoverable marks:

- **Monday**: One unseen poem, four minutes, three techniques named, one effect written. Time strictly.
- **Wednesday**: One film still or visual extract, three features, one inference. Five minutes max.
- **Friday**: One spoken-text transcript (podcast clip, drama excerpt), note the turn-taking and the register shift. Five minutes.
- **Sunday**: Thirty minutes on a *studied* text, re-reading a chapter or scene with the question "what does this writer do that another writer in this period would not?" — the comparative lens.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Comparative engagement across three texts

The new specification's comparative mode (introduced in 2025 and revised in the live SEC paper from 2026 onwards) asks candidates to study *three* texts together in a sustained comparison, with one further text studied individually. Confirm the live comparative brief on curriculumonline.ie for each year — the prescribed rotation is published annually by the SEC.

A Higher-level comparative answer that earns H1 grade does three things in roughly equal measure: (a) it identifies a *thematic* link across the three texts (e.g. memory, identity, conflict) before quoting; (b) it grounds each comparative point in *short, precise* quotation from at least two of the texts in the same paragraph; (c) it draws a *conclusion* — a sentence that says "across these three writers, X is the central concern, but each arrives at it through Y". The mark scheme credits the conclusion as a discrete mark; many candidates end with a summary instead.

#### Engaging with Shakespeare at Higher level

At Higher level, Shakespeare is mandatory within the comparative mode. A common failure mode is treating Shakespeare as a "set text" to be memorised line-by-line; a more efficient use of time is to study one scene in detail (the court scene in *The Merchant of Venice*, the balcony scene in *Romeo and Juliet*, or the "To be or not to be" soliloquy in *Hamlet*) until the candidate can name four techniques and two effects without notes. Then transfer that depth to one or two further scenes for comparison.

The examiner rewards candidates who connect Shakespeare's *choices* to his theatrical context — the soliloquy as a stage convention, the prose/verse shift as a class marker, the shared culture of the audience. Treating Shakespeare as a writer working within a particular medium, rather than as a cultural monument, is what H1 candidates do.

#### Cross-cultural and multimedia literacy

The strand's reference to "spoken, written, visual, multimedia" and to engagement "across cultures and time" reflects a deliberate broadening of what counts as a text. A strong study plan includes a film or a graphic novel in the rotation alongside the poetry and prose. The mark scheme for the leaving certificate oral and for Paper 2 treats a film adaptation as a separate text — not as a substitute for the written source. Candidates who analyse the *choices* the director makes (the cut, the soundtrack, the framing) score higher than candidates who summarise the plot.

A practical edge case for the new specification is the podcast or TED-style talk as a spoken text. These are increasingly common on Paper 1 comprehension items. The analysis treats them as transcripts: note the speaker's register, the rhetorical structure (problem–solution, narrative–reflection), and the moments where the speaker shifts pace or emphasis for effect.

#### Common mistakes that depress the grade

- **Generic introductions** ("In this text, the writer explores many themes...") waste 20 seconds and earn zero marks. Open on the *specific* point you are about to make.
- **Plot summary** is the largest recoverable gap on Paper 2. If a sentence describes what happens next, cut it; if it describes how the writer makes it happen, keep it.
- **Biographical context** — knowing the writer's life story is not the same as understanding the context of the text. Examiners credit the second.
- **Forgetting the question stem** — re-read it after every paragraph and check the answer is still on point. Drifting off-question is the most common reason a script drops a grade.
- **Under-quoting at Higher level** — three short, precise quotations per comparative paragraph is the floor; one long quotation is rarely enough to anchor an argument.
