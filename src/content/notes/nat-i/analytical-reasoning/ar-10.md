# Statement and Assumptions

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Statement and Assumptions** is a logical-reasoning item type in NAT-I (NTS) Analytical Reasoning. A **statement** presents a claim or proposed action, and an **assumption** is an unstated premise that must be true for the statement to hold. The task is to pick the hidden premise that is logically indispensable — the **supplicit assumption**.

- **Statement**: the explicit assertion, policy, or course of action printed in the question stem.
- **Assumption**: an unstated, taken-for-granted belief that supports the statement.
- **Supplicit (must-be-true) assumption**: an assumption whose falsity would collapse the statement.
- **Implicit premise**: synonymous with assumption in NAT-I usage.
- **Course of action**: a desirable follow-up step, NOT an assumption.
- **Must-be-true test**: negate the candidate assumption; if the statement falls, the candidate is valid.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **S.A.M. — Statement, Assumption, Must-be-true.** Always run the negation test on each option: if denying it kills the statement, it is the answer.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Definitions and Logical Position

An **assumption** is the hidden bridge between evidence and claim. In NAT-I questions, four choices accompany the statement; in standard papers one is a valid supplicit assumption, while the other three are distractors that are either possible-but-non-essential, irrelevant, or actively weakening. The candidate must isolate the option whose denial makes the statement illogical.

Assumptions fall into three recurring forms:

- **Undeclared cause-effect** — the statement's action or claim rests on an unstated causal link.
- **Unstated condition** — a precondition the writer assumes is already satisfied.
- **Unchallenged value judgment** — a norm or preference taken for granted without proof.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Concept | Where it appears | How to test | Typical NAT-I signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Assumption | Hidden premise inside a statement | Negate it; the statement collapses | Words like *should*, *must*, *best*, *always* |
| Conclusion | What follows from the premises | Must be supported, not assumed | Closing sentence of a passage |
| Inference | A logical deduction from given data | Must be derivable, not assumed | In many papers in data-interpretation sets |
| Course of Action | A practical step to address the issue | Must be feasible and relevant | "What should be done?" framing |
| Strong Argument | Open reason supporting the claim | Stated explicitly, not hidden | Listed among reasons |

#### Standard Procedure for Solving

1. Read the statement twice; identify the claim, policy, or recommendation.
2. List every premise the author takes for granted but never writes.
3. Apply the **must-be-true test**: mentally negate each candidate assumption.
4. Eliminate any choice whose negation leaves the statement intact.
5. Eliminate any choice that restates an explicit point (it is a conclusion, not an assumption).
6. Eliminate any choice introducing outside facts the statement never implies.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

#### Question:
*Statement:* "The municipal corporation should impose a complete ban on the use of plastic bags in the city to prevent environmental degradation."

#### Assumptions:
1. Plastic bags are a major contributor to environmental degradation in the city.
2. Banning plastic bags will not affect small shopkeepers' livelihoods.
3. People will switch to cloth or paper bags if plastic bags are banned.
4. Other cities have successfully banned plastic bags.

#### Solution:
Apply the must-be-true test to each option.

- **(1)** Negate it: *Plastic bags are NOT a major contributor to degradation.* Under this negation, the justification for the ban collapses, so the statement becomes illogical. **Valid assumption.**
- **(2)** Negate it: *Banning WILL severely affect livelihoods.* The statement can still stand; the corporation can simply accept the cost. **Not must-be-true.**
- **(3)** Negate it: *People will NOT switch.* The ban can still be enforced regardless of consumer behaviour. **Not must-be-true.**
- **(4)** Negate it: *Other cities have NOT done so.* The corporation's policy can still be proposed. **Not must-be-true.**

#### Correct answer: (1).

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Option (3) feels plausible because bans "sound" useless without compliance, but the statement only proposes the ban — it does not assume the ban will succeed. NTS examiners deliberately use this surface-plausibility trap.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Boundary Conditions

Assumption questions contain subtle quantifier traps. A choice that begins with **"only," "always," "never," "none,"** or **"every"** over-generalises and is in most keys wrong, because the statement rarely demands such universal claims. Conversely, valid assumptions in many papers use softer language: **"some," "likely," "in this case,"** or simply omit quantifiers.

#### Advanced Trap Catalog

1. **Restatement trap** — the option rewrites the statement verbatim; it is a conclusion, not an assumption.
2. **External-fact trap** — the option adds data the statement never implies (population figures, dates, comparisons).
3. **Reverse-direction trap** — the option weakens or opposes the statement instead of supporting it.
4. **Over-quantified trap** — "only," "always," "never" claims that the statement cannot guarantee.
5. **Course-of-action confusion** — the option describes what *should be done next* rather than what is *taken for granted*.
6. **Double-barrelled trap** — the option packs two claims; if one is false, the whole option fails as an assumption.

#### Connection to Adjacent Topics

Statement and Assumptions sits beside **Statement and Conclusions**, **Statement and Arguments**, and **Statement and Courses of Action** on the NAT-I Analytical Reasoning axis. The same stimulus can be re-cast across these four formats, so mastering the negation test for assumptions also sharpens your handling of strength/weakness-of-argument items.

#### Practice Prompts

1. Identify the supplicit assumption in: *"Private schools should mandatorily include moral-science classes to improve student character."* List two distractors and explain why each fails the must-be-true test.
2. Rewrite the above statement so that option (3) from the worked problem — *compliance assumption* — becomes the actual must-be-true assumption. What single word must be added to the statement to flip the logic?

> 💡 **High-Yield Revision Hook:** Before marking an answer, run **N-E-R-O**: **N**egate the option, **E**liminate if the statement survives, **R**estatement check, **O**ver-quantifier scan. Four checks, ten seconds per question.
