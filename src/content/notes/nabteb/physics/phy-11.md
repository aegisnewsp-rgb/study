# Magnetic Field and Electromagnetism

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **magnetic field** is a region where magnetic forces act on magnets, current-carrying wires, and moving charges. Its strength is given by **magnetic flux density B** (unit: **tesla, T**), and the field around a long straight conductor forms concentric circles whose direction is found with the **right-hand grip rule** (thumb points along current, fingers curl along B).

The force on a straight conductor in a field is **F = BIL sin θ**, with direction from **Fleming's left-hand rule** (first finger = Field, second = Current, thumb = Motion). A coil with *N* turns per metre carrying current *I* in free space has internal field **B = μ₀ n I** (where μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A).

**Magnetic flux** through an area is **Φ = B A cos θ** (unit: **weber, Wb**). When flux changes, an EMF is induced: **ε = −N (dΦ/dt)** — this is **Faraday's law**, and the negative sign is **Lenz's law** (induced current opposes the change). NABTEB typically tests one 4-mark calculation on F = BIL or Φ, plus a definition/short note on induction.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Magnetic Forces on Conductors and Charges
When a current *I* flows through a length *L* of wire in a magnetic field of flux density *B*, the force is **F = BIL sin θ**, where θ is the angle between the wire and field. This force is maximum when the wire is perpendicular to the field (sin θ = 1) and zero when parallel. For a single moving charge *q* travelling at velocity *v*, the Lorentz force becomes **F = qvB sin θ**.

**Fleming's left-hand rule** fixes the direction: thumb = force/motion, forefinger = field (N→S), middle finger = current (conventional, + to −).

#### Solenoids and Electromagnets
A **solenoid** is a helix of wire; when current flows, it behaves like a bar magnet with **north and south poles determined by the right-hand grip rule** (curl fingers in current direction, thumb points to N). Inserting a soft iron core multiplies the field many times because iron has very high **permeability (μ)**, producing an **electromagnet** used in relays, bells, and scrapyard cranes. When all domains align, the iron reaches **magnetic saturation** and increasing current further produces no extra field.

#### Electromagnetic Induction
**Faraday's law** states the induced EMF in a coil of *N* turns is **ε = −N (dΦ/dt)**. **Lenz's law** explains the minus sign: the induced current always opposes the change in flux (an application of energy conservation, since work must be done against this opposing force to keep flux changing).

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Flux density | B | tesla (T) = Wb/m² |
| Magnetic flux | Φ | weber (Wb) |
| Permeability (free space) | μ₀ | 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A |

#### Transformers and Energy Transfer
An ideal transformer obeys **Vₛ/Vₚ = Nₛ/Nₚ = Iₚ/Iₛ**. Step-up transformers raise voltage and lower current (used in grid transmission to reduce I²R losses), while step-down transformers do the reverse for domestic supply. Real transformers lose energy through resistance heating, eddy currents in the iron core, and hysteresis; laminating the core and using soft iron minimise these.

#### Typical NABTEB Question Patterns
- Calculate force/turns/flux using F = BIL sin θ, Φ = BA cos θ, or ε = −N(dΦ/dt) — usually 3–4 marks.
- Describe an experiment to demonstrate Oersted's effect (current producing a magnetic field).
- Explain the working principle of an **AC generator (alternator)**, **DC motor**, or **transformer** with a labelled diagram.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Generators and Motors: Reversed Machines
An **AC generator (alternator)** rotates a rectangular coil at angular velocity ω in a uniform field; flux through the coil varies as Φ = BA cos ωt, so ε = NBAω sin ωt — a sinusoidal EMF. Slip rings give continuous AC output. A **DC generator (dynamo)** swaps the slip rings for a **split-ring commutator**, which reverses the external connection every half-cycle, rectifying the output to unidirectional (though still rippled) current. A **DC motor** is the same physical device run in reverse: supply current to the coil in the field, the forces on the two vertical sides produce a couple τ = NBIA cos θ that rotates the coil; a commutator keeps the torque unidirectional.

#### Self-Induction and Mutual Induction
A changing current in a coil produces a changing flux that, by Faraday's law, induces a back-EMF in the same coil — this is **self-inductance L**, with ε = −L (dI/dt) (unit: henry, H). When flux from one coil links a neighbouring coil, the effect is **mutual induction M**, the principle of the transformer. The energy stored in an inductor's magnetic field is **E = ½ L I²**.

#### Common Mistakes to Avoid in NABTEB
- Confusing **Fleming's left-hand rule (motor)** with **Fleming's right-hand rule (generator)** — left is for motors, right is for generators.
- Forgetting that the angle in F = BIL sin θ and Φ = BA cos θ is the same angle measured differently (between conductor and field vs between field and area normal).
- Writing "induced current creates energy" — Lenz's law shows it *opposes* the change, conserving energy.
- Treating transformers as 100% efficient; in NABTEB questions include the qualifying phrase "assuming an ideal transformer" before quoting the voltage/turns ratio.

#### Worked Example
A straight wire of length 25 cm carrying a current of 4 A lies perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field of flux density 0.30 T. (i) Calculate the force on the wire. (ii) The same wire is then moved out of a region of flux density 0.20 T and area 0.050 m² in 0.50 s; find the average induced EMF, assuming the coil has 1 turn.
- (i) F = BIL = 0.30 × 4 × 0.25 = **0.30 N**.
- (ii) Φ = BA = 0.20 × 0.050 = 0.010 Wb; ε = −N(ΔΦ/Δt) = 0.010 / 0.50 = **0.020 V = 20 mV**.

#### Practice Prompts
1. State Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction and explain, with the aid of a diagram, how Lenz's law determines the direction of the induced current when a bar magnet is withdrawn from a coil.
2. A transformer has 1200 turns on the primary and 80 turns on the secondary. If the primary is connected to a 240 V AC supply delivering 0.50 A, calculate the secondary voltage and current (assuming 100% efficiency), and state one reason why real transformers are not perfectly efficient.
