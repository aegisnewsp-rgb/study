# Comprehension

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Reading Comprehension (RC) is the single highest-yield zone in the CLAT English section, which itself forms about **25%** of the 120-mark paper. In standard papers **4–5 passages** appear with roughly **10 questions** drawn from passages of 300–1000 words. The Consortium of NLUs prescribes four skill levels: **literal, inferential, evaluative, and analytical** reading.

| Skill Level | What It Tests | Trigger Words in Questions |
|---|---|---|
| Literal | Direct recall of facts | "According to the passage...", "The author mentions..." |
| Inferential | Reading between the lines | "The author implies...", "It can be inferred..." |
| Evaluative | Tone, purpose, attitude | "The author's tone is...", "The passage is primarily concerned with..." |
| Analytical | Argument structure, devices | "The author uses X to...", "Which of the following weakens the argument?" |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"LIMES"** — **L**iteral, **I**nferential, **M**ain idea, **E**valuation (tone/purpose), **S**tructure (argument/ devices). Read the first and last paragraph first, then every transition word ("however", "therefore", "in contrast").

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Anatomy of a CLAT Passage

CLAT RC passages fall into five recurring types — **factual/editorial**, **literary**, **narrative**, **argumentative**, and **abstract/philosophical**. Each demands a slightly different reading speed. Factual editorials from *The Hindu* or *The Economist* require close reading because every clause carries an assertion; abstract passages reward slow first-pass reading to map the argument before chasing options.

A passage is best attacked in three passes: **(1) Skim** the first sentence, topic shifts, and the final paragraph to lock the **central idea**; **(2) Mark** the author's **tone markers** (words of certainty, qualification, emotion); **(3) Locate** specific lines when a question references a detail.

#### Distinguishing Question Types

| Question Type | What Examiner Wants | Wrong-Answer Pattern to Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Main Idea / Title | A statement fully supported by the whole passage | Options that mention only one paragraph or are too broad/narrow |
| Inference | Something strongly suggested but not literally stated | Options directly copied from the text (those are literal, not inferential) |
| Tone / Attitude | The author's emotion-laden word choice | Confusing reader emotion with author tone |
| Vocabulary-in-Context | Meaning shaped by surrounding sentence | Choosing the dictionary's most common meaning over the contextual one |
| Fact vs Opinion | Whether the claim can be verified or is a judgment | Marking any strong statement as opinion without checking verifiability |
| Paired Passage | Which author agrees, where they diverge | Attributing Author A's argument to Author B |

#### Strategy for the 9-Minute Passage

A passage of 500 words with 3–4 questions should take roughly **9 minutes** — about 2 minutes per question on average. Read actively with a pen: underline the **thesis sentence**, circle **contrast words** (but, however, yet), and box **evaluative adjectives** (lamentable, admirable). When two options seem possible, return to the text and pick the one **more directly supported** by the author's own words.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

#### Question:
*"The passage criticizes a piece of modern legislation for privileging procedural speed over deliberative justice. Which of the following most accurately states the author's central claim?"*
(A) The legislation is unconstitutional.
(B) The legislation prioritizes efficiency at the cost of fairness.
(C) Deliberative justice is impossible in modern legislatures.
(D) Procedural speed has no place in lawmaking.

#### Solution:
Step 1 — Locate the thesis. The first sentence uses the verb *"criticizes"* and the phrase *"privileging procedural speed over deliberative justice"* — this is the author's claim.
Step 2 — Match the claim to the options. Option (B) — *"prioritizes efficiency at the cost of fairness"* — is a paraphrase of the author's words (procedural speed = efficiency; deliberative justice = fairness).
Step 3 — Eliminate overreachers. Option (A) introduces *unconstitutionality*, which the passage never asserts. Option (C) is an extreme universal ("impossible"). Option (D) is absolute ("no place").

#### Answer: (B)

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students in many papers pick (A) because the word "criticism" sounds legal-constitutional. Central idea must be **fully supported**; an unsupported leap, no matter how plausible, is wrong. Also watch for **absolute qualifiers** ("no place", "impossible", "always") — they are almost always decoys.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### The Inference Engine: From Text to Conclusion

Inference questions demand a **defensible bridge** between the text and the answer. The rule of three: the inference must be **(a) consistent** with explicit statements, **(b) supported by at least two textual cues**, and **(c) not contradicted by any passage line**. Single-cue inferences are fragile and in most keys lose to a stronger option. A 2023 CLAT passage on judicial discretion, for instance, asked why the author cited a 1978 dissent — the right answer relied on *both* the historical reference and the author's earlier criticism of majority reasoning, not on the dissent alone.

#### Argument Mapping for Analytical Passages

Analytical passages require mapping **claim → evidence → warrant → counter-argument → rebuttal**. The *warrant* is the unstated assumption linking evidence to claim, and CLAT increasingly tests it. To map quickly: write "BECAUSE" after each evidence sentence and "THEREFORE" before each conclusion sentence. Claims that survive without evidence are **assertions**, not arguments.

| Argument Component | Clue Words | Examiner's Favourite Question |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | "argue", "contend", "maintain" | "The author's primary assertion is..." |
| Evidence | "for example", "data show", "in 2019" | "Which of the following, if true, would strengthen/ weaken?" |
| Warrant | (unstated, implicit) | "The author's argument relies on which assumption?" |
| Counter-argument | "however", "critics argue" | "Which objection does the author acknowledge?" |
| Rebuttal | "but", "yet", "still" | "How does the author respond to critics?" |

#### Edge Cases and Examiner Favourites

1. **Negation traps** — *"Which of the following is NOT true?"* — students lose ~30% accuracy by reading the stem as positive.
2. **The "echo" trap** — an answer uses the passage's own nouns and verbs but shifts their meaning. Always re-read the sentence around the original word.
3. **Tone drift in long passages** — author tone can shift between paragraphs; the *dominant* tone (≥60% of evaluative cues) wins.
4. **Paired-passage attribution** — when Passages A and B argue opposing views, each author's argument stays with that author unless the passage explicitly states otherwise.
5. **Qualifier blindness** — "some judges", "in many papers", "can" weaken claims; "all", "always", "never" strengthen them. Misreading qualifiers flips the answer on about 1 in 5 inference questions.

#### High-Impact Practice Prompts

1. Take any 600-word editorial from *The Hindu* or *The Indian Express*. Without reading the questions, write a one-line **central idea**, list the author's **tone markers**, and identify one **assumption** underlying the argument.
2. Solve a CLAT 2022–2024 paired passage and mark every transition word. For each, decide whether it signals **agreement, contrast, causation, or elaboration** — these four categories cover ~85% of analytical questions.
