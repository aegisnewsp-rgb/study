# Partnership Law & Business Structures

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **partnership** under South African law is *"the relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit"* (Partnership Act 9 of 1989, s.1). Four elements must coexist: two or more persons, carrying on a business, in common, and with a profit motive.

> **One-line mnemonic — "PPBP":** **P**ersons + **P**rofitable **B**usiness carried on in common → partnership.

- **No separate legal personality** — the firm is not a juristic person; partners are principals *and* agents of one another (mutual agency, ss.5–7).
- **Joint and several liability** under s.13 — every partner is personally liable to the full extent of their personal assets for firm debts.
- **Liability ≠ Company** — a (Pty) Ltd has its own legal personality and shareholders enjoy limited liability; a partnership does not.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Sources Governing Partnerships
South African partnership law rests on a **hybrid base**: the Partnership Act 9 of 1989 (uniform national statute) layered with residual **common law**. **Limited partnerships** sit under a separate older statute — the Limited Partnerships Act 24 of 1907.

#### Four Essential Elements (s.1)
1. **Two or more persons** (natural or juristic — at least 20 in a *limited* partnership).
2. **Carrying on a business** — repeated, lawful commercial activity (not a single venture).
3. **In common** — with a *community of interest* in the business, not merely shared property.
4. **With a view of profit** — the motive must be to generate distributable profits; charitable clubs and non-profit associations fail this test.

#### Partnership vs Company vs Close Corporation vs Co-operative
| Feature | Partnership | (Pty) Ltd Company | Close Corporation | Co-operative |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Legal personality | None (partners = firm) | Separate legal person | Separate legal person | Separate legal person |
| Founding instrument | Deed of partnership (optional) | Memorandum of Incorporation | Founding statement | Constitution |
| Statute | Act 9 of 1989 | Companies Act 71 of 2008 | Close Corporations Act 69 of 1984 | Co-operatives Act 14 of 2005 |
| Liability of members | Joint and several (unlimited) | Limited to shareholding | Limited to contribution | Limited to member's share |

#### Mutual Agency and Implied Authority
Each partner is an **agent** of the **firm** and of the **co-partners** for acts done in the **ordinary course of business** (s.7). Such acts bind the firm and the other partners personally. Acts *outside* the ordinary course do not bind a partner who has not authorised them — though the firm may still be liable to innocent third parties under s.6 if the partner had **ostensible authority**.

#### Liability Rules (s.13 & s.14)
- **s.13** — Every partner is **jointly and severally** liable with the other partners for all debts and obligations incurred while they were a partner, after default by any partner on a firm obligation.
- **s.14** — An **incoming partner** is liable only for debts arising *after* admission, unless they expressly agree otherwise.

#### Typical Exam Question Patterns
- Scenario identification: *"Do these facts constitute a partnership?"* — apply the four elements.
- Liability vignettes featuring a **silent partner**, **nominal partner**, or **ostensible partner**.
- Distinguish partnership dissolution (ss.29–35) from withdrawal of a single partner (s.31).

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Categories of Partner
- **Real/active partner** — participates in management; fully liable.
- **Silent (sleeping) partner** — takes no part in management; **still jointly and severally liable** to third parties (s.13). Silence does not insulate.
- **Nominal partner** — lends their name but holds no real interest; still liable to those who *acted in good faith believing them a true partner* (s.16).
- **Ostensible partner** — held out, by words or conduct, as a partner; liable to creditors who relied on that representation.
- **Limited partner** (Limited Partnerships Act 24 of 1907) — contributes capital, takes no part in management, and liability is **capped at contribution**; contravening the no-management rule forfeits limited status.

#### Dissolution Mechanics
A partnership is **dissolved** by (a) agreement, (b) notice in a partnership at will, (c) death/insolvency of a partner (subject to contrary agreement), (d) court order on just grounds, or (e) illegality. Upon dissolution under **ss.39–44**, account is settled: **losses** are paid from **profits**, then from **capital**, then **individually** by partners *pro rata* to their profit-share — the inverse of contribution. Goodwill is included in valuation only where the deed provides.

#### Partnership vs Co-ownership
Sharing title to property alone is **co-ownership**, not a partnership; the *business + profit motive* overlay is decisive. Conversely, an agreement to share *gross returns* is generally **not** a partnership (s.2 proviso), while sharing *net profits* raises a *rebuttable presumption* of partnership.

#### Common Mistakes Examiners Exploit
1. Treating the **firm** as a legal person — it is not; pleadings name the *partners individually*.
2. Forgetting **s.14** — making incoming partners liable for old debts on default.
3. Assuming a **deed of partnership** is compulsory; statute supplies default rules in ss.5–14.
4. Conflating **dissolution of the firm** with **ceasing to be a partner** (s.31).

> **Trap:** "A silent partner has limited liability." → False. Silence changes visibility, not liability under s.13.

#### Practice Prompts
1. Five friends run an unregistered bakery, share profits equally, and one — *who never visits the bakery* — lends his name and capital. A supplier sues for unpaid invoices. Identify the partnership, classify each partner, and state each one's liability.
2. A new partner joins a firm two days before a debt arose; the creditor demands payment from the new partner only. Apply s.13 and s.14 to determine liability.
