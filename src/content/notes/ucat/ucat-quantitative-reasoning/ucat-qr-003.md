A basic on-screen calculator is available for the Quantitative Reasoning subtest (the official Consortium test tools page also lists it for Decision Making). The instinct is to use it for every question. The candidates who score highest in QR use the calculator on roughly half of the items and run mental arithmetic for the rest. The reason is that the calculator popup takes focus from the screen, costs eye movement, and slows each keypress relative to doing the arithmetic in your head for simple numbers. This note separates the questions where the calculator pays from the questions where it costs.

Standard subtest facts to keep straight: 36 QR questions in 26 minutes after a two-minute instruction section, scored on the published 300 to 900 scale with one mark per correct answer and no negative marking. Anything about cohort averages, percentile thresholds, or university cut-offs should be checked live on ucat.ac.uk before your sitting, since those figures change each year.

---

### Quick revision

- **Default: mental first, calculator second.** Run the arithmetic in your head; reach for the calculator only if the numbers have three or more digits.
- **Three keystrokes is the boundary.** If the calculation needs three or more calculator operations, the calculator is faster. Two or fewer and your head wins.
- **Verify, do not solve.** Use the calculator to check a mental result when the answer choices are close together.
- **Don't reach across screens.** Each calculator open and close is half a second. The mouse cost compounds.
- **Multiplication of three- and four-digit numbers, division of decimals, and percentages applied to decimal numbers all want the calculator.** Two-digit times one-digit, percentage multipliers and simple ratios do not.

### Standard conceptual deep dive

#### What the calculator is and is not

The UCAT on-screen calculator is a basic arithmetic tool — add, subtract, multiply, divide, percentage, sign change, memory. It does not have a fraction mode, a square root button or a unit converter. It is built into the interface and pops up over the question. You do not have to close it to read the question; you can leave it open and read through it.

The trap is that "available" reads as "necessary." It is not. UCAT questions are designed so that the mental arithmetic is faster than the calculator for most operations. The exam's design goal is to test whether you can locate the right calculation, not whether you can punch in three numbers.

#### Five calculations that are faster mentally

These appear in nearly every QR paper. Each takes less than five seconds in your head.

**Single-digit multiplication tables.** 7 × 8 = 56, 6 × 9 = 54, 8 × 9 = 72. If a question reduces to one of these, the calculator is a waste of two seconds.

**Percentage multipliers (recap from the previous note).** 15% off = 0.85, 20% VAT = 1.20, 30% increase = 1.30. Memorise the common multipliers as you would memorise a vocab list. The question is then one multiplication: 240 × 0.85 = 204.

**Simple ratios.** A 3 : 5 split of 240 → one part = 30, A = 90, B = 150. Two divisions, one multiplication, all one-digit once you split.

**Halving, doubling, thirding.** 240 / 8 = 240 / 4 / 2 = 60 / 2 = 30. Chained halving is faster than the calculator for any divisor under 8 that is a power of two.

**Reading fractions of familiar numbers.** 25% of 240 = 60. 75% of 240 = 180. 10% of 350 = 35. These are 240 × ¼ and 350 × ⅒. Treat them as single-digit multiplications.

#### Four calculations that want the calculator

These appear often enough that opening the calculator once is cheaper than doing them in your head.

**Three-digit by three-digit multiplication.** 247 × 18 = ? In your head: 247 × 20 = 4,940, minus 247 × 2 = 494, gives 4,446. That is a calculation you can check on the calculator. The mental route is faster for some candidates after weeks of drill, but the calculator is unambiguous for the rest.

**Division of a large number by a small non-factor.** 1,648 / 14. Mental factoring of 14 is 2 × 7, so 1,648 / 14 = (1,648 / 2) / 7 = 824 / 7 ≈ 117.7. Most candidates type this one.

**Percentage applied to a decimal value.** What is 17.5% of £382.40? The 0.175 multiplier makes the question calculator-shaped.

**Square roots and powers that involve a prime.** 5² = 25, that is mental. 13² = ? — calculator. Cube roots similarly. The calculator is the right call when the mental answer is "I think …?"

#### Worked example — mental wins

```
  Question: "A coffee shop sells 240 lattes per day. After a marketing
  push, sales rise by 15%. What is the new daily figure?"

  Mental route:
    Multiplier for 15% rise: 1.15.
    240 × 1.15 = 240 + 0.15 × 240 = 240 + 36 = 276.

  Calculator route:
    Open calculator.
    Type 240 × 1.15.
    Read result 276.
    Close calculator.

  Net difference: 5 seconds saved, every time this pattern appears.
```

#### Worked example — calculator wins

```
  Question: "A patient is prescribed a drug at 2.3 mg per kg of body
  weight, three times a day. The patient weighs 67.4 kg. What is the
  total daily dose?"

  Calculator route:
    2.3 × 67.4 = 154.94 per dose.
    154.94 × 3 = 464.82 mg per day.

  Mental route:
    2.3 × 67.4 ≈ 2 × 67.4 + 0.3 × 67.4 = 134.8 + 20.22 = 155.02.
    Three times a day: 155 × 3 = 465.

  The mental route rounds along the way and lands close but not
  exact. The calculator gives the precise value. The answer choices
  will let you tell which one is correct; if they are tight, use
  the calculator to confirm.
```

#### Worked example — where over-reliance costs you the question

```
  Question: "Two drugs are mixed 3 : 7. The cheaper drug is £14 per
  gram. The total value of the mixture is £252. How much does the
  more expensive drug cost per gram?"

  Trap route (calculator-heavy):
    Open calculator.
    Type 252 × 3 / 10 = 75.6 (cost contribution from cheaper drug).
    Subtract to find the rest: 252 − 75.6 = 176.4 (cost contribution
    from more expensive drug).
    Realise 176.4 / 7 = £25.20 per gram.
    Realise you needed the cost-per-gram of the cheaper drug first to
    find the ratio of cost-per-gram, then re-do the calculation.
    Spend 80 seconds on a question where 40 would have done.

  Cleaner route (mental first):
    One part of the ratio = 252 / 10 = £25.20.
    So the cheaper-drug portion (3 parts) costs 3 × £25.20 worth of
    mixture, and the more expensive (7 parts) costs 7 × £25.20.
    The expensive drug's mixture contribution = 7 × 25.20 = £176.40.
    The expensive drug is 7 parts out of 10 parts by mixture weight
    = 70% of the mixture.
    But the question asks for cost per gram. So we still need the
    weight contributed by the expensive drug.
    Cheaper portion = 3 parts = 3 × £25.20 = £75.60. Divide by the
    cheaper's £14 per gram = 5.4 g.
    Total mixture = 252 / mix cost per gram. Wait, mixture gram cost
    is not given.

  Realising you don't need the cheaper drug at all: the cost contribution
  per part = £25.20 of total for ten parts. The expensive drug contributes
  7 parts of weight. So cost-per-gram for expensive drug = (7 × 25.20)
  / 7g = £25.20 per gram. The ratio works out symmetrically when each
  drug is at one price.

  This is a contrived example, but the lesson is real: if the
  calculation needs a setup you haven't decided yet, the calculator
  is not the problem — the work-flow is. Slow down to identify the
  calculation, then decide whether you need the calculator.
```

#### The verification habit

Use the calculator to verify, not to solve. After computing mentally, check the answer with the calculator only when:

- The answer choices are clustered within 5% of each other.
- The arithmetic involved a decimal or three-digit number.
- The previous question in the set was a "same shape" question and you want to confirm the pattern held.

When answer choices are spread out (one near zero, one near 200, one near 600), the mental estimate is enough. The calculator is for the borderline cases where rounding might push you to the wrong option.

#### Common misuses

- **Carrying forwards a previous calculator result.** Each question should be a fresh calculation unless the stem says otherwise.
- **Using the percentage button when the multiplier is simpler.** The "5%" button on most calculators gives 0.05, not the 1.05 multiplier you actually want. Type the multiplier manually.
- **Rounding too aggressively.** Mental arithmetic rounds to two significant figures. The calculator gives exact values. Use the calculator's exact value when the answer choices are tight.

#### How to drill

Ten days of calculator discipline drills:

1. **Same question, two passes.** One timed with the calculator, one without. Record the time on each.
2. **Calculator-free days.** Use no calculator for a full QR set. Force the multiplier trick and the chain-halving trick.
3. **Mental-only flashcard round.** 30 questions, simple-multiplication and simple-percentage, five seconds per answer.
4. **Calculator-only mock.** Use the calculator on every question. Time the run. Compare to a calculator-free run.

The comparison reveals the questions where the calculator helps and the questions where it is your crutch.

### Memory anchors

- **Mental first.** Always.
- **Use the calculator when there are three or more digits in any operand.**
- **Verify with the calculator when the answer choices are within 5% of each other.**
- **The percentage button gives 5%, not ×1.05.** Type the multiplier.
- **Each calculator open and close is half a second.** Track your mouse time.

---

### Common traps

- Reaching for the calculator because you "always use it," instead of because you need it.
- Using the percentage button instead of the (1 + p) multiplier.
- Trusting a calculator answer for a question where you typed the wrong value. The on-screen calculator does not flag misentry.
- Forgetting to close the calculator popup before clicking on the next question. The popup blocks part of the screen on some browsers.
- Doing the four simple-arithmetic cases in your head with no estimate, and accepting a wildly wrong answer because the digits looked plausible.

---

### What to do next

1. Time ten QR questions twice: first calculator-free, then calculator-allowed. Record both times. The difference is your personal speed-up.
2. Build a flashcard set for the percentage multipliers (5%, 8%, 12%, 15%, 20%, 25%, 30%) and drill both forms (× 0.05 vs × 0.95).
3. On the next two timed mocks, count how many times you open the calculator. Aim to cut it by a third.
4. Spend one practice block on questions you cannot solve with mental arithmetic only — these are the ones that genuinely need the calculator. Anything else, leave the calculator closed. The official UCAT practice tests are the right benchmark, and the per-subtest scoring scale (300-900) is on the UCAT Consortium test-format page at ucat.ac.uk; cohort figures and percentile bands are published there each year and should be checked at booking time.
