# Metallurgy

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Metallurgy is the branch of chemistry that covers **extracting metals from ores**, **purifying them**, and **alloying** them for use. Every extraction flows through four stages: **concentration of ore → conversion to oxide → reduction → refining**.

The key thermodynamic rule is the **Ellingham diagram**: a metal whose oxide line lies lower on a ΔG° vs T plot can reduce the oxide of a metal whose line lies higher. The lines for **C → CO** and **CO → CO₂** slope downward, which is why carbon reduces FeO and ZnO only at high temperatures.

- **Calcination** = heating carbonate/hydrated ores in **limited/absence of air** (CO₂/H₂O released).
- **Roasting** = heating sulphide ores in **excess air** (SO₂ released, oxide forms).
- **Froth flotation** is selective for **sulphide ores**; gangue is wetted by water, ore by oil.
- **Electrolytic refining** uses Faraday's law: **m = (I × t × M) / (n × F)**, F = 96500 C mol⁻¹.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Four Stages of Extraction

1. **Concentration** — removes gangue using hydraulic washing, magnetic separation, froth flotation, or leaching (e.g., bauxite → NaAlO₂ with NaOH).
2. **Conversion to oxide** — calcination for carbonates/hydrates; roasting for sulphides.
3. **Reduction** — C, CO, H₂, Al (thermite), or self-reduction (e.g., Cu₂S + Cu₂O → Cu + SO₂).
4. **Refining** — distillation, liquation, electrolytic, zone refining, Van Arkel, Mond process.

#### Flux, Gangue, and Slag

**Flux** is added during smelting to combine with **gangue** (earthy impurity) and form a fusible **slag**. Acidic flux (SiO₂) pairs with basic gangue (CaO, FeO); basic flux (CaO, MgO) pairs with acidic gangue (SiO₂). Slag is used in road-making and cement.

#### Ellingham Diagram and Reducing Agents

The Ellingham diagram plots ΔG° of oxide formation against temperature. Most metal oxide lines slope upward (ΔS° < 0 for 2M + O₂ → 2MO), but the **C → CO line** slopes sharply downward because ΔS° is strongly positive. Where a carbon line crosses below a metal oxide line, carbon reduction becomes thermodynamically spontaneous.

| Reducing agent | Best for | Temperature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carbon (C) | Zn, Fe, Sn | High (>1000 K for Zn) |
| Carbon monoxide (CO) | Fe (FeO, Fe₃O₄) | Moderate (lower blast-furnace zones) |
| Hydrogen (H₂) | CuO, WO₃ | Moderate |
| Aluminium (Al) | Cr₂O₃, Fe₂O₃ (thermite) | Any (ΔG°(Al₂O₃) very negative) |
| Electrolysis of fused salt | Na, K, Ca, Mg, Al | — |

Metals high in the activity series (Na, K, Ca, Mg, Al) sit at the top of the Ellingham chart and **cannot be reduced by C or CO** — fused-salt electrolysis is the only practical route.

#### Refining Methods Matched to Metals

| Metal | Refining method | Principle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cu, Zn, Ni | Electrolytic | Anode dissolves, pure metal deposits at cathode |
| Sn | Liquation | Sn melts and flows out of solid impurities |
| Hg, Zn | Distillation | Volatile metal vapour condensed back |
| Ti, Zr | Van Arkel | TiI₄ vapour decomposed on hot tungsten wire |
| Ni | Mond process | Ni(CO)₄ formed at 330 K, decomposed at 450 K |
| Si, Ge, Ga | Zone refining | Heater zone sweeps; k = C(s)/C(l) ≪ 1 purifies |

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Examiner Traps

CUET UG MCQs often hinge on a single subtle distinction. The five highest-frequency traps are:

1. **Calcination vs roasting** — calcination gives CO₂/H₂O (limited air, carbonates/hydrates); roasting gives SO₂ (excess air, sulphides).
2. **Gangue vs slag** — gangue is *in the ore*; slag is *formed in the furnace* after flux reacts with gangue.
3. **Highly reactive metals** — Na, K, Ca, Mg, Al cannot be reduced by C/CO; only fused-salt electrolysis works.
4. **Ellingham slope direction** — most oxide lines slope *upward*; C → CO and CO → CO₂ lines slope *downward*, which is why carbon reduction succeeds only above the crossover temperature.
5. **Zone refining** works only when the distribution coefficient **k = C(solid)/C(liquid) ≪ 1**; for typical metals k ≈ 1, so the method is restricted to low-melting semiconductors such as Si, Ge, Ga, and B.

#### Mechanism Spotlight — Mond Process for Nickel

Impure Ni is treated with CO at **330 K and 1 atm**, forming volatile **Ni(CO)₄** (a tetracarbonyl). Impurities (Cu, Fe, Co) do not form stable carbonyls under these conditions and remain behind. The gas is then heated to **450 K**, where Ni(CO)₄ decomposes back to pure Ni and CO. This is a classic example of **vapour-phase refining**.

#### Worked Example — Electrolytic Refining of Copper

In the refining of blister copper, the impure slab acts as the **anode** and a thin strip of pure copper as the **cathode** in acidified CuSO₄ solution. Calculate the mass deposited in **2 hours** at a current of **100 A**, given M(Cu) = 63.5 g mol⁻¹, n = 2.

Using Faraday's law: **m = (I × t × M) / (n × F)** = (100 × 7200 × 63.5) / (2 × 96500) ≈ **237 g** of pure copper.

#### Practice Prompts

1. The Ellingham diagram for MgO lies above that of Al₂O₃ across the entire temperature range. Predict whether Al can reduce MgO — and justify with a ΔG° argument.
2. Match the metals Hg, Ti, Sn, Ga to their refining methods and identify which one relies on carbonyl chemistry.
