# Analysing — Leaving Certificate (Established) English Notes

Analysing is the close-reading strand of the NCCA Leaving Certificate English specification: the skill of taking a single poem, prose extract, drama scene or film sequence and unpacking how the writer (or director) constructs meaning through diction, imagery, syntax, structure and rhetorical device. It is the technical backbone of the leaving certificate English paper — every comprehension question on Paper 1, every single-text question on Paper 2, and every comparative paragraph requires some version of the analysing skill. The strand carries a weight rating of 4 out of 5 in the topic catalogue, reflecting that it is the discrete skill the SEC credits most directly.

At Higher level, analysing extends into the *named* literary devices (rhetorical figures, metrical patterns, dramatic conventions) and demands that the candidate move from "what is the writer doing" to "why is the writer doing it here, in this context". At Ordinary level the same close-reading is required but the mark scheme is more forgiving on vocabulary — a candidate who can name "imagery" and explain its effect earns marks that an Ordinary-level candidate does not need to specify as "synesthetic imagery" or "objective correlative". The progression from Ordinary to Higher is largely a progression in the *precision* of the vocabulary and the *depth* of the inference.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Strand weight & spread**: Weight 4/5 in the topic catalogue. Close-reading marks sit on every question on both papers — under-preparing it depresses the entire script.
- **Three steps to every analytical paragraph**: *Anchor* (name the word or phrase), *Technique* (name the device), *Effect* (state what it does for the reader). Skip any of the three and the paragraph reads as descriptive, not analytical.
- **PETER framework for unseen texts**: *Process, Example, Technique, Effect, Response*. On an unseen poem or extract, write down the technique, anchor it to a word, and state the effect in a single sentence. Speed comes from practising the pattern.
- **Diction is not "vocabulary"**: Diction is *the writer's choice of this word rather than a synonym*. The analysing mark is for noticing that "shattered" is not the same word as "broken". Note the difference and state the effect.
- **Syntax is sentence shape**: A short sentence among longer ones earns its emphasis. A question where a statement would have done earns its inquiry. A fragment earns its emphasis. Mark scheme rewards noticing the shape.
- **Imagery is sensory**: Visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory, gustatory, kinetic. The strongest imagery is *specific* (a single named colour, a single named sound) rather than generic (bright, loud).
- **Structure is the architecture**: Stanza break, volta, line length, paragraph rhythm, the shift from past to present tense, the shift from third person to first. Name the structural choice and state what it does.
- **Rhetorical devices are named, not gestured at**: Simile, metaphor, personification, hyperbole, anaphora, alliteration, assonance, enjambment, caesura. A candidate who can name the device correctly earns the vocabulary mark; a candidate who can describe the device without naming it forfeits it.

#### Examiner traps

- Writing a "what happens next" paragraph instead of an analytical paragraph. The analysing mark is for *how the writer does it*, not what the writer is saying.
- Using the word "effective" without specifying what the effect is. "This is effective" earns no marks; "this slows the pace, drawing attention to the speaker's hesitation" earns the effect mark.
- Confusing personal response ("I felt sad here") with critical analysis ("the writer uses a monosyllabic verb to flatten the emotional register"). The mark scheme rewards the second.
- Treating every device as equally weighted. The mark scheme reads for *the most relevant* technique in the passage — picking the wrong device (e.g. naming "imagery" where the operative move is "syntax") forfeits the precision mark.
- Ignoring the line break or stanza break in poetry. Structure is a mark in poetry as much as it is in prose, and the line break is one of the most consistently under-marked structural choices.

#### 30-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Can you name the technical term for any device you notice in under five seconds?
- [ ] Have you written at least one analytical paragraph per genre (poetry, prose, drama, film) in the past month?
- [ ] Do you always anchor a quotation to a *single word or short phrase* before stating the effect?
- [ ] Can you distinguish diction, syntax, imagery and structure without confusing them?

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### Building an analytical vocabulary

The vocabulary of close reading is finite, and the candidate who learns it once does not need to re-learn it. A working vocabulary of forty to fifty named devices covers 95% of what the SEC examination requires:

- **Sound devices**: alliteration, assonance, consonance, sibilance, onomatopoeia, cacophony, euphony.
- **Figure devices**: simile, metaphor, personification, hyperbole, litotes, synecdoche, metonymy, pathetic fallacy.
- **Rhetorical devices**: anaphora, epistrophe, antithesis, climax, rhetorical question, direct address.
- **Syntax devices**: short sentence, fragment, periodic sentence, loose sentence, inversion, subordination, coordination.
- **Structural devices**: stanza break, volta, enjambment, caesura, parenthesis, flashback, frame narrative, shift in tense or person.
- **Dramatic devices**: soliloquy, aside, dramatic irony, stage direction, exposition, climax, dénouement.
- **Film devices**: shot type (close-up, wide, tracking), cut, transition, lighting, sound, framing, composition.

The vocabulary is the *floor*. The H1-grade candidate's vocabulary is precise — naming "sibilance" rather than "alliteration" when the operative sound is the *s*, naming "periodic sentence" rather than "long sentence" when the operative structure is the deferred meaning. Build the vocabulary through practice, not through memorisation: read a passage, find the operative device, name it, then check the answer in a glossary.

#### The "anchor → technique → effect" scaffold

Every analytical paragraph should follow a three-step scaffold:

1. **Anchor**: Quote a single word or short phrase from the passage. Underline it on the page or put it in quotation marks in the response.
2. **Technique**: Name the device operating on that word or phrase. Be precise: "sibilance", not "alliteration".
3. **Effect**: State what the device does for the reader. Be specific: "this creates a hushed, whispering quality that suggests the speaker is concealing something", not "this is effective".

A paragraph that follows the scaffold earns three marks (anchor, technique, effect). A paragraph that skips the anchor earns one mark (technique and effect are inferred). A paragraph that names the technique but does not state the effect earns one mark.

#### Reading the architecture of a text

Structure is the analytical layer most candidates under-prepare. The SEC mark scheme consistently rewards candidates who notice structural choices — the volta in a sonnet, the frame narrative in a novel, the soliloquy in a Shakespeare scene, the cut in a film sequence. The trap is treating structure as a synonym for "what happens next"; structural analysis is about *why* the writer chose this architecture *here*.

A practical drill: take any poem you have studied and re-read it three times — once for the line-by-line imagery, once for the stanza breaks and volta, once for the closing move. The closing move of a poem is one of the most consistently under-marked opportunities on Paper 2; candidates who notice and analyse the closing image earn marks that most candidates forfeit.

#### Working across poetry, prose, drama and film

The analytical habit applies across all four modes but the operative vocabulary differs:

- **Poetry**: diction, imagery, sound, syntax, stanza structure, volta, line break, enjambment, caesura. The line is the unit; the line break is the structural choice.
- **Prose**: diction, imagery, syntax, paragraph rhythm, sentence length, narrative voice, focalisation, motif, symbolism. The paragraph is the unit; the paragraph break is the structural choice.
- **Drama**: dialogue, stage direction, soliloquy, aside, dramatic irony, character voice, subtext. The beat (the smallest unit of dramatic action) is the unit; the scene break is the structural choice.
- **Film**: shot, cut, transition, sound, framing, lighting, performance, mise-en-scène. The shot is the unit; the cut is the structural choice.

A four-week pass before mock examinations should rotate through the four modes — one week per mode, two analytical paragraphs per week, with each paragraph following the anchor → technique → effect scaffold.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Higher-level analytical moves

At Higher level the SEC mark scheme rewards moves that Ordinary-level candidates are not expected to make:

- **Linking device to theme**: An H1-grade analytical paragraph does not stop at "this is a metaphor"; it adds "and the metaphor establishes the central theme of memory-as-construction".
- **Linking device to context**: An H1-grade paragraph connects the device to the writer's context — "the sibilance here echoes the hiss of the early-modern stage, drawing the audience into the whispered conspiracy". This is the contextual-analytical move.
- **Linking device to form**: An H1-grade paragraph notices the *form* — "the volta in this sonnet arrives at line 9, which coincides with the shift from octave to sestet — the device is doing the work of the form". This is the formal-analytical move.
- **Linking device to the comparative**: An H1-grade paragraph on Paper 2 connects the device to the same device in another comparative text. "The same sibilance appears in [writer B], but where [writer A] uses it to suggest concealment, [writer B] uses it to suggest intimacy".

These four moves are what separate H1 from H2. A Higher-grade candidate who practises all four for a month before the mock examination will reliably pull the grade on close-reading questions.

#### Analysing film and multimedia texts

The new specification's emphasis on "spoken, written, visual, multimedia" makes film analysis a more regular feature of the SEC paper than under the older specification. A working vocabulary of film analysis:

- **Shot types**: extreme long shot (establishing), long shot, medium shot, close-up, extreme close-up, over-the-shoulder, point-of-view. Each shot type does different work; a close-up earns its intimacy; a wide shot earns its context.
- **Camera movement**: pan, tilt, dolly, tracking, crane, handheld. The choice of movement is a mark; a handheld camera earns its instability.
- **Sound**: diegetic (within the world of the film), non-diegetic (the score), silence. The choice of sound is a mark; silence earns its weight.
- **Editing**: cut, dissolve, fade, jump cut, match cut. The choice of edit is a mark; a jump cut earns its disruption.
- **Lighting and colour**: high-key, low-key, natural, chiaroscuro. The colour palette is a mark; a desaturated palette earns its bleakness.

The mark scheme does not require the candidate to be a film studies graduate. It requires *observation* and *inference*: notice what the director has chosen, name it as precisely as you can, state the effect. The same anchor → technique → effect scaffold that applies to a poem applies to a film still.

#### Common mistakes that depress the grade

- **The "device list" failure mode** — naming five devices in a paragraph without analysing any of them. The mark scheme rewards depth on one device, not breadth across five.
- **The "effect is 'it makes the reader feel sad'" failure mode** — generic emotion words are not effects. State *how* the device produces the emotion; "the monosyllabic verb flattens the emotional register, forcing the reader to construct their own emotional response" is an effect.
- **The "personal response" failure mode** — slipping from "the writer does X" into "I felt Y". Personal response is not analysing; the two belong in different paragraphs.
- **The "no anchor" failure mode** — naming a device without quoting a single word or short phrase to anchor it. Without the anchor, the technique mark is forfeit.
- **The "no context" failure mode** — analysing a passage without ever connecting the device to the writer's context. At Higher level the context mark is what separates H1 from H2.
