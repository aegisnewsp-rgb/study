# Introduction to Economics

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> Scarcity is the single word that turns household choices, firm decisions and government budgets into economics.

Economics is the study of how scarce resources get allocated among unlimited wants. CA Foundation tests this chapter for terminology, micro vs macro split, and a few core formulas, not calculations. The Business and Commercial Knowledge paper (Paper 4) gives Economics around 3% weight, so 1–3 MCQs typically appear from definitions, central problems, and revenue relationships.

#### Core idea: scarcity → choice → cost

- **Scarcity** — resources (land, labour, capital, enterprise) are limited.
- **Choice** — because resources are limited, society must pick what to produce.
- **Opportunity Cost** — the value of the next best alternative foregone when a choice is made.

#### Must-know formula set

| Relationship | Formula | Variables & Units |
|---|---|---|
| Demand function | Qd = a − bP | Qd in units, a in units, b in units per ₹, P in ₹ |
| Price Elasticity of Demand | Ep = (%ΔQd) / (%ΔP) | Dimensionless ratio |
| Total Revenue | TR = P × Q | P in ₹ per unit, Q in units, TR in ₹ |
| Average / Marginal Revenue | AR = TR/Q ; MR = ΔTR/ΔQ | ₹ per unit |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

> The whole chapter reduces to one sentence: people optimise under constraint. Every concept below is a different camera angle on that sentence.

#### Central problems every economy must answer

Every economy, regardless of its political system, faces four questions that are the reason economics exists at all.

- **What to produce** — which goods and services, and in what quantities.
- **How to produce** — which technique (labour-intensive vs capital-intensive).
- **For whom to produce** — how output is distributed across income groups.
- **How to utilise resources efficiently** — full employment of factors, with minimum wastage.

These four problems are not academic. They are exactly the questions a CA Foundation MCQ will disguise inside a real-world scenario (a firm choosing between two technologies, a government allocating a budget).

#### Microeconomics vs Macroeconomics

| Dimension | Microeconomics | Macroeconomics |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of study | Individual consumer, firm, market | Whole economy |
| Key variables | Price, output of one good, elasticity | GDP, inflation, unemployment, money supply |
| Policy lever | Price mechanism, firm strategy | Fiscal policy, monetary policy |
| Typical question | "How does a ₹10 rise in petrol affect demand for two-wheelers?" | "How does RBI raising repo rate affect inflation?" |
| Tools | Demand–supply, PPC, cost curves | National Income accounting, multiplier |

The most common trap is the reverse — treating GDP as a micro variable or treating one firm's revenue as an aggregate.

#### Demand, supply and market equilibrium

The Law of Demand states an inverse relation between price (P) and quantity demanded (Qd), holding everything else constant (ceteris paribus). The Law of Supply states a direct relation between price and quantity supplied (Qs). Equilibrium is the price at which Qd = Qs.

Movement along the curve happens only when P changes. A shift of the entire curve happens when non-price determinants change — income, tastes, prices of related goods, expectations, number of buyers.

- **Normal good** — demand rises as income rises.
- **Inferior good** — demand falls as income rises.

> 💡 **High-Yield Revision Hook: Movement ≠ Shift.** "Along" is a price story; "shift" is an income/taste story. ICAI's MCQs love confusing these.

#### Worked exam-level problem (continuous prose)

Suppose the demand function for a product is Qd = 500 − 4P, where Qd is in units and P is in ₹ per unit, and the supply function is Qs = 100 + 2P. Find the equilibrium price and quantity, then compute Total Revenue at equilibrium and the price elasticity of demand at that point using the point-elasticity method.

Setting Qd = Qs gives 500 − 4P = 100 + 2P, so 400 = 6P and P = ₹66.67 per unit. Substituting back, Q = 100 + 2(66.67) = 233.33 units. Total Revenue at equilibrium is TR = P × Q = 66.67 × 233.33 ≈ ₹15,556. Point elasticity of demand uses Ep = (dQ/dP) × (P/Q). From the demand function, dQ/dP = −4. Therefore Ep = −4 × (66.67 / 233.33) ≈ −1.143. The absolute value |Ep| > 1, so demand at the equilibrium point is **elastic**.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students often forget that elasticity is usually reported as an absolute value when classifying demand as elastic, unit-elastic or inelastic. A negative sign indicates the law of demand (inverse relation), not that elasticity itself is "negative" in the business sense. ICAI MCQs test this sign convention directly.

#### Revenue concepts and their relationships

| Concept | Formula | Plain meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Total Revenue | TR = P × Q | Money actually received from sales |
| Average Revenue | AR = TR/Q = P | Revenue per unit sold |
| Marginal Revenue | MR = ΔTR/ΔQ | Revenue from selling one more unit |

Under perfect competition, P = AR = MR. Under monopoly or monopolistic competition, AR > MR and MR falls as Q rises.

#### Production Possibility Curve (PPC)

The PPC shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce when all resources are fully and efficiently employed, given a fixed resource base and technology. Its equation is X + Y = constant (a straight-line frontier in the simplest case), while the curved version shows increasing opportunity cost.

- Points **on** the curve — efficient production.
- Points **inside** the curve — underutilisation of resources.
- Points **outside** the curve — unattainable today; becomes attainable only when the curve shifts outward (economic growth from more resources or better technology).

#### Forms of market at a glance

- **Perfect Competition** — many buyers, many sellers, homogeneous product, free entry/exit.
- **Monopoly** — single seller, no close substitutes, strong barriers to entry.
- **Oligopoly** — few large firms, interdependent pricing decisions.
- **Monopolistic Competition** — many sellers, differentiated products, free entry/exit.

#### Macro aggregates: National Income

National income accounting uses several measures. GDP (Gross Domestic Product) is the value of all final goods produced within a country in a year. GNP adds net factor income from abroad. NNP (Net National Product) is obtained after subtracting depreciation. Switching between **market price (NNPmp)** and **factor cost (NNPfc)** requires adjusting for net indirect taxes: NNPfc = NNPmp − Net Indirect Taxes.

#### Fiscal vs Monetary policy

| Policy | Tool | Who controls | Target variable |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fiscal Policy | Government taxation and spending | Central + State Governments | Aggregate demand, income distribution |
| Monetary Policy | Repo rate, CRR, money supply | RBI | Inflation, credit availability, interest rates |

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

> Almost every CA Foundation mistake on this chapter is a boundary-condition mistake, not a definition mistake. The advanced traps below are where marks actually slip.

#### Edge cases and boundary conditions

- **Ceteris paribus is non-negotiable.** The Law of Demand holds only when income, tastes, prices of related goods, expectations and number of buyers are held constant. Once any one of them moves, you are no longer "along" the curve; the curve has shifted. ICAI frequently tests this by giving a scenario where income rises and asking whether the demand curve moves or the price moves — only price changes cause movement.
- **Elasticity has three readings.** Price elasticity (Ep), income elasticity (Ei) and cross elasticity (Ec). For normal goods Ei > 0; for inferior goods Ei < 0. Substitutes have Ec > 0; complements have Ec < 0. Many students treat "elasticity" as a single concept — examiners exploit this.
- **PPC shift vs movement.** A movement along the PPC reflects reallocation of existing resources. An outward shift reflects growth (more resources, better technology, improved productivity). A point outside the curve is not "inefficient" — it is currently **unattainable**, which is why economic growth matters.
- **Opportunity cost is not the monetary payment.** The fee paid for a course is the accounting cost. The opportunity cost is the salary forgone by not working during that period. CA Foundation treats opportunity cost as the next-best alternative's value, full stop.
- **NNPmp vs NNPfc trap.** Market price includes net indirect taxes; factor cost excludes them. They differ by exactly net indirect taxes, and confusing them produces wrong national-income figures by thousands of crores.
- **Business Economics is applied, not pure.** Business Economics (also called Managerial Economics) borrows micro tools — demand analysis, production functions, cost curves, break-even analysis — to solve firm-level decisions. It is not "economics in business"; it is the application layer between theory and managerial practice.

#### Concept comparison matrix (commonly confused pairs)

| Pair | Difference that matters |
|---|---|
| Movement vs Shift of demand curve | Caused by own-price change vs by non-price determinant |
| Normal vs Inferior good | Income effect is positive vs negative |
| AR vs MR | Revenue per unit vs revenue from one extra unit |
| Perfect Competition vs Monopolistic Competition | Homogeneous vs differentiated product |
| Fiscal vs Monetary policy | Government spending/tax vs RBI rate/money supply |
| NNPmp vs NNPfc | Differs by net indirect taxes |
| Microeconomics vs Macroeconomics | Individual unit vs whole economy |
| Accounting cost vs Opportunity cost | Actual payment vs next-best alternative forgone |
| Elastic vs Inelastic demand | \|Ep\| > 1 vs \|Ep\| < 1 |
| GDP vs GNP | Domestic production vs domestic + net factor income from abroad |

#### Advanced practice prompts

1. A drought destroys 30% of a country's agricultural output. Using the PPC framework, explain the short-run consequence on the production of industrial goods and the long-run mechanism through which the PPC could shift outward again. Identify which fiscal-policy lever is most likely to accelerate the recovery and which monetary-policy stance would conflict with it.
2. Demand for a good is Qd = 800 − 5P and supply is Qs = 200 + 3P, with P in ₹ and Q in units. Compute equilibrium P, Q, TR and point elasticity of demand at equilibrium. Classify demand as elastic, unit-elastic or inelastic. Now suppose consumer income rises by 20% and the good is inferior — describe verbally how the demand curve shifts and what happens to equilibrium price and quantity without solving new equations. State the sign convention you used and why ICAI-style answers expect |E|, not signed E, when classifying.
