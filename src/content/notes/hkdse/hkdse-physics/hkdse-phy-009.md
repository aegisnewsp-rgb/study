# IX. Medical Physics (Elective) — HKDSE Physics Notes

This topic covers Elective Topic IX of the HKDSE Physics Elective Part. Candidates sit any two of the four electives on Paper 2. The topic addresses making sense of the eye and the ear (vision correction, hearing aids); medical imaging using non-ionizing radiation (ultrasound, MRI); and medical imaging using ionizing radiation (X-rays, CT scans, PET scans, radiation safety and dose).

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **The human eye**: cornea, aqueous humour, iris, lens, vitreous humour, retina (with rods for low-light vision and cones for colour vision). The eye focuses light on the retina by changing the lens shape (accommodation).
- **Vision defects**:
  - **Myopia** (near-sighted): image forms in front of the retina. Corrected with a diverging (concave) lens.
  - **Hyperopia** (far-sighted): image forms behind the retina. Corrected with a converging (convex) lens.
  - **Astigmatism**: uneven cornea or lens. Corrected with cylindrical lenses.
  - **Presbyopia**: age-related loss of accommodation. Corrected with bifocal or varifocal lenses.
- **The human ear**: outer ear (pinna, ear canal), middle ear (eardrum, ossicles — malleus, incus, stapes), inner ear (cochlea, semicircular canals). The cochlea converts sound waves into nerve impulses via hair cells.
- **Hearing aids**: amplify sound; placed in or behind the ear.
- **Ultrasound**: sound waves with frequency > 20 kHz. Used in medical imaging (sonography). Frequency ~1-15 MHz. The probe emits pulses and detects echoes from tissue interfaces.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)**: uses strong magnetic fields and radio-frequency pulses to to image hydrogen nuclei (protons) in tissue. Excellent soft-tissue contrast; no ionising radiation.
- **X-rays**: produced by accelerating electrons onto a metal target (typically tungsten). The bremsstrahlung and characteristic X-rays are used for imaging. Dense tissue (bone) absorbs more X-rays and appears white on the image.
- **CT (Computed Tomography)**: X-ray imaging from multiple angles, reconstructed by computer into 3D images. Higher dose than a single X-ray image but more diagnostic information.
- **PET (Positron Emission Tomography)**: a radioactive tracer (typically FDG, ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose) emits positrons; the positron-electron annihilation produces two 511 keV gamma photons detected in coincidence. PET shows metabolic activity.
- **Radiation dose**: absorbed dose (gray, Gy = J/kg), equivalent dose (sievert, Sv = Gy × radiation weighting factor), effective dose (Sv × tissue weighting factor).
- **ALARA**: As Low As Reasonably Achievable — the principle for radiation protection.

#### Examiner traps

- Treating X-ray images as showing " "soft tissue" — X-rays show dense tissue (bone) as white, air as black.
- Confusing the absorbed dose (Gy) with the equivalent/effective dose (Sv).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### The eye and vision correction

**Refraction by the eye**: the cornea provides most of the refractive power (~43 dioptres); the lens provides additional ~19 dioptres at rest (more when accommodating). The lens changes shape to focus on objects at different distances.

**Myopia**: the eyeball is too long or the cornea too curved. Light from distant points points focuses in front of the retina. Correction: diverging lens (negative power) moves to the image back onto the retina.

**Hyperopia**: the eyeball is too short or the cornea too flat. Light focuses behind the retina. Correction: converging lens (positive power).

**Astigmatism**: the cornea is more curved in one direction than another. Correction: cylindrical lens with appropriate axis.

**Presbyopia**: loss of lens elasticity with age. The lens cannot accommodate to focus on near objects. Correction: reading glasses (positive power) or bifocals.

#### Ultrasound

**Generation and detection**: a piezoelectric crystal in the probe converts electrical pulses to ultrasonic pulses and back. The probe is coupled to the skin with gel to eliminate air gaps.

**Imaging**: the time between emission and detection of an echo gives the depth of the reflecting interface. The intensity of the echo gives the acoustic impedance mismatch.

**Doppler ultrasound**: detects the Doppler shift of echoes from moving blood to measure blood flow velocity.

**Applications**: obstetrics (fetal imaging), cardiology (heart structure and function), abdominal imaging (liver, kidney, pancreas), vascular imaging.

#### MRI

**Principle**: hydrogen nuclei (protons) in tissue have magnetic moments. In a strong magnetic field (~1-3 T), the moments align with the field. A radio-frequency pulse tips the moments; as they relax back, they emit radio signals. The relaxation times T1 and T2 depend on the tissue type, giving image contrast.

**Advantages**: excellent soft-tissue contrast; no ionising radiation; can image in any plane.

**Disadvantages**: expensive; long scan times; contraindicated for patients with certain implants (pacemakers, some metal implants); loud noise during scanning.

#### X-rays

**Production**: an X-ray tube has a cathode (filament) and an anode (target, typically tungsten). The cathode emits electrons when heated; the electrons are accelerated by a high voltage (~30-150 kV) and strike the target, producing X-rays.

**Types of X-rays**:

- **Bremsstrahlung** (braking radiation): produced by the deceleration of electrons in the target. Continuous spectrum.
- **Characteristic X-rays**: produced when inner-shell electrons are ejected and outer-shell electrons fill the vacancy. Discrete lines.

**Imaging**: X-rays pass through tissue and are absorbed to varying degrees. Bone (high Z, high density) absorbs more; air absorbs least. The image is the inverse of the absorption pattern: bone appears white.

**Contrast agents**: iodine or barium compounds enhance the contrast of blood vessels or the digestive tract.

**CT**: a rotating X-ray source and detector produce a series of projections; computer reconstruction produces 3D images. Higher dose than a single X-ray but more information.

#### PET

**Principle**: a positron-emitting radionuclide (e.g. ¹⁸F, ¹¹C, ¹⁵O, ¹³N) is attached to a biological molecule (e.g. FDG, a glucose analogue). The tracer is injected into the the patient. The emitted positron annihilates with a nearby electron, producing two 511 keV gamma photons at ~180° apart. The coincidence detection identifies the annihilation point.

**Applications**: oncology ( (FDG uptake is high in metabolically active tumours), neurology (brain metabolism), cardiology.

#### Radiation protection

**Principles**: time (minimise exposure duration), distance (maximise distance from source), shielding (use appropriate barriers).

**Personal dosimetry**: thermoluminescent dosimeters (TLDs) or electronic dosimeters measure cumulative dose.

**Dose limits (ICRP)**: occupational dose limit 20 mSv/year averaged over 5 years; public dose limit 1 mSv/year.

**Typical doses**:

- Chest X-ray: ~ 0.02 mSv.
- CT head: ~ 2 mSv.
- CT chest: ~ 7 mSv.
- PET-CT: ~ 25 mSv.
- Natural background (annual): ~ 2-3 mSv (varies by location).

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Cross-topic links

- **III. Wave Motion**: ultrasound, the Doppler effect.
- **IV. Electricity and Magnetism**: MRI uses magnetic fields.
- **V. Radioactivity and Nuclear Energy**: radiation sources, half-life.

#### Exam technique

- **For vision correction questions**: identify the defect, state the lens type and sign.
- **For ultrasound questions**: calculate depth = (v × t) / 2.
- **For radiation dose questions**: convert between Gy and Sv using the radiation weighting factor.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "X-rays are radioactive." X-rays are a form of electromagnetic radiation; the X-ray tube produces them when needed but is not radioactive when off.
- "MRI uses X-rays." MRI uses magnetic fields and radio-frequency pulses; no ionising radiation.
- "PET scans use X-rays." PET scans use gamma rays from positron-electron annihilation.
