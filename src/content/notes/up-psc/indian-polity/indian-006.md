# Indian Judiciary: Supreme Court, High Courts & Subordinate Courts

The Indian Constitution establishes an integrated, single judicial system despite the federal character of the polity. Unlike the United States, where federal and state laws are adjudicated by separate hierarchies of courts, the Indian judiciary enforces both Central and State enactments through a unified hierarchy headed by the Supreme Court of India. In UPPSC PCS (General Studies Paper II for Prelims and GS Paper II for Mains), judicial architecture, constitutional jurisdictions, appointment controversies, judicial review, and landmark constitutional bench verdicts represent one of the most frequently tested subject areas.

## Complete 3-Tier Study Guide

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Revision (1h–1d sprint)

For quick revision before Prelims, lock down these constitutional anchors and numeric thresholds:

- **Constitutional Framework**:
  - **Supreme Court**: Part V, Chapter IV (Articles 124 to 147). Established on 28 January 1950, replacing the Federal Court of India (established under the Government of India Act 1935) and the Privy Council.
  - **High Courts**: Part VI, Chapter V (Articles 214 to 231). Currently 25 High Courts across 28 States and 8 Union Territories.
  - **Subordinate Courts**: Part VI, Chapter VI (Articles 233 to 237).
- **Bench Strength & Tenure**:
  - Supreme Court strength is determined by Parliament via legislation (Article 124(1)). Currently 34 judges (1 Chief Justice of India + 33 puisne judges) following the *Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2019*. Retirement age is **65 years**.
  - High Court strength is determined at the discretion of the President of India based on workload. Retirement age is **62 years** (raised from 60 by the 15th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963).
- **Core Jurisdictions of the Supreme Court**:
  - **Original Jurisdiction (Article 131)**: Exclusive dispute resolution between Centre vs. one or more States, or State vs. State on questions of legal or constitutional right. Does *not* cover inter-state river water disputes (Article 262) or pre-constitutional treaties.
  - **Writ Jurisdiction (Article 32)**: Direct constitutional remedy for enforcement of Part III Fundamental Rights. The Supreme Court cannot refuse to hear a petition under Article 32 because the right to move the court is itself a Fundamental Right (*heart and soul of the Constitution* per Dr. B.R. Ambedkar).
  - **Appellate Jurisdiction (Articles 132, 133, 134, 136)**: Constitutional, civil, criminal appeals, and Special Leave Petitions (SLP under Art 136).
  - **Advisory Jurisdiction (Article 143)**: Presidential reference to the Supreme Court on questions of law or public fact (clause 1: opinion discretionary; clause 2: pre-constitutional disputes, opinion mandatory). The opinion is advisory and non-binding on the President.
  - **Court of Record (Article 129)**: Decisions possess evidentiary value; power to punish for contempt of itself (civil and criminal).
  - **Complete Justice (Article 142)**: Inherent power to pass any decree or order necessary for doing complete justice in any cause or matter.
- **Key UPPSC Prelims Pitfalls**:
  - High Courts are *not* subordinate to the Supreme Court administratively; they are constitutional courts of coordinate status within their territorial sphere.
  - Article 226 writ jurisdiction of High Courts is **wider** than Article 32 of the Supreme Court: High Courts issue writs for Fundamental Rights *and* for "any other legal purpose", whereas Article 32 is restricted strictly to Fundamental Rights.
  - A High Court judge submits resignation to the **President of India**, not the State Governor.
  - Removal of SC and HC judges requires an address by each House of Parliament supported by a special majority (majority of total membership + not less than two-thirds of members present and voting) under Article 124(4) on grounds of *proved misbehaviour or incapacity*.

### 🟡 Standard — Core Exam Concepts (2d–2mo preparation)

In this standard preparation tier, we examine the structural separation of powers, appointment mechanics, jurisdiction matrices, and the constitutional friction between judicial independence and executive accountability.

#### 1. Appointment Mechanisms & The Collegium Evolution

The text of Article 124(2) states that every judge of the Supreme Court shall be appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal after "consultation" with such judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts as the President may deem necessary. The evolution of the term "consultation" represents one of the most intense constitutional conflicts in modern Indian governance:

1. **First Judges Case (*S.P. Gupta v. Union of India*, 1981)**:
   - The Supreme Court held that "consultation" does not mean "concurrence".
   - The executive retained ultimate primacy in judicial appointments, reducing the Chief Justice's role to an advisory opinion.
2. **Second Judges Case (*Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association (SCAORA) v. Union of India*, 1993)**:
   - The 9-judge bench overruled *S.P. Gupta*, ruling that "consultation" actually implies "concurrence".
   - The Chief Justice of India must formulate the recommendation in consultation with the **two senior-most puisne judges** of the Supreme Court.
   - The executive cannot appoint any judge whose name was rejected by the Collegium.
3. **Third Judges Case (Presidential Reference under Article 143, 1998)**:
   - Clarified the Collegium architecture:
     - For Supreme Court appointments: CJI + **four senior-most puisne judges**.
     - For High Court appointments: CJI + **two senior-most puisne judges**, in consultation with the Chief Justice and senior colleagues of the relevant High Court.
   - If two or more judges of the Collegium express an adverse opinion, the CJI cannot send the recommendation to the government.
4. **Fourth Judges Case (*Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India*, 2015)**:
   - Parliament unanimously passed the 99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014, creating the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC), composed of the CJI, two senior SC judges, Union Law Minister, and two eminent persons.
   - A 5-judge Constitution Bench struck down both the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act by a 4:1 majority as unconstitutional, holding that judicial independence is a fundamental facet of the **Basic Structure Doctrine** and that executive presence on the appointment commission jeopardizes that independence.
   - The Collegium system was revived in full force, accompanied by a directive to draft an updated Memorandum of Procedure (MoP).

#### 2. Constitutional Writs Matrix (Articles 32 and 226)

Both the Supreme Court and High Courts possess extraordinary powers to issue prerogative writs derived from English common law:

| Writ Name | Literal Meaning | Constitutional Purpose & Scope | Against Whom Issued | Crucial Exceptions |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Habeas Corpus** | "To have the body of" | Protects personal liberty against illegal, arbitrary detention; directs detaining authority to produce detainee before court | Public authorities and private individuals | Cannot be issued if detention is lawful, for contempt of legislature/court, or outside territorial jurisdiction |
| **Mandamus** | "We command" | Commands a public official, inferior court, tribunal, or corporation to perform a mandatory statutory public duty | Public officials, statutory bodies, subordinate courts | Cannot be issued against the President, State Governors (Art 361), private bodies, or to enforce non-statutory contracts |
| **Prohibition** | "To forbid" | Issued by a superior court to prevent an inferior court or quasi-judicial tribunal from exceeding or usurping jurisdiction | Judicial and quasi-judicial authorities | Cannot be issued against administrative bodies, legislative assemblies, or private individuals |
| **Certiorari** | "To be certified / informed" | Quashes an order already passed by an inferior court or quasi-judicial tribunal for lack of jurisdiction, excess of jurisdiction, or violation of natural justice | Judicial, quasi-judicial, and administrative authorities affecting rights | Cannot be issued against legislative bodies or private companies |
| **Quo-Warranto** | "By what warrant or authority" | Inquires into the legal validity of a person's claim to a public office created by statute or constitution; prevents substantive usurpation | Any person holding a substantive public office | Cannot be issued against ministerial offices, private posts, or temporary administrative designations |

#### 3. Original, Appellate, and Advisory Jurisdiction Compared

| Feature | Supreme Court (Article 124–147) | High Court (Article 214–231) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Constitutional Basis** | Part V, Chapter IV | Part VI, Chapter V |
| **Writ Power** | Article 32: Restricted strictly to Fundamental Rights | Article 226: Fundamental Rights plus any other legal right |
| **Nature of Writ Right** | Article 32 is itself a guaranteed Fundamental Right | Article 226 is a constitutional discretion, not an enumerated Fundamental Right |
| **Territorial Scope** | Nationwide across all States and Union Territories | Confined to territorial jurisdiction of the State/UT (or where cause of action arises) |
| **Exclusive Original Disputes** | Article 131: Federal disputes (Centre vs. State, State vs. State) | No federal disputes; original civil jurisdiction exists in select HCs (Bombay, Calcutta, Madras, Delhi) |
| **Superintendence Power** | No administrative superintendence over High Courts | Article 227: Administrative and judicial superintendence over all subordinate courts and tribunals in state |
| **Retirement Age** | 65 years | 62 years |
| **Removal Process** | Parliamentary special majority + Presidential order (Art 124(4)) | Identical grounds and parliamentary procedure as SC judge (Art 217(1)(b) r/w Art 124(4)) |

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Analytical Study (3mo+ mastery)

In this advanced tier, we address high-scoring themes required for UPPSC PCS Mains GS Paper II: Judicial Review, Basic Structure Doctrine, Judicial Activism vs. Judicial Overreach, Public Interest Litigation, and Subordinate Judiciary governance.

#### 1. The Doctrine of Judicial Review & Basic Structure

Judicial review is the constitutional power of superior courts to determine the constitutional validity of legislative enactments and executive actions. While the phrase "judicial review" does not appear explicitly in the text of the Constitution, the power is deeply embedded in:
- **Article 13(2)**: Any law made by the State taking away or abridging Fundamental Rights is void to the extent of the contravention.
- **Articles 32 and 226**: Prerogative remedy channels.
- **Article 131 to 136**: Appellate and original determinations.
- **Article 245 and 246**: Enforcing territorial and legislative competence limits between Union and State lists.

#### 2. Judicial Activism vs. Judicial Overreach

In UPPSC Mains answer writing, you must clearly distinguish between legitimate judicial activism and unconstitutional judicial overreach:

1. **Judicial Activism**:
   - The judiciary plays a dynamic, proactive role in protecting citizens' constitutional rights, particularly when the legislature or executive fails to act (*governance vacuum*).
   - Conceptualized in India by **Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer** and **Justice P.N. Bhagwati** through Public Interest Litigation (PIL) in the late 1970s.
   - Core examples:
     - *Hussainara Khatoon v. Home Secretary, State of Bihar (1979)*: Recognised the right to speedy trial as part of Article 21, securing release of over 40,000 undertrial prisoners.
     - *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997)*: Laid down legally enforceable workplace sexual harassment guidelines in the complete absence of parliamentary statute.
     - *M.C. Mehta Cases*: Enforced vehicular emission norms (CNG transition in Delhi), protected the Taj Mahal from industrial emissions, and applied the *Precautionary Principle* and *Polluter Pays Principle*.
2. **Judicial Overreach**:
   - Occurs when the judiciary crosses the boundary of separation of powers (Article 50), entering the policy-making or administrative domain reserved for elected institutions.
   - Criticisms include lack of electoral accountability, lack of administrative expertise in fiscal matters, and undermining executive legitimacy.
   - Examples cited by constitutional jurists:
     - Imposing a blanket ban on the sale of liquor within 500 metres of national and state highways (2016).
     - Cancellation of 122 telecom 2G spectrum licenses (2012) rather than adjudicating criminal culpability.
     - Regulating school bus safety specifications and vehicle firecracker sales.

#### 3. Public Interest Litigation (PIL) Principles

Traditional Anglo-Saxon jurisprudence required strict **locus standi** — only a person whose direct legal rights were infringed could approach the court. PIL overturned this doctrine:

- **Relaxation of Locus Standi**: Any public-spirited individual or organization can approach the superior courts on behalf of marginalized, indigent, or vulnerable citizens who cannot access justice themselves.
- **Epistolary Jurisdiction**: Courts can convert informal postcards, telegrams, newspaper reports, or letters written by prisoners into writ petitions (*Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration, 1980*).
- **Guidelines to Prevent Abuse**: In *State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010)*, the Supreme Court established 8 mandatory guidelines for High Courts to weed out vexatious, politically motivated, or blackmailing PILs and impose exemplary costs on frivolous litigants.

#### 4. Subordinate Judiciary & Administrative Tribunals

The subordinate judiciary forms the immediate point of citizen interface:
- **District Judges (Article 233)**: Appointed by the Governor in consultation with the High Court. A person must have at least 7 years of standing as an advocate or pleader.
- **Subordinate Services (Article 234)**: Appointments made by Governor in accordance with rules framed after consultation with the State Public Service Commission (UPPSC) and High Court.
- **Control over Subordinate Courts (Article 235)**: Complete administrative control, including postings, promotions, leave, and discipline, vests in the High Court, ensuring insulation from political executive pressure.
- **Tribunals (Part XIV-A, Articles 323A and 323B)**: Added by 42nd Amendment Act, 1976.
  - **Article 323A**: Administrative tribunals for public service disputes (Central Administrative Tribunal / CAT). Can only be established by Parliament.
  - **Article 323B**: Tribunals for other matters (taxation, land reforms, foreign exchange). Can be established by both Parliament and State Legislatures.
  - In *L. Chandra Kumar (1997)*, the Supreme Court struck down clauses excluding the jurisdiction of High Courts, mandating that decisions of CAT and state tribunals are appealable before a Division Bench of the respective High Court.

## Worked UPPSC Exam Questions

### Question 1 (UPPSC Prelims GS Paper I Pattern)

**Question:** Which of the following statements regarding the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court of India is/are correct?
1. The Supreme Court has exclusive original jurisdiction in disputes between the Government of India and one or more States.
2. The Supreme Court's advisory opinion tendered under Article 143(1) is legally binding on the President of India.
3. The Supreme Court cannot decline to entertain a petition filed under Article 32 alleging violation of a Fundamental Right.
4. An appeal by Special Leave under Article 136 can only be granted against final orders of High Courts.

Select the correct code:
- (A) 1 and 2 only
- (B) 1 and 3 only
- (C) 2, 3 and 4 only
- (D) 1, 3 and 4 only

**Detailed Solution:**
- **Statement 1 is correct**: Article 131 grants exclusive original jurisdiction to the Supreme Court in any dispute between the Government of India and one or more States, or between two or more States, provided the dispute involves any question on which the existence or extent of a legal right depends.
- **Statement 2 is incorrect**: An advisory opinion tendered by the Supreme Court under Article 143 is solely advisory; it is neither a judicial judgment nor an executive command, and it is **not binding** on the President of India (*Re Kerala Education Bill, 1958*).
- **Statement 3 is correct**: Unlike Article 226 where High Courts have wide discretion, Article 32 is itself an enumerated Fundamental Right under Part III (*Right to Constitutional Remedies*). Therefore, the Supreme Court is the protector and guarantor of Fundamental Rights and cannot routinely refuse to entertain a genuine petition claiming infringement.
- **Statement 4 is incorrect**: Article 136 is an extraordinary, discretionary power. It allows the Supreme Court to grant special leave to appeal against **any judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order** in any cause or matter passed or made by **any court or tribunal** in the territory of India (except military tribunals under Armed Forces acts). It is not limited to High Courts nor to final orders alone.
- *Correct Answer:* **(B) 1 and 3 only**.

### Question 2 (UPPSC Mains GS Paper II Analytical Model)

**Question:** "The power of Judicial Review in India is not an obstruction to democracy, but its primary safeguard." Critically examine this statement in light of the Basic Structure doctrine and judicial pronouncements. (15 Marks, 250 Words)

**Model Answer Framework:**

1. **Introduction**:
   - Define Judicial Review: The inherent power of constitutional courts (Articles 13, 32, 226) to scrutinize legislative enactments and executive decrees against the touchstone of constitutional supremacy.
   - State the core premise: In a constitutional democracy, legislative majorities are bound by constitutional limitations; judicial review ensures that majoritarianism does not degenerate into constitutional authoritarianism.
2. **Safeguard to Democracy**:
   - *Protection of Fundamental Freedoms*: Defends Article 14 (Equality), 19 (Civil Liberties), and 21 (Right to Life) from arbitrary executive incursions (*Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India, 1978*).
   - *Preservation of Federal Balance*: Neutral umpire resolving Union-State friction (Article 131) and curbing misuse of President's Rule under Article 356 (*S.R. Bommai v. Union of India, 1994*).
   - *Institutional Integrity & Clean Elections*: Declared the right to know candidates' criminal antecedents a basic right (*ADR Case, 2002*) and struck down opaque Electoral Bonds (*ADR v. Union of India, 2024*).
3. **Addressing the "Obstruction" Critique**:
   - Unelected judges overriding acts of elected legislators (*Counter-Majoritarian Difficulty*).
   - Instances of judicial overreach venturing into policy spheres (liquor bans, executive committees).
   - Enormous judicial backlog (over 80,000 pending cases in SC, 60 lakh in HCs) delaying infrastructural and administrative decision-making.
4. **Conclusion**:
   - As emphasized in *Minerva Mills (1980)*, the Constitution created a delicate equilibrium between Parliament, the Executive, and the Judiciary. Judicial review preserves that equilibrium. By acting as the sentinel on the qui vive, the judiciary preserves the constitutional spirit without displacing the legitimate legislative mandate.

## Common Preparation Traps for UPPSC PCS

- **Trap 1: Confusing Article 32 with Article 226 Scope**: Do not write that the Supreme Court has wider writ powers than High Courts. High Courts have wider territorial subject-matter scope (Fundamental Rights + ordinary legal rights), whereas the Supreme Court has broader territorial reach (all-India) but narrower subject matter (Fundamental Rights only).
- **Trap 2: High Court Judge Transfer vs. Appointment**: While the President appoints and transfers High Court judges, transfers require consultation with the CJI, who must consult four senior-most judges of the Supreme Court plus the Chief Justices of both relevant High Courts.
- **Trap 3: Impeachment Terminology**: The Constitution uses the word "impeachment" strictly for the President of India (Article 61). Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts are removed by an **Address to the President** passed by Parliament (Article 124(4)), not by impeachment.
- **Trap 4: Contempt Powers Scope**: Both Supreme Court (Article 129) and High Courts (Article 215) are Courts of Record with inherent powers to punish for contempt. The Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 regulates this power but cannot abrogate or curtail the constitutional jurisdiction.
