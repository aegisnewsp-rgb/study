# V. Radioactivity and Nuclear Energy — HKDSE Physics Notes

This topic covers Topic V of the Compulsory Part of HKDSE Physics. It addresses radiation and radioactivity (alpha, beta and gamma radiation, radioactive decay, half-life, activity); the atomic model (Rutherford's scattering experiment, the Bohr model, energy levels); and nuclear energy (nuclear fission and fusion, mass-energy equivalence E = mc², nuclear reactors).

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Alpha radiation (α)**: helium nucleus (₂⁴He), 2 protons + 2 neutrons. Charge +2e, mass 4u. Strongly ionising; weakly penetrating; stopped by paper.
- **Beta-minus radiation (β⁻)**: high-energy electron emitted when a neutron converts to a proton. Charge -1e, mass ≈ 0. Moderately ionising; moderately penetrating; stopped by a few mm of aluminium.
- **Beta-plus radiation (β⁺)**: positron (anti-electron). Charge +1e, mass ≈ 0.
- **Gamma radiation (γ)**: high-energy electromagnetic radiation. No charge, no mass. Weakly ionising; very penetrating; stopped by several cm of lead or metres of concrete.
- **Radioactive decay**: a spontaneous process; the nucleus of an unstable atom emits radiation and becomes a different nucleus (transmutation).
- **Half-life T₁/₂**: the time for half the radioactive nuclei to decay. The activity A = λN (where λ is the decay constant, N is the number of nuclei). T₁/₂ = ln 2/λ ≈ 0.693/λ.
- **Rutherford's scattering experiment (1911)**: alpha particles directed at a thin gold foil; most pass through (mostly empty space), some are deflected at large angles (small, dense, positively charged nucleus).
- **Bohr model**: electrons orbit the nucleus in fixed orbits; each orbit has a fixed energy; electrons can jump between orbits by absorbing or emitting photons of energy ΔE = hf.
- **Nuclear fission**: a heavy nucleus (e.g. U-235) splits into two smaller nuclei, releasing energy and several neutrons. Chain reaction if enough neutrons cause further fissions.
- **Nuclear fusion**: light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy (e.g. the Sun: 4 ¹H → ⁴He + 2e⁺ + 2ν + energy).
- **Mass-energy equivalence**: E = mc² (mass-energy equivalence; the binding energy is the energy equivalent of the mass deficit in a nucleus).
- **Nuclear reactor**: controlled chain reaction. Moderator (water, heavy water, graphite) slows neutrons; control rods (boron, cadmium) absorb neutrons; coolant removes heat; the heat boils water to drive turbines.

#### Examiner traps

- Treating half-life as the average lifetime — half-life is the time for half to decay, not the average lifetime (which is 1/λ).
- Confusing activity (decays per second) with the number of nuclei.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Radioactivity

**Alpha decay**: ²³⁸U → ²³⁴Th + α. The daughter nucleus has Z reduced by 2 and A reduced by 4.

**Beta-minus decay**: neutron → proton + electron + antineutrino. ¹⁴C → ¹⁴N + β⁻ + ν̄. The daughter nucleus has Z increased by 1, A unchanged.

**Beta-plus decay**: proton → neutron + positron + neutrino. ²²Na → ²²Ne + β⁺ + ν. The daughter nucleus has Z decreased by 1, A unchanged.

**Gamma decay**: an excited nucleus emits a gamma photon. The daughter nucleus has the same Z and A but is in a lower-energy state.

#### Half-life and activity

**Activity A**: A = -dN/dt = λN. Units: Bq (becquerel) = decays per second.

**Half-life T₁/₂**: time for N to halve. T₁/₂ = ln 2/λ ≈ 0.693/λ.

**Decay equation**: N(t) = N₀ e^(-λt) or N(t) = N₀ (1/2)^(t/T₁/₂).

**Example**: a sample has N₀ = 10⁶ nuclei, T₁/₂ = 1 hour. After 3 hours: N = 10⁶ · (1/2)³ = 1.25 × 10⁵.

#### Atomic model

**Rutherford's scattering experiment**: alpha particles fired at a thin gold foil; the observation that some particles were deflected at large angles (and some bounced back) led to the nuclear model of the atom: a small, dense, positively charged nucleus surrounded by mostly empty space.

**Bohr model**: hydrogen atom has discrete orbits; each orbit has energy E_n = -13.6 eV/n² (n = 1, 2, 3, ...); electrons can jump between orbits by absorbing or emitting photons of energy |ΔE| = hf (where f is the frequency of the photon and h is Planck's constant).

The Bohr model succeeded in explaining the hydrogen spectrum but fails for more complex atoms. The modern quantum mechanical model replaces Bohr's orbits with electron probability distributions (orbitals).

#### Nuclear energy

**Binding energy**: the energy required to separate a nucleus into its constituent nucleons. Equivalent to the mass deficit (the difference between the sum of individual nucleon masses and the actual nucleus mass).

**Mass-energy equivalence**: E = mc². The binding energy is the energy equivalent of the mass deficit.

**Fission**: e.g. ²³⁵U + n → ¹⁴¹Ba + ⁹²Kr + 3n + ~200 MeV. Each fission releases ~200 MeV of energy and several neutrons. If the neutrons cause further fissions, a chain reaction occurs.

**Fusion**: e.g. ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n + 17.6 MeV. Releases energy because the binding energy per nucleon is higher for the product.

**Energy per nucleon curve**: peaks around Fe (iron). Fission releases energy for heavy nuclei (above Fe); fusion releases energy for light nuclei (below Fe).

**Nuclear reactor**: controlled fission chain reaction.

- Fuel rods: U-235 (enriched uranium) or Pu-239.
- Moderator: slows neutrons to thermal speeds (water, heavy water, graphite).
- Control rods: absorb neutrons (boron, cadmium) to control the rate.
- Coolant: removes heat from the core (water, gas, liquid metal).
- Steam generator: uses heat to boil water, driving turbines.
- Containment: thick concrete and steel shielding.

#### Exam technique

- **For decay questions**: identify the type of decay; write the balanced nuclear equation; check Z and A.
- **For half-life questions**: apply N(t) = N₀ · (1/2)^(t/T₁/₂) or use the decay constant λ.
- **For nuclear energy questions**: apply E = mc²; check units (MeV, eV, J).

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "All radiation is dangerous." Background radiation is everywhere; the danger depends on the type, intensity and duration of exposure.
- "Half-life is the average lifetime." No — half-life is the time for half to decay; the average lifetime is 1/λ ≈ 1.44·T₁/₂.
- "Nuclear fission and fusion are the same." They are opposite: fission splits heavy nuclei; fusion combines light nuclei.
