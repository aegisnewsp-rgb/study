# Biochemistry and Metabolism

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

Metabolism questions are usually a chain question: a defect at one enzyme produces a pile-up upstream, a shortfall downstream, and a clinical picture that falls out of the block. If you can locate the block, most of the answer follows.

| Pathway | Rate-limiting step | What the block does to the patient |
|---|---|---|
| Glycolysis | Phosphofructokinase-1 (committed step) | Lactate builds up; the pathway slows before pyruvate |
| Pyruvate oxidation | Pyruvate dehydrogenase | Lactate rises, ketones rise in starvation, no acetyl-CoA enters the TCA cycle |
| TCA cycle | Isocitrate dehydrogenase (also citrate synthase, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase) | Citrate accumulates and exports to the cytosol |
| Fatty acid synthesis | Acetyl-CoA carboxylase (acetyl-CoA → malonyl-CoA) | Malonyl-CoA rises and slows the carnitine shuttle, so fat oxidation drops |
| Gluconeogenesis | Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase | Lactate and alanine accumulate; blood glucose falls |
| Urea cycle | Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I | Ammonia accumulates; the brain is the first casualty |
| Purine breakdown | Xanthine oxidase | Uric acid rises; gout and uric acid stones |
| Cholesterol synthesis | HMG-CoA reductase | Cholesterol falls; statins work here |

**Energy production, the shape of it.** Glycolysis gives 2 ATP and 2 NADH per glucose and needs no oxygen. Pyruvate dehydrogenase converts pyruvate to acetyl-CoA, producing 1 NADH each. Each acetyl-CoA through the TCA cycle yields 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂ and 1 GTP. The electron transport chain converts NADH to about 2.5 ATP and FADH₂ to about 1.5 ATP. Net from one glucose: roughly 30–32 ATP depending on which shuttle carries the cytosolic NADH.

**Oxidative phosphorylation, the four things that get asked.**
- The electron transport chain sits in the inner mitochondrial membrane; the ATP synthase too.
- The membrane is impermeable to protons, and that is what makes the gradient.
- Complex I is blocked by rotenone; complex IV by cyanide; complex V by oligomycin. All three stop ATP production.
- An uncoupler such as 2,4-dinitrophenol collapses the gradient: oxygen consumption rises, ATP falls, and the energy comes out as heat.

**Enzyme kinetics, the minimum you need.** Vmax is the rate at saturation. Km is the substrate concentration at which the rate is half of Vmax, so a low Km means high affinity. Competitive inhibition raises Km and leaves Vmax unchanged; non-competitive inhibition lowers Vmax and leaves Km unchanged; uncompetitive inhibition lowers both.

**Acid-base, the two lines that unlock most stems.**
- pH = 6.1 + log[HCO₃⁻ ÷ (0.03 × PaCO₂)]
- Anion gap = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻); normal is about 8–12 without potassium.

**Memorise for the Lite tier:**
- Glycolysis nets 2 ATP, 2 NADH and 2 pyruvate; the committed step is PFK-1.
- Pyruvate dehydrogenase needs five cofactors: TPP, lipoic acid, CoA, FAD and NAD⁺.
- TCA nets 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 1 GTP and 2 CO₂ per acetyl-CoA.
- NADH gives about 2.5 ATP; FADH₂ about 1.5.
- Cyanide blocks complex IV; rotenone blocks complex I; DNP uncouples.
- Competitive inhibition: Km up, Vmax unchanged.
- Respiratory acidosis has high PaCO₂; metabolic acidosis has low HCO₃⁻.
- Anion gap: high in ketoacidosis, lactic acidosis, uraemia and toxins; normal in diarrhoea, renal tubular acidosis and vomiting.
- Homocysteine is remethylated by methionine synthase, which needs methylcobalamin (B12) and 5-methyltetrahydrofolate (folate).

### 🟡 Standard — Full Coverage (1–2d)

#### Worked example: the DKA blood gas

A patient with type 1 diabetes has a blood gas of pH 7.12, PaCO₂ 24 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 9 mmol/L, Na⁺ 134, Cl⁻ 105.

Step one, the pH of 7.12 is acidemia. Step two, the HCO₃⁻ of 9 is low, so the primary process is metabolic acidosis. Step three, the PaCO₂ of 24 is low, so there is respiratory compensation, and it is appropriate: Winter's formula predicts a PaCO₂ of 1.5 × 9 + 8 = 21.5, with a usual tolerance of about ±2, and the measured 24 sits inside that window. A PaCO₂ below that range would mean a second, superimposed respiratory acidosis.

Step four, the anion gap is 134 − (105 + 9) = 20, which is elevated, so this is a high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis. In a patient with diabetes, ketoacidosis is the answer, and the missing piece is that insulin deficiency drives lipolysis, which floods the liver with fatty acids, which the liver converts to ketone bodies. Acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate are acids, and the sodium deficit from osmotic diuresis explains why the measured sodium looks low even though total body sodium is depleted.

Now change one number: if the PaCO₂ were 40 with the same bicarbonate of 9, the compensation is absent and this is a mixed metabolic and respiratory acidosis — the kind of picture seen in salicylate poisoning or in sepsis with a pneumonia.

#### Worked example: an inhibitor on a Michaelis-Menten plot

An enzyme has Vmax of 100 µmol/min and Km of 2 mmol/L. At a substrate concentration of 2 mmol/L the rate is 50 µmol/min, because substrate equals Km.

Now a competitive inhibitor is added. Vmax stays at 100 because adding more substrate outcompetes the inhibitor and the enzyme can still reach the same maximum, but Km rises, so the same substrate concentration now gives a lower rate. On a double-reciprocal plot the lines cross on the y-axis. For a non-competitive inhibitor the y-intercept rises instead, because Vmax has fallen and the lines cross on the x-axis. Uncompetitive inhibition is the rare third pattern: the inhibitor binds only to the enzyme-substrate complex, so both Km and Vmax fall and the parallel lines meet on the x-axis.

#### Amino acid metabolism: how to approach the block

Amino acids are handled in three stages. First, nitrogen is collected by transamination: aminotransferases move the amino group onto α-ketoglutarate to make glutamate, and the coenzyme is pyridoxal phosphate from vitamin B6. ALT works on alanine and AST on aspartate, which is why aminotransferases rise in liver injury and why AST rises in myocardial injury, where the enzyme is washed out of the tissue.

Second, the amino group is disposed of. Liver urea cycle: carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I is the rate-limiting step and uses N-acetylglutamate as an activator; ornithine transcarbamylase, argininosuccinate synthetase, argininosuccinate lyase and arginase follow. Third, the carbon skeletons enter the TCA cycle as glucogenic or ketogenic amino acids, and the carbon skeleton determines the glucose or ketone outcome.

The inherited defects are worth knowing as blocks rather than as lists. Phenylketonuria from phenylalanine hydroxylase deficiency gives high phenylalanine and low tyrosine, and the treatment is a low-phenylalanine diet with tyrosine supplementation. Maple syrup urine disease is a branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase defect, so leucine, isoleucine and valine rise. Homocystinuria is usually cystathionine β-synthase deficiency, giving high homocysteine and low cysteine, and it predisposes to thrombosis and to lens dislocation, which is the opposite of Marfan syndrome. Alkaptonuria turns urine dark on standing because homogentisic acid oxidises to a pigment.

Hyperammonaemia is the emergency that matters. Ammonia is neurotoxic, the brain handles most clearance of it, and the liver converts it via the urea cycle. A high ammonia with normal liver enzymes suggests a urea cycle defect; a high ammonia with abnormal liver function suggests liver failure. The treatment principle in both is to remove nitrogen: restrict protein temporarily, give sodium benzoate or phenylacetate to trap nitrogen as excretable conjugates, and give arginine, which is an intermediate of the urea cycle.

#### Nucleotide metabolism: two pathways and three drugs

Purines are synthesised de novo on a ribose-phosphate scaffold, salvaged from the breakdown of existing nucleotides, or broken down to uric acid. Pyrimidines are synthesised de novo as orotate from aspartate and carbamoyl phosphate. The clinically useful drugs all target one of three steps.

- Azathioprine and 6-mercaptopurine are converted to 6-thioguanine nucleotides and inhibit de novo purine synthesis; 6-MP is also a substrate for HGPRT, so it also blocks the salvage pathway, and the combination is why the drug works in acute lymphoblastic leukaemia.
- Methotrexate inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, so thymidylate synthesis and purine synthesis both stall. Its toxicity is rescued by folinic acid, which enters the pathway downstream of the blocked enzyme.
- 5-fluorouracil inhibits thymidylate synthase, and its active metabolite is incorporated into DNA.

HGPRT deficiency causes Lesch-Nyhan syndrome: hyperuricemia, self-mutilation, and a characteristic neurological picture in boys. The other salvage defect is ADA deficiency, which causes severe combined immunodeficiency because both lymphocyte proliferation and toxic purine metabolites accumulate.

#### Worked example: the foetus with an enzyme defect

Folate and vitamin B12 share the methionine cycle. 5-methyltetrahydrofolate donates a carbon to homocysteine via methionine synthase, producing methionine; methionine synthase also needs methylcobalamin from B12. A B12 deficiency traps the folate as 5-methyltetrahydrofolate, so folate is unavailable for thymidylate and purine synthesis, and you get megaloblastic anaemia with a raised homocysteine and a raised methylmalonic acid. A folate deficiency gives the same anaemia with a raised homocysteine but a normal methylmalonic acid. That single distinguishing molecule is the question.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (3–5d)

#### Common mistakes

Believing glycolysis requires oxygen. It does not. The oxygen requirement sits at the electron transport chain; without it, NADH cannot be oxidised, so pyruvate is reduced to lactate to regenerate NAD⁺. Anaerobic glycolysis therefore still yields 2 ATP per glucose.

Reading Km as affinity in the wrong direction. Km is the substrate concentration at half-maximal rate, so a high Km means half-maximal rate needs more substrate, which means lower affinity. Competitive inhibition raises Km.

Counting ATP without noting the shuttle. Cytosolic NADH from glycolysis must be shuttled into the mitochondrion. The malate-aspartate shuttle yields about 2.5 ATP per NADH and the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle about 1.5, which is the whole difference between the 30 and 32 figures.

Treating an uncoupler as something that reduces metabolism. 2,4-dinitrophenol increases oxygen consumption and heat production while reducing ATP synthesis. Clinically, this is the picture of the hyperthermia and lactic acidosis of a salicylate overdose, and it is why nitroglycerin can be used as a mild uncoupler at high doses.

Forgetting that the urea cycle needs arginine as an intermediate. Arginase deficiency causes hyperammonaemia with citrullinaemia, and arginine becomes a therapeutic arginine supplement. Also remember that carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I is mitochondrial and uses N-acetylglutamate, while carbamoyl phosphate synthetase II is cytosolic and part of pyrimidine synthesis — two different enzymes with confusingly similar names.

#### Exam traps

NADH and NADPH look alike and behave differently. NADH drives catabolic pathways and the electron transport chain; NADPH drives anabolic pathways and antioxidant defence, because glutathione reductase and the pentose phosphate pathway both use it.

Oxaloacetate, not pyruvate, is the net product of the TCA cycle, and because gluconeogenesis drains it, the cycle depends on anaplerotic reactions to replace it — pyruvate carboxylase, which adds bicarbonate to pyruvate, is the main one.

Congenital galactosaemia from galactose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase deficiency shows up after milk feeding, with jaundice, hepatomegaly and E. coli sepsis — treat by removing lactose and galactose from the diet.

Lesch-Nyhan is X-linked, so it appears in boys, and it is one of the few syndromes where patients injure themselves.

#### Integrated mini-cases

**Case one.** A newborn has jaundice, hepatomegaly, cataracts and a reducing substance in the urine that is not glucose. The lens finding and the neonatal onset point to galactose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase deficiency. The reducing substance is galactose-1-phosphate and galactose, which react like glucose with the test strip but do not ferment with yeast.

**Case two.** A child is noted to have a high resting respiratory rate and a normal oxygen saturation. The blood gas shows a pH of 7.50 with a HCO₃⁻ of 35 and a PaCO₂ of 42. This is a metabolic alkalosis with appropriate respiratory compensation, since the PaCO₂ rises by roughly 0.7 mmHg for each 1 mmol/L rise in bicarbonate. Common causes are vomiting, diuretics and hyperaldosteronism.

**Case three.** A patient on a prolonged course of isoniazid for tuberculosis develops numbness in the hands and feet and a megaloblastic anaemia. Isoniazid interferes with pyridoxine, vitamin B6, which is a cofactor for the transaminases and for the decarboxylation steps that convert tryptophan to serotonin. Pyridoxine supplementation prevents the neuropathy; the anaemia reflects the folate trap shared with B12 deficiency.
