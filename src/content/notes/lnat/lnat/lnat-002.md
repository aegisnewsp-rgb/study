# Section A — Inference and Assumption — LNAT Notes

A run of Section A questions asks what follows from a passage rather than what it states outright. The three types — conclusion, assumption and the claim an argument would collapse without — look similar on the page, but they test different moves, and the wording of the stem usually tells you which one you are being asked to do. The skill is reducing each passage to its argument in one sentence: what is being claimed, what is offered as support, and what has been left unsaid. Once you can name the unsaid part, the answer options sort themselves. One option will bridge the gap the argument needs, and the others will either restate the passage or go further than the passage can carry.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Three question-types and how to tell them apart

- **Conclusion.** "Which of the following is the author most likely to conclude?" or "The argument's main conclusion is…". The right answer is the claim that follows from the support already given. It is a step further than what the passage says, but a small step.
- **Assumption.** "Which of the following is an assumption of the argument?" or "The argument relies on the assumption that…". The right answer is the claim the support depends on without saying so. Without it the chain breaks.
- **Necessary claim.** "Which of the following must also be true?" or "The argument would be undermined if…". The right answer is the claim the passage silently takes for granted. Remove it and the argument falls.

The stem wording is the difference. "Most likely to conclude" → conclusion. "Assumes" or "relies on" → assumption. "Must also be true" or "would weaken the argument" → necessary claim.

#### One-sentence reduction of a passage

After the first read, write down in one sentence: *the author claims that X, supports it by Y, and does not address Z*. That single sentence is the map for the inference and assumption questions. The conclusion question asks for X. The assumption question asks for the bit of Z that the argument silently relies on. The "must also be true" question asks for a further claim X depends on.

#### Two rules that stop most wrong answers

- **The right answer stays inside the passage's reach.** If the option would still be defensible without the passage, it is probably too far. Section A answers are marked against the printed words.
- **The right answer is the smallest defensible step.** If two options could both follow from the passage, pick the one that requires the smaller leap. Large leaps are usually wrong; the gap they bridge is the gap the passage deliberately left open.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### The conclusion question

A conclusion question asks: given the support in the passage, what claim is the author most likely to commit to? It is not the same as the topic sentence. The topic sentence is what the author says; the conclusion is what they have earned the right to say by the end of the passage.

**Worked example.** Read the passage with the one-sentence reduction in mind.

**Passage B.** *Several UK cities have piloted " pavement-level" cycling infrastructure — light segregation by planters and curbs at the same level as the footway, rather than a fully separated cycle track. Proponents argue the design is cheap and politically easier to install than Dutch-style separated lanes, and studies of the early pilots show measurable increases in cycling on the treated streets. Critics point out that pavement-level infrastructure is associated with higher rates of pedestrian-cyclist conflict than fully separated lanes, because the two user groups share the same surface. The conflict rates in the pilot data are low in absolute terms, but they are not low relative to the lower volume of cyclists the design attracts. The author's own view, stated in the closing paragraph, is that pavement-level infrastructure is a useful first step but should not be mistaken for the endpoint.*

**Q3.** Which of the following is the author most likely to conclude?

A. Cities should not install pavement-level cycling infrastructure at all.
B. Cities should install pavement-level cycling infrastructure, but only as a step toward fully separated lanes.
C. Pavement-level cycling infrastructure reduces pedestrian-cyclist conflict.
D. The political case for pavement-level infrastructure is stronger than the safety case against it.

**Reasoning.** The author calls the design "a useful first step but should not be mistaken for the endpoint." That is the conclusion. Option B states it. Option A is too strong — the author says it is useful. Option C contradicts the passage (conflict is higher, not lower). Option D invents a comparison the author never makes. Answer: **B**.

The trap in this kind of question is to pick the option that summarises the passage. Summarising is restating; concluding is what the support has earned. The two diverge whenever the author hedges ("a useful first step", "but not the endpoint") — the hedge is the conclusion.

#### The assumption question

An assumption question asks: what claim is the support relying on without saying so? The standard method is to state the argument in one sentence and ask which claim, if false, would collapse the support.

**Worked example.** Using the same passage B.

**Q4.** The argument that pavement-level infrastructure is "a useful first step" relies on the assumption that

A. cycling volumes on treated streets are lower than on streets with separated lanes.
B. there exists a more effective form of cycling infrastructure than the pavement-level design.
C. pedestrian-cyclist conflict can be measured reliably in the pilot data.
D. political ease of installation is a benefit worth preserving in later designs.

**Reasoning.** "A useful first step" only makes sense if there is somewhere further to step to. Without a more effective design to step toward, calling pavement-level infrastructure a first step is empty. Option B states that. Option A is a fact the passage mentions but does not depend on. Option C is a methodological concern the passage does not raise. Option D adds a value claim the passage does not make. Answer: **B**.

The trap in assumption questions is to pick an option that is true but not load-bearing. The test for load-bearing: remove the option, see if the chain still holds. If the chain still holds, the option was not the assumption.

#### The "must also be true" question

A "must also be true" question asks: what further claim does the argument silently take for granted? It is structurally similar to an assumption question but asks in the positive direction.

**Worked example.** Still using passage B.

**Q5.** Which of the following must also be true if the author's argument is to hold?

A. Pavement-level infrastructure is cheaper to install than fully separated lanes.
B. Pedestrian-cyclist conflict is a meaningful measure of cycling-infrastructure quality.
C. Cycling volumes will eventually fall if no further infrastructure is built.
D. Cities that installed pavement-level infrastructure will not later remove it.

**Reasoning.** The author's argument treats pedestrian-cyclist conflict as a relevant cost and treats the higher conflict rate as a real downside. If conflict were not a meaningful measure, the entire critique section of the passage would not register. Option B states that. Option A is true in the passage but is not load-bearing for the author's own view. Option C is invented. Option D is invented. Answer: **B**.

The trap in this kind of question is to pick an option the passage happens to mention. The test for "must also be true": deny the option, see if the argument still stands. If denying it leaves the argument intact, the option was not load-bearing.

#### How to read the stem in 5 seconds

A short checklist that works for most inference and assumption questions:

1. **What is the stem asking for?** Conclusion (a claim the support earns), assumption (a claim the support relies on), or necessary claim (a further claim the argument takes for granted).
2. **State the argument in one sentence.** Author claims X, supports it with Y, leaves Z unsaid.
3. **Find the option that matches the type.** A conclusion follows the support; an assumption is what the support depends on; a necessary claim is what the conclusion depends on.
4. **Test the option against the printed words.** A conclusion that needs information the passage never supplied is too far. An assumption that is not load-bearing (the chain holds without it) is wrong.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Why this skill transfers

Inference and assumption are the spine of legal reasoning. A barrister reading a case does exactly what Section A tests: separate what the court stated from what it decided, identify the claim the reasoning relies on without saying, and decide which further claim must hold for the decision to follow. The LNAT is not testing legal knowledge, but the skills it tests are the same ones first-year law students use when they read a case for the first time. Train them here and you are training a skill you will use across a law degree.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- **"An inference is just a guess."** An inference is a claim that follows with reasonable certainty from what is stated, not a leap into speculation. The right inference is the smallest defensible step the support earns. Anything larger is overreach.
- **"Assumptions are weaknesses in the argument."** Every argument has assumptions. The question is whether they are reasonable and whether they are stated. Section A usually asks you to name the unstated ones.
- **"If two options look defensible, I should pick the more interesting one."** Pick the smaller step. The more interesting option is often the larger leap, and the larger leap is the one the passage cannot carry.
- **"I should look for the option that uses the same words as the passage."** Word-matching is a weak signal. The right answer often paraphrases the passage; the wrong answer often quotes it. Match the move the option makes, not the words it uses.
- **"If I cannot find the assumption I should pick an option that sounds cautious."** Cautious-sounding options are often vague. The assumption is specific: deny it and the chain breaks.

#### Practice routine that builds inference and assumption

Take one passage a day from the LNAT practice test at lnat.ac.uk. After reading, write the one-sentence reduction on paper: *the author claims X, supports it with Y, leaves Z unsaid*. Then attempt each of the passage's three or four questions without looking at the options for a moment — write your own answer in one sentence. Then look at the options. If your answer matches one of them, you have read the passage correctly. If none of them matches, your answer is too far or not far enough; either way, the gap between your answer and the options is the lesson for the day.

The point of writing your own answer first is that it forces you to commit to a reading. Skimming the options before you have a reading is the most common way to lose a mark on inference questions — the option that looks most familiar tends to win, regardless of whether it is the one the passage earns.
