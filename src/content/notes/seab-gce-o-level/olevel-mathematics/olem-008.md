# Statistics and Probability — O-Level Mathematics (4052) Notes

Statistics and Probability is the third of the three strands of the O-Level Mathematics 4052 syllabus (alongside Number / Algebra and Geometry / Measurement). It covers data presentation and analysis (histograms, cumulative-frequency curves, box plots, stem-and-leaf diagrams), measures of central tendency and spread (mean, median, mode, standard deviation), and probability (sample space, Venn diagrams, tree diagrams, conditional probability). The strand is often paired with the geometry / measurement strand on a single Paper-2 question and is a reliable source of marks for a well-prepared Sec 4 student.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### The seven facts every Sec 4 student needs

1. **Mean**: sum of the values divided by the number of values.
2. **Median**: the middle value when the data is arranged in order (or the mean of the two middle values for an even number of values).
3. **Mode**: the value that occurs most often.
4. **Range**: the largest value minus the smallest value.
5. **Standard deviation**: a measure of spread around the mean; a small standard deviation means the data is clustered close to the mean.
6. **Probability of an event**: P(A) = (number of favourable outcomes) / (total number of equally likely outcomes). Always between 0 and 1.
7. **Complementary events**: P(A') = 1 − P(A).

#### Key notations

- μ = population mean
- x̄ = sample mean
- σ = population standard deviation
- s = sample standard deviation
- P(A) = probability of event A
- P(A ∩ B) = probability of both A and B occurring
- P(A ∪ B) = probability of A or B (or both) occurring
- P(A | B) = probability of A given that B has occurred

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Data presentation

**Frequency tables** list each value (or class interval) and the number of times it occurs. They are the foundation for every other form of data presentation.

**Histograms** are bar charts for continuous data; the bars touch (because the data is continuous) and the horizontal axis is a number line. Each bar's area is proportional to the frequency it represents; for equal class widths the bar height itself represents the frequency.

**Cumulative-frequency curves** plot the cumulative frequency against the upper class boundary. From the curve, the median is read off at the 50% cumulative-frequency level. The interquartile range is the difference between the upper quartile (75%) and the lower quartile (25%).

**Box plots** summarise a data set with five values: minimum, lower quartile, median, upper quartile, maximum. A box plot makes it easy to compare two or more data sets side by side.

**Stem-and-leaf diagrams** preserve the original data while organising it. The stem is the leading digit (or digits); the leaf is the trailing digit. A back-to-back stem-and-leaf diagram compares two data sets side by side.

#### Measures of central tendency

The **mean** is the sum of the values divided by the number of values. It is sensitive to outliers (one very large or very small value can pull the mean significantly).

The **median** is the middle value when the data is arranged in order. For an even number of values, the median is the mean of the two middle values. It is resistant to outliers — adding a single very large value to a data set may shift the mean but usually not the median.

The **mode** is the value that occurs most often. A data set can have more than one mode (bimodal, multimodal) or no mode at all. The mode is the only measure of central tendency that applies to categorical data.

A useful summary:
- Use the mean when the data has no outliers and the distribution is roughly symmetric.
- Use the median when the data has outliers or the distribution is skewed.
- Use the mode when the data is categorical or when the most common value is of interest.

#### Measures of spread

The **range** is the largest value minus the smallest value. It is sensitive to outliers.

The **interquartile range (IQR)** is the difference between the upper quartile (Q3) and the lower quartile (Q1). It is resistant to outliers — the IQR describes the spread of the middle 50% of the data.

The **standard deviation** is a measure of spread around the mean. A small standard deviation means the data is clustered close to the mean; a large standard deviation means the data is spread out. The standard deviation is sensitive to outliers.

For a data set of n values x₁, x₂, …, xₙ with mean x̄:
- Variance σ² = (Σ (xᵢ − x̄)²) / n
- Standard deviation σ = √(variance)

For a sample, divide by (n − 1) instead of n (Bessel's correction).

#### Probability

A probability is a number between 0 and 1 that measures how likely an event is to occur. P(certain) = 1; P(impossible) = 0.

**Sample space** is the set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment. For a coin toss, the sample space is {Heads, Tails}. For two coin tosses, it is {HH, HT, TH, TT}.

**Equally likely outcomes**: if every outcome in the sample space is equally likely, then P(event) = (number of favourable outcomes) / (total number of outcomes).

**Complementary events**: P(A') = 1 − P(A). The event A' is "A does not occur".

**Addition rule**: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B). For mutually exclusive events (events that cannot both occur), P(A ∩ B) = 0, so P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B).

**Multiplication rule**: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B | A). For independent events (events where the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other), P(B | A) = P(B), so P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B).

**Conditional probability**: P(A | B) is the probability of A given that B has occurred. P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B).

#### Venn diagrams and tree diagrams

A **Venn diagram** shows the relationships between two or three events as overlapping circles inside a rectangle (the rectangle represents the sample space). The number of outcomes in each region is written inside the region. The diagram is used to compute P(A ∪ B), P(A ∩ B), P(A | B) by counting outcomes in the appropriate regions.

A **tree diagram** shows a sequence of events as branches. Each branch is labelled with the probability of that branch occurring. The probability of a path (a complete set of branches from root to leaf) is the product of the probabilities along the path. The probability of an event that corresponds to multiple paths is the sum of the probabilities of those paths.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Where Statistics and Probability appears across the papers

- **O-Level Mathematics 4052 Paper 1**: short structured items on mean / median / mode, simple probability, and reading data from a table or chart.
- **O-Level Mathematics 4052 Paper 2**: long structured items on cumulative-frequency curves, box plots, conditional probability, and the interpretation of real-world data (e.g. weather statistics, sports statistics).
- **Across both papers**: drawing a clear diagram (Venn diagram, tree diagram, cumulative-frequency curve) is the highest-leverage exam technique.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "The mean is always the best measure of central tendency." It is sensitive to outliers; the median is the better measure for skewed data.
- "Two events are mutually exclusive if they cannot occur at the same time." That is the definition of mutually exclusive. "Independent" means the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other.
- "P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)." Only when A and B are mutually exclusive. Otherwise, P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B).
- "Standard deviation is in the same units as the mean." Standard deviation is in the same units as the data; variance is in the square of those units.
- "A box plot shows every data point." A box plot summarises the data with five values; it does not show the individual data points.

#### Exam technique

- For statistics questions, state the formula before substituting. The formula line is the method mark.
- For probability questions, list the sample space if it is small (≤ 12 outcomes). The list is the method mark.
- For Venn diagram questions, label every region with its count or probability. An unlabeled region earns no marks.
- For tree diagram questions, label every branch with its probability. The branch label is the method mark.
