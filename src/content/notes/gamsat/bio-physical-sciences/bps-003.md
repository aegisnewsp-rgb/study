# Cell Biology and Physiology

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review

ACER's 2026 Information Booklet sets out how this section is built: 75 multiple-choice questions in 150 minutes, sat at a test centre, with "four alternative answers from which test takers choose the most appropriate", and the discipline areas mixed through the paper rather than blocked. The booklet gives the proportions as "Chemistry 40% Biology 40% Physics 20%", so cell biology and physiology sit inside the Biology share. It also sets the knowledge ceiling: the biology and chemistry needed "generally corresponds to the first year of university studies", while adding that the test "focuses primarily on problem solving and on the use of the prerequisite knowledge".

First-year biology is the boundary. Within it, this topic is four areas, and each one arrives in a shape you can recognise.

| Sub-area | What the stem hands you | The decision you have to make |
|---|---|---|
| Organelles | A structure, a function, or a symptom | Which compartment is responsible |
| Membrane transport | A gradient, a molecule, an energy budget | Whether ATP is being spent |
| Enzyme kinetics | A curve, or one variable changed | What the new shape means |
| Homeostasis | A variable that has moved off its range | What pushes it back |

**The four areas are one system, not four lists.** Organelle function decides which transporters a cell has. Transporters decide what the cell can take in and throw out. Enzyme kinetics sets how fast any of it runs. Homeostasis decides when the whole thing gets adjusted. A question that crosses two of these is the normal case.

**Name the compartment from the observation.** You are not asked to list organelles; you are handed a clue and have to name the structure that explains it.

| Compartment | What it does | The clue in the question |
|---|---|---|
| Nucleus | Holds the DNA, transcribes messages, builds ribosomes | Double membrane, nucleolus, site of transcription |
| Rough ER | Folds and modifies proteins for export | Ribosomes on the surface, secreted protein |
| Smooth ER | Lipid synthesis, detoxification, calcium storage | No ribosomes, steroid hormone |
| Golgi apparatus | Modifies, sorts and packages proteins | Flattened cisternae, vesicles |
| Mitochondrion | ATP by oxidative phosphorylation | Double membrane, inner membrane folded into cristae |
| Lysosome | Acid hydrolases, digests waste and worn-out organelles | Acidic interior, hydrolytic enzymes |
| Peroxisome | Shortens very long-chain fatty acids, destroys hydrogen peroxide | Catalase, fatty acids |
| Cytoskeleton | Shape, transport inside the cell, movement | Microtubules, actin, intermediate filaments |

**Read the gradient, not the molecule.** Whether the substance is oxygen, water, glucose or sodium, only two things decide the mechanism: which way it is being pushed, and whether energy is available.

| Process | Direction | Uses ATP | Typical case |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple diffusion | Down the gradient | No | Oxygen and carbon dioxide across the alveolar membrane |
| Osmosis | Water down its own gradient | No | Water from blood into tissue fluid |
| Facilitated diffusion | Down the gradient | No | Glucose through a GLUT transporter |
| Active transport | Against the gradient | Yes | The sodium-potassium pump; glucose absorbed in the gut |

**The plateau is the examinable part of enzyme kinetics.** Rate against substrate concentration rises, then flattens, because every active site is occupied and more substrate buys nothing. The curve is described by

V = Vmax[S] / (Km + [S])

which you read as: when substrate concentration equals Km, the rate is half of Vmax. Below Km, substrate is limiting. Above Km, enzyme is limiting.

**Homeostasis is a loop, not a wall.** A variable moves, a sensor detects the deviation, a control centre compares it against a set point, an effector pushes it back. Thermoregulation, blood glucose, blood pressure and water balance are the four systems you will meet. Positive feedback, which amplifies the change rather than opposing it, shows up in blood clotting and the contractions of labour.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study

#### Identifying a compartment from the clue in the stem

The stem rarely names the organelle. It describes something the organelle does, and you match it. "Protein with a sugar chain added, then sorted and dispatched in a vesicle" points at the Golgi apparatus. "Protein made on a membrane studded with ribosomes" points at the rough ER. "Fatty acid broken down and hydrogen peroxide destroyed" points at the peroxisome, and only the peroxisome has catalase.

Two pairs cause most of the errors. Mitochondrion and chloroplast are both double-membraned and both make ATP, but only the mitochondrion makes ATP by oxidising fuel with oxygen; the chloroplast does it with light and water. Rough and smooth ER are the same organelle with different jobs, and ribosomes are the only thing that separates them in a question.

One more distinction is worth holding onto, because it shows up in questions about secretion. A protein destined for secretion is made on a ribosome attached to the rough ER, threaded into the ER lumen, modified, and shipped in vesicles that bud from the ER and fuse with the Golgi. It leaves the cell by exocytosis. If the question asks what happens when the rough ER is damaged, the answer runs backwards down that route: no folding, no Golgi processing, no secretion.

#### Two tests that eliminate most membrane-transport options

Test one: direction and energy. If the stem says the substance moves against its concentration gradient, or gives an energy budget that cannot cover the movement, the answer involves ATP. If the substance moves down its gradient with nothing being spent, the answer is one of the diffusion family.

Test two: does a protein have to be involved? Water moving across a membrane is osmosis. A polar molecule that cannot cross the lipid bilayer on its own moves by facilitated diffusion through a carrier or channel. A small nonpolar molecule such as oxygen crosses directly by simple diffusion.

Within the passive family, water is the one that has its own name, so it is worth remembering that osmosis is not "diffusion of solutes". Osmosis is the movement of water from a region of higher water potential to lower water potential, and a cell placed in a hypertonic solution loses water and shrinks while a cell in a hypotonic solution takes water in and swells.

The sodium-potassium pump is the case worth knowing in detail, because it sets up almost everything else. It moves three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in per ATP hydrolysed, both against their gradients. That makes the membrane electrically negative inside and sets up the gradients that the cotransporters then exploit, so secondary active transport (glucose absorbed across the gut wall) costs no ATP directly but depends on a pump that does.

#### Reading the enzyme curve and its shifts

| Change | Effect on Vmax | Effect on Km |
|---|---|---|
| More enzyme | Vmax rises | Km unchanged |
| More substrate | Vmax unchanged | Km unchanged |
| Competitive inhibitor | Vmax unchanged | Km rises, curve shifts right |
| Non-competitive inhibitor | Vmax falls | Km unchanged |
| Temperature above the optimum | Vmax falls as protein denatures | Not a meaningful shift |
| pH away from optimum | Vmax falls | Not a meaningful shift |
| Irreversible inhibitor | Vmax falls | Not a meaningful shift |

The two inhibitor rows are the ones that get swapped under pressure, so fix the signature rather than the story: a competitive inhibitor competes for the active site, so more substrate outcompetes it and the maximum rate is reachable at a higher substrate concentration. A non-competitive inhibitor binds somewhere that is not the active site and changes the enzyme's working shape, so adding more substrate cannot rescue the maximum rate.

Temperature and pH behave differently again. Raising temperature speeds the reaction until the enzyme's structure starts to fail, so the curve rises and then the enzyme denatures and the rate falls away. pH does the same thing through the ionisation of the active site, which is why the pH curve has a peak rather than a plateau, and why a shift of only a few pH units can halve the rate.

#### The homeostatic loop and the ways it fails

| System | Variable | Sensor | Effector |
|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Core temperature | Thermoreceptors in skin and hypothalamus | Sweat glands, skin blood vessels, shivering |
| Blood glucose | Glucose concentration | Beta and alpha cells of the pancreas | Insulin drives glucose into cells; glucagon releases it from the liver |
| Blood pressure | Arterial pressure | Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch | Heart rate, stroke volume, vessel constriction; kidneys adjust salt and water |
| Water balance | Plasma osmolarity and volume | Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus | Thirst centre; kidneys alter water reabsorption and ADH release |

A loop is only as good as its weakest part, and questions often name the part that has failed. If the control output fails, the loop structure can look perfect and the variable still runs away. If the effectors are overwhelmed, the sensor and control centre are reading the deviation correctly and cannot respond. If the set point itself is moved, the loop is working exactly as designed and the variable still ends up away from where it started.

Positive feedback is the deliberate exception. In childbirth, cervical stretching triggers oxytocin release, which strengthens contractions, which causes more stretching. In clotting, platelets attract platelets at a damaged vessel. These amplify the original event instead of opposing it, and an item that asks you to identify a reinforcing loop is pointing at one of them.

#### A worked example that runs three sub-areas at once

After a meal, blood glucose rises. Pancreatic beta cells detect the rise and release insulin. Insulin binds receptors on muscle and fat cells, and those cells insert more GLUT transporters into their membranes. Glucose then enters those cells by facilitated diffusion, which is passive and spends no ATP. The transporters themselves had to be made and moved into the membrane, which does cost energy, and the liver stops releasing glucose. The result is that blood glucose falls back toward its set point.

Map it: homeostasis supplies the target, the hormone is the control output, the transporter is the membrane mechanism, and receptor binding is saturable. When a question asks which step requires ATP, the obvious answer (glucose crossing the membrane) is the passive one, which is exactly why students who assume the important-looking step is the expensive step get it wrong.

#### Common mistakes

Confusing osmosis with the diffusion of solutes, so water movement is described as "diffusion of water molecules down the gradient" with no membrane in the picture. Reading a plateau as "the reaction has stopped" rather than "the enzyme is saturated". Swapping competitive and non-competitive inhibition. Assuming that because a molecule matters to the body, moving it must cost energy. Treating the set point as a constant that cannot move. And naming an organelle from its job alone when the stem has given a structural clue that identifies a different one.

### 🔴 Extended — Mastery

#### The saturation curve is the same object in three places

Read a plateau once and you can read it everywhere. Enzyme catalysis, membrane transport and receptor signalling all saturate, and all three behave the same way.

| System | What saturates | What the plateau means | What raises the ceiling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enzyme catalysis | Active sites on the enzyme | Substrate is no longer limiting | More enzyme |
| Membrane transport | Carrier and channel proteins | The transporters are all occupied | More transporter protein |
| Receptor signalling | Binding sites on the receptor | Maximum response is reached | More receptor |

The one-line version: what saturates is always a finite number of protein molecules, and the ceiling always rises by adding more of that protein. This is why doubling the enzyme concentration doubles Vmax while doubling the substrate concentration changes nothing once Km has been passed.

#### Where marks are lost

| Symptom | Where it comes from | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Every movement "needs energy" | Assuming a molecule matters, so moving it must cost ATP | Find the gradient in the stem first |
| Plateau read as inhibition | Confusing saturation with a stopped reaction | Saturation means enzyme, not substrate, is the limit |
| Inhibitor types swapped | Remembering only which one changes Vmax | Fix the signature: competitive raises Km, non-competitive lowers Vmax |
| Organelle confused with its partner | Mitochondrion against chloroplast, rough against smooth ER | Use the structural clue the stem gives you |
| Homeostasis read as "stops change" | Treating the set point as fixed | The loop opposes deviation; the set point can move |

#### Negative feedback has a ceiling

A homeostatic loop opposes deviation. It does not prevent it, and the set point is not a constant. During exercise, core temperature rises and the loop works to bring it down while allowing a temporary rise, because heat production and heat loss have both changed. A fever is the same shape with the set point moved upward by pyrogens, so the loop is working correctly to bring temperature to a new target rather than failing.

The failure cases carry the marks, because each one points at a different part of the loop.

| What is wrong | Which part failed | Observable result |
|---|---|---|
| Diabetes mellitus | Control output: insulin is absent or the cells do not respond to it | Glucose stays high although the loop structure is intact |
| Fever | Set point moved up by pyrogens | Temperature high, and the loop is behaving as designed |
| Heat stroke | Effectors overwhelmed, sweating fails | Temperature high with no negative feedback response |
| Dehydration | Fluid loss beyond what the loop can correct | Volume falls, osmolarity rises, thirst increases |

#### Why the transport and enzyme answers look different

Transport and enzymes both saturate, but the variable that saturates them is different, and that changes what a drug does to the curve. A transporter blocker that stops some transporters working lowers the maximum rate, because the ceiling is set by how many transporters remain functional. A pump inhibitor removes the energy supply, so the whole curve drops rather than shifting sideways. An enzyme inhibitor that competes for the active site shifts the curve right and leaves the ceiling reachable at a higher substrate concentration. An inhibitor that binds elsewhere lowers the ceiling and no amount of substrate brings it back.

This is the same discrimination the exam rewards in pharmacology and physiology, expressed in cell biology terms: ask what has been taken away, and whether the ceiling is still reachable.

#### A five-step routine for this topic

Name the mechanism before you read the options, so you know what you are judging them against. Find the gradient or the variable that has moved. Ask whether energy is being spent and from where. Translate the stem into a physical consequence in one sentence. Then discard any option that assumes something the stem never gave you, such as a specific organelle, a hormone or a transport protein that was never mentioned.

#### When a question crosses into physiology

Cell biology stops being a topic and becomes the reasoning when a stem describes a symptom and asks for the mechanism. Polyuria in diabetes is an osmotic consequence: glucose cannot enter cells without adequate insulin, so it stays in the plasma, raising plasma osmolarity, so water leaves the cells and follows the glucose out through the kidney. Each step is a cell-biology fact, and the answer to a question about the mechanism is always one of those steps rather than the disease name.

The same pattern covers hypothermia, exercise heat load, altitude acclimatisation, and why a fever produces a thirst response. Find the loop, find the variable, find the step that failed, and the option writes itself.
