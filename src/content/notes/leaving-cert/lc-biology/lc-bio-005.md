# Characteristics of Life and Viruses — Leaving Certificate (Established) Biology Notes

Unit 1.1 opens Strand 1, Organisation of Life, and it does two jobs. It gives you the set of properties that separate living systems from non-living ones, and then it hands you viruses — the deliberate awkward case in the whole course — and asks you to argue about which side of that line they belong on. The specification wording is "discuss the difficulty of defining viruses". Candidates who memorise "viruses are non-living" collect the easy mark and forfeit every reasoning mark, because a discussion question is scored on both sides of the argument.

The properties the specification names are **organisation, response, respiration, nutrition, excretion, reproduction, growth, metabolism, homeostasis, heredity and the cellular basis of life**. That is eleven, which is longer than the seven-item acronym most textbooks hand you, and reproducing the specification list on paper is worth a mark. The last item — the cellular basis of life — is why unit 1.1 sits directly beside unit 1.3 on Cells: everything from this unit onwards rests on the claim that cells are the unit of life.

This unit carries weight 2 in the topic catalogue, the lowest of the strand 1 numbered units, but it feeds two heavy units: unit 2.4 revisits virus replication and immune response, and unit 3.2 treats viruses alongside bacteria, fungi and protists. An hour spent here is not wasted; it is an hour that makes two later units cheaper.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **The eleven properties (specification list).** Organisation, response, respiration, nutrition, excretion, reproduction, growth, metabolism, homeostasis, heredity, cellular basis of life. Never answer "what are the characteristics of living things" with a seven-word acronym; write the specification list and then add detail to any two.
- **Nutrition split.** *Autotrophic* organisms make their own food by photosynthesis or chemosynthesis; *heterotrophic* organisms obtain food from other organisms or organic matter. Plants, algae and some bacteria are autotrophic; animals, fungi and most bacteria are heterotrophic.
- **A virus is acellular.** It has no cytoplasm, no ribosomes, no mitochondria, no cell membrane of its own in the naked capsid types. It cannot make ATP, cannot synthesise protein, and cannot grow or respond.
- **A virus is only DNA *or* RNA, never both.** This is the cleanest fact in the unit and the most frequently examined. DNA viruses include adenovirus, herpes virus, HIV (as a retrovirus it carries RNA but replicates through a DNA intermediate) and bacteriophages such as T-even phage. RNA viruses include influenza, rabies, polio, measles, tobacco mosaic virus and coronaviruses.
- **Structure.** Nucleic acid core + protein coat called a **capsid**, assembled from capsomeres. Some viruses add a **lipid envelope** derived from the host cell membrane; enveloped viruses are generally more fragile and more easily destroyed by detergents and drying.
- **Obligate intracellular parasitism.** A virus can only reproduce inside a living host cell, and only by hijacking the host's ribosomes and enzymes. Outside a cell a virion is inert and metabolically inactive.
- **Two replication routes.** *Lytic cycle*: the virus enters, hijacks, lyses the cell and releases new virions. *Lysogenic (latent) cycle*: viral DNA is incorporated into the host chromosome and copied passively each time the host divides, with no damage until an inducing stress event triggers lytic replication.
- **Economic and medical importance.** Crop damage (tobacco mosaic virus in tobacco; foot-and-mouth virus; plant viruses generally), human disease (influenza, COVID-19, measles, polio, HIV, rabies, HPV), and diagnostics — bacteriophages are used as precise detectors of specific bacteria in water testing, and some are in clinical use as a treatment for bacterial infections.

#### Examiner traps

- **"Viruses are dead."** Too blunt. The scored answer is that viruses show *some* characteristics of life (they have genetic material, they reproduce, they respond to stimuli in the sense that a specific host molecule is required, they can evolve) and *lack* others (no metabolism, no independent growth, no cellular organisation). Saying "dead" with no evidence scores zero; saying "living" with no evidence also scores zero.
- **Saying a virus "respires" or "grows".** Neither happens. Growth and division occur only inside the host.
- **Confusing capsid with envelope.** The capsid is protein and is always present; the envelope is lipid, is optional, and is taken from the host.
- **Calling a virus a bacterium.** A bacterium is a prokaryote with a cell wall, plasmid, ribosomes and its own metabolism. A virus has none of these.
- **Forgetting the "either DNA or RNA" distinction.** When a question asks you to compare a DNA virus with an RNA virus, that single sentence is usually the first mark.
- **Assuming one host only.** Bacteriophages are host-specific — that specificity is what makes them useful as indicators of bacterial contamination.

#### 30-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Can you write all eleven characteristics of living things from memory, in specification order?
- [ ] Can you label capsid, capsomere, nucleic acid and envelope on a diagram of a virus, and state which one is optional?
- [ ] Can you define obligate intracellular parasitism in one sentence?
- [ ] Can you sketch the lytic and lysogenic cycles side by side and say which one damages the host cell?
- [ ] Can you name one DNA virus and one RNA virus without hesitating?

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### Testing a proposed organism against the life processes

The way to answer "is it alive?" is never to assert. It is to run the proposed organism against each property and record the evidence. This is the move that earns the reasoning marks.

| Property | Evidence for a bacterium | Evidence for a virus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Organisation | Cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, DNA in a nucleoid | Capsid and genome only; no organelles |
| Nutrition | Autotrophic (some) or heterotrophic; absorbs nutrients | None; cannot absorb or synthesise food |
| Respiration | Aerobic or anaerobic respiration, ATP produced | None; no ATP production |
| Excretion | Wastes diffuse out across the membrane | None |
| Response | Chemotaxis, membrane receptor changes | Only a specific chemical match to a host receptor |
| Reproduction | Binary fission, independent of a host | Only inside a host cell |
| Growth | Cells increase in size then divide | Virions are assembled, not grown |
| Metabolism | Sum of all the enzyme-catalysed reactions | No metabolic machinery present |
| Homeostasis | Maintains internal pH, ion balance, osmotic balance | No internal conditions to maintain |
| Heredity | DNA carries heritable information; mutations arise | Genome carries heritable information; high mutation rate |

A workable exam paragraph: *"A bacterium meets eight of the eleven criteria but fails the strict 'cellular basis' test only in the sense that it lacks a membrane-bound nucleus — it is still a cell. A virus meets heredity and the capacity to reproduce, but fails organisation, nutrition, respiration, excretion, metabolism and homeostasis, because it possesses none of the machinery for those processes."* That paragraph picks up both sides of the discussion.

#### How a virus actually reproduces

Every virus follows the same four-step shape regardless of host: **attachment, penetration, synthesis, assembly and release**. Learn it as APSAR or use the longer version if memory is good.

1. **Attachment.** Specific proteins or glycoproteins on the capsid or envelope bind to matching receptor molecules on the host cell membrane. This specificity is why a given virus infects a given cell type — which is why HIV targets helper T lymphocytes bearing CD4 receptors.
2. **Penetration.** The whole virion is taken in by endocytosis, or the viral genome alone is injected and the capsid is left outside.
3. **Synthesis.** The viral genome hijacks the host's ribosomes, enzymes, nucleotides and ATP. For an RNA virus the genome must first be copied into DNA by **reverse transcriptase** if the virus is a retrovirus such as HIV, because translation can only proceed from DNA.
4. **Assembly.** New genome and capsid proteins are assembled together into virions.
5. **Release.** The host cell lyses (lytic cycle) or buds the virions out taking part of the host membrane as an envelope.

Antibiotics do not work against viruses because they target bacterial structures — the bacterial cell wall or the bacterial ribosome — that a virus does not possess. This is a standard Higher-level explanatory question and the answer is one sentence long once you know why.

#### The lytic and lysogenic cycles

In the lytic cycle the viral nucleic acid is separate from the host chromosome. It is used as a template immediately, new virions are assembled, and the cell bursts. Repeating this kills the host cell and is how the illness manifests.

In the lysogenic cycle viral DNA is integrated into the host chromosome as a **prophage**. It is replicated along with the host DNA every time the cell divides, so every daughter cell inherits the virus and nothing goes wrong. A stressor — ultraviolet light, certain chemicals, stress in the host — can trigger the prophage to excise itself and switch to the lytic cycle. Herpes virus and the human papillomavirus behave this way, which is why cold sores recur at the same site and why the virus can be dormant for years.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Prions and the edge of the definition

Unit 2.4 lists **prions** among the pathogens. A prion is an infectious protein with no nucleic acid at all. It causes a normal protein to misfold into the abnormal shape, and the misfolded form then templates more misfolding. Scrapie in sheep, BSE in cattle and Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease in humans are the named examples.

A prion is the sharpest test case in the specification for the definition of life, because it has no genome — which removes the one criterion viruses usually satisfy. If a question asks you to discuss why viruses and prions are difficult to classify, prions give you the stronger half of the argument: a virus has genetic information and the capacity to direct its own reproduction inside a host; a prion has neither, yet it propagates. The best answers treat this as evidence that the living/non-living line is a continuum rather than a clean cut.

#### How this unit pays elsewhere

- **Unit 2.4 (Response)** reassesses viruses: replication inside cells, differences between DNA and RNA viruses, innate versus adaptive immunity, and the emergence of infectious disease.
- **Unit 3.2 (Microorganisms)** places viruses in a group with bacteria, fungi, protists and parasitic animals, and asks you to distinguish bacteria from fungi on structure, nutrition and cellular nature.
- **The Biology in Practice Investigation** is 40% of the overall grade and is built on the unifying strand. Designing an investigation into antimicrobial effect, or into how a plant extract affects virus spread on leaf discs, uses exactly the reasoning set out here.

#### Mistakes students actually make

1. **Answering the discussion question with a verdict.** "Viruses are alive" earns nothing on its own. Write the two-sided case and then, if a conclusion is asked for, reach one and justify it with a named property.
2. **Listing MRS GREN instead of the specification list.** If your school taught you seven, keep seven in your head as a memory aid but write the specification's eleven on the page.
3. **Omitting "either DNA or RNA, never both."** It is the single most testable sentence about virus structure.
4. **Describing viruses as "small bacteria" or "half-living cells."** Neither phrase is defensible.
5. **Forgetting that enveloped viruses are killed by detergents and drying.** It is the practical reason handwashing with soap works, and it turns directly into a mark on a pathogens question.
6. **Assuming all bacteriophages are harmful.** They are host-specific, which is why they are used to identify bacteria in water quality testing and are being trialled as a treatment where antibiotics fail.
7. **Leaving a virus question without a named example.** Every claim about DNA or RNA viruses, lytic or lysogenic cycles, or crop damage is stronger with a named case attached.
