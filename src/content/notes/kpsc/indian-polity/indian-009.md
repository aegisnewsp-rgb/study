# Judiciary and Constitutional Bodies

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

The Indian constitutional framework splits adjudicatory power between the **judiciary** (Supreme Court → High Courts → District Courts → Subordinate Courts, under Articles 124–147 and 214–237) and a set of **constitutional bodies** established across Parts IX, IX-A, XIV-A, and Chapter II of Part V.

**Key bodies to memorise:** Election Commission of India (Art. 324), Finance Commission (Art. 280), GST Council (Art. 279A), CAG (Art. 148), Attorney General (Art. 76), UPSC and State PSCs (Art. 315–323), Lokpal, and NHRC.

- **Writs (Art. 32 SC, Art. 226 HC):** Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto. Article 32 is itself a **fundamental right**.
- **SC judge tenure:** appointed by the President, retire at **65 years**; HC judges at **62 years**.
- **15th Finance Commission** recommended **41%** devolution to States (2021–26).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Court Hierarchy and Jurisdiction

The Supreme Court sits at the apex with **original jurisdiction** in Centre-State and fundamental-rights disputes (Art. 32), **appellate jurisdiction** in civil, criminal and constitutional matters (Art. 132–136), and **advisory jurisdiction** on presidential references (Art. 143). High Courts hold writ jurisdiction under Article 226 — wider than Article 32 because it extends beyond fundamental rights to "any other purpose". Article 141 makes every Supreme Court declaration binding on **all courts in India**.

#### Independence Safeguards

The Constitution protects judicial independence through three concrete mechanisms: **security of tenure** (SC judges retire at 65, HC at 62; removal only by Parliament on grounds of proved misbehaviour or incapacity), **salaries charged on the Consolidated Fund** (Art. 125, 221), and a **post-retirement practice ban** (Art. 124(7)).

| Article | Purpose | Key fact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Art. 124(2) | SC judge appointment | By President after consultation; retire at 65 |
| Art. 125 | SC salaries | Charged on Consolidated Fund of India |
| Art. 217 | HC judge appointment | Retire at 62; consultation with CJI mandatory post-99th Amendment |
| Art. 227 | HC supervisory power | Over all subordinate courts and tribunals |

#### Judicial Review and Collegium

The **Basic Structure Doctrine** emerged in *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973) and was reaffirmed in *Minerva Mills v. UoI* (1980). The **Collegium** (CJI + 4 senior-most puisne SC judges for SC appointments; CJI + 2 senior-most HC judges for HC appointments) evolved through three Judges Cases (1982, 1993, 1998). The **National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Act, 2014** was struck down in *Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India* (2015) for violating the basic structure.

#### Constitutional Bodies at a Glance

- **Election Commission (Art. 324):** now a **three-member body** (1 CEC + 2 ECs) after the Election Commissioners Act, 2023; tenure 6 years or age 65, whichever is earlier.
- **Finance Commission (Art. 280):** appointed every five years by the President; recommends tax devolution; **advisory, not binding** (Art. 281).
- **CAG (Art. 148–151):** audits Union and State accounts; removable on grounds similar to an SC judge.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Examiner Traps

Article 226 is **not** a subordinate form of Article 32 — both operate as parallel, independent jurisdictions. A petitioner bypasses the SC by going directly to a High Court for non-fundamental-rights matters. Article 32's scope is restricted to **enforcement of fundamental rights**, but its availability is itself a guaranteed right. KPSC Mains frequently tests this distinction in assertion-reason format.

The **NHRC, Lokpal, and CBI** sit outside the strict constitutional-body list but are tested together: NHRC was created by statute (Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993), Lokpal by the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013, while CBI derives from the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946. Conflating their statutory origin with their constitutional status is a common error.

#### Worked Scenario

A writ petition is filed in the Karnataka High Court challenging a state law on grounds that infringe **Article 14 (equality)**. The HC issues a writ. The state appeals to the SC under **Article 136 (Special Leave Petition)**. The SC admits the SLP and decides the question of law. Under **Article 141**, all courts in India — including lower Karnataka courts and tribunals — are now bound by the SC's interpretation of Article 14 in this matter.

#### Common Mistakes

- Treating NJAC as a parallel operational system — it has been **struck down** since 2015.
- Assuming Finance Commission recommendations are **mandatory**; they are advisory (Art. 281).
- Confusing **CAG's appointment** (by President) with **removal** (grounds similar to an SC judge).
- Quoting the **15th FC's 41%** devolution figure for the 16th FC — the 16th FC was constituted on 31 December 2024 with a five-year window.

#### Exam Strategy

In KPSC KAS Prelims (GS Paper II), this cluster yields **2–4 questions per cycle**, usually two on constitutional bodies and one on a recent SC judgment. Mains (GS-II) expects a 150-word answer comparing the Collegium and NJAC models, citing the Fourth Judges Case verdict.

#### Practice prompts:
1. Compare writ jurisdiction under Articles 32 and 226. Why is Article 32 called the "heart and soul" of the Constitution?
2. Discuss the constitutional status, composition, and independence safeguards of the Election Commission after the 2023 amendment.
