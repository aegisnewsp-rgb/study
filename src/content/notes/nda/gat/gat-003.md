# Comprehension

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Comprehension** in NDA GAT measures how accurately you extract meaning from an unseen passage. The paper presents 2–4 passages followed by objective questions that test four skills: **literal recall**, **inference**, **vocabulary-in-context**, and **tone/purpose identification**. Around **5% of the GAT paper** (English section) is devoted to this.

The fastest scoring technique is to **read the questions first**, then scan the passage for keywords. Treat each passage as a one-pass job — 6–8 minutes including reading and answering — and never leave a comprehension question blank since there is no negative marking.

#### Must-Know Question Types

| Type | What it tests | Speed trick |
|------|---------------|-------------|
| Main idea | Central theme of the passage | Look at first and last paragraph |
| Vocabulary | Word meaning from context | Read two sentences before and after |
| Inference | What is implied, not stated | Eliminate anything explicitly written |
| Tone/Attitude | Author's emotional stance | Note adjectives and adverbs |
| Fact extraction | Names, dates, numbers | Scan vertically for the keyword |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

The NDA GAT English paper tests comprehension through unseen passages drawn from editorials, science write-ups, biographies, and narrative pieces. Each passage is followed by 4–6 MCQs, worth roughly 1–2 marks each. **NDA marking is +2.5 for correct, –0.83 for wrong, 0 for unattempted**, so blind guessing is costly but educated elimination pays.

#### Core Skills You Must Build

**Skimming** is rapid reading to capture the central idea. Read the first sentence of each paragraph, the last paragraph, and any bold or repeated words. This takes 30–45 seconds per passage and gives you the scaffolding for every question.

**Scanning** is the reverse: move your eyes vertically (a column at a time) hunting for a specific name, date, figure, or keyword named in the question. Most factual questions are answered in under 20 seconds once you locate the line.

**Inference** questions are the most frequent trap. The correct answer is **supported by the passage but not directly stated**. Three filters help: (1) if the answer is verbatim in the passage, it is recall, not inference; (2) if the answer contradicts the passage, reject it; (3) if the answer needs outside knowledge to be true, reject it.

**Vocabulary in context** replaces rote dictionary learning. The surrounding sentences — clauses of contrast, cause, or example — give the intended sense. Look for signal connectors: *however, although, for instance, in other words*.

#### Common Answer Patterns

| Passage feature | Correct-answer signal | Wrong-answer signal |
|-----------------|----------------------|---------------------|
| Qualifying words (*often, usually, sometimes*) | Carefully matches the degree | Absolute (*always, never*) |
| Author's adjectives | Mirrors the emotional register | Neutral or opposite emotion |
| Cause-effect connectors | Follows the stated chain | Adds an unstated cause |
| Tone questions | Describes the writer, not the topic | Describes the subject matter |

Practice one passage daily from *The Hindu* editorials or *Indian Express* op-eds, timing yourself at 7 minutes per passage.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Question-Type Anatomy

NDA comprehension questions fall into five mechanical types, each with a distinct elimination method.

- **Central idea questions** ask for the *primary* purpose. The correct choice is broad enough to cover every paragraph; specific details and minor sub-points are wrong.
- **Tone and attitude questions** hinge on **lexical markers** — words like *regrettably, heartening, alarmingly, lament* — rather than subject matter. A passage about a war can still be written in a *neutral, analytical* tone.
- **Author's purpose** is distinct from tone. Purpose asks *why* the author wrote: to inform, persuade, criticise, entertain, or reflect. Tone asks *how* — the emotional colouring.
- **Vocabulary-in-context** tests the dictionary sense *as used* by the author. Two sentences of context usually resolve ambiguity. Never substitute a memorized meaning.
- **NOT / EXCEPT questions** invert the task. Re-read each option and ask, "Is this true according to the passage?" The one false statement is the answer.

#### Connections to Adjacent NDA Topics

Strong comprehension feeds directly into **Sentence Completion / Cloze Test** and **Ordering of Sentences**, both of which also appear in the GAT English paper. The same scanning, inference, and connector-reading skills apply. Practising comprehension daily raises your overall English accuracy by 8–12% in mock tests, according to coaching-tracked data from CDS aspirants.

#### Practice Prompts

1. Read a 350-word editorial on climate policy. Without highlighting, answer four MCQs: one main idea, one tone, one inference, and one vocabulary-in-context. Time yourself at 6 minutes.
2. Take any past NDA comprehension passage, identify the qualifying words in each answer choice, and mark which choice violates the passage's actual degree (*most* vs *all*). This builds the filter examiners exploit.

#### Examiner Traps to Avoid

| Trap | How it works | Defence |
|------|-------------|---------|
| Keyword echo | Wrong answer lifts real passage words but changes meaning | Check the verb and object, not just nouns |
| Over-generalisation | Choice is true in real life but not in the passage | Ask: *does the author commit to this?* |
| Detail creep | Choice is a true minor fact on a main-idea question | Re-read the question stem: *central* vs *specific* |
| Tone mismatch | Choice describes the topic, not the author's attitude | Look for first-person cues and evaluative adjectives |
