# Writing — Junior Cycle English Notes

The Writing strand is the third integrated strand in the NCCA Junior Cycle English specification, alongside Oral Language and Reading. It assesses the creation of texts in a range of genres - personal, imaginative, discursive, argumentative, transactional, and reflective - with attention to register, audience, rhetorical purpose, and the writer's voice. The strand is tested through a sustained writing task on the SEC English written paper and through CBA 2 (The Collection of the Student's Texts), completed in third year, which requires students to submit a portfolio of texts they have composed across the three years. It carries a weight rating of 3 out of 5 because, while the CBA grade is critical for the descriptor, the marks on the written paper are weighted toward Reading. Under-preparing Writing depresses the CBA grade and weakens the ability to write sustained, controlled prose under exam conditions - the higher-mark items on the written paper require candidates to write analytically, not just descriptively.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Weight & Frequency**: Rated 3/5 - assessed through CBA 2 (The Collection of the Student's Texts) in third year and through a sustained writing task on the SEC written paper. Confirm the live CBA windows on the SEC and NCCA sites before planning.
- **Timing Goal**: CBA portfolio work is best done as a continuous habit across the three years; the sustained writing task on the paper should sit in the 30 to 45 minute band for a 400 to 600 word response.
- **Core Principle**: Master five writing habits: (1) plan before writing - five minutes of planning saves fifteen minutes of rewriting, (2) match register to genre (formal for an argumentative piece, conversational for a personal piece), (3) vary sentence length and structure, (4) use specific detail rather than generalisation, (5) close with a deliberate final sentence that links back to the opening.
- **Genre awareness**: Personal writing uses first-person reflection. Imaginative writing builds a scene with sensory detail. Discursive writing balances two perspectives on an issue. Argumentative writing takes a clear position and defends it. Transactional writing (letter, article, review) follows the conventions of the form.
- **Punctuation for control**: A semicolon joins two related independent clauses; a colon introduces an explanation or list; an em-dash creates a sharp, dramatic pause. Variety signals a confident writer.
- **Voice**: Use specific, concrete language. "The wind howled through the broken window" is more vivid than "It was windy."
- **Answer Verification**: Re-read the writing brief before submitting - have I addressed every part of the prompt? Is the register consistent? Does the closing sentence earn its place?

#### Examiner traps

- Writing a generic response that does not address the specific brief - the mark scheme rewards relevance to the prompt.
- Spending so long on the opening that the body and closing are rushed - the planning step prevents this.
- Letting one dominant perspective crowd out a balanced argument in discursive writing - both sides must be represented.
- Using informal register ("gonna", "yeah", "stuff") in a formal writing task - the register mark is forfeit.
- Leaving a CBA portfolio to the last week of third year - the NCCA brief explicitly values work developed across the cycle.

#### 20-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Can you write a sustained 400 to 600 word response in any of the four main genres (personal, imaginative, discursive, argumentative) within 40 minutes?
- [ ] Do you know the conventions of at least three transactional forms (letter to editor, article, review, speech)?
- [ ] Can you vary sentence length and structure within a single paragraph for rhythm and emphasis?
- [ ] Have you added at least one piece to your CBA portfolio in the current term, with reflection on what you learned?

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Conceptual framework and solving methodology

Writing on the SEC paper rewards a four-step scaffold that mirrors the mark scheme's focus on purpose, audience, structure, and craft. Apply this scaffold to every sustained writing task:

1. **Decode the brief and identify the genre and audience**:
   What is the prompt asking me to write? Who is the implied reader? What register is appropriate? State these in your plan: "I am writing a personal reflective piece addressed to a sympathetic adult reader, in a conversational but controlled register."

2. **Plan the structure before writing**:
   Open with a hook (a specific image, a question, a brief anecdote). Lay out two or three main paragraphs, each with a clear point and supporting detail. Close with a deliberate final sentence that links back to the opening. For discursive writing, plan the balance: one paragraph for perspective A, one for perspective B, one for your own synthesis.

3. **Execute with deliberate craft**:
   Vary sentence length. Use specific, concrete language. Embed sensory detail in imaginative writing; embed evidence and examples in argumentative writing; embed reflection in personal writing. Check the register against the genre.

4. **Verify and refine**:
   Re-read your draft against the brief. Have you addressed every part of the prompt? Is the structure clear? Are there any spelling or punctuation errors that would cost marks in a controlled-conditions assessment? Make targeted edits - do not rewrite from scratch.

#### Detailed worked example

**Problem Context**:
A discursive writing task typical of the Ordinary or Higher level paper.

**Item Prompt**:
Write a discursive piece on the following topic: "Social media does more harm than good." Develop your response in 400 to 600 words. Support your argument with reference to one or more relevant examples from your own experience or current events.

**Step-by-Step Solution**:
- **Step 1 (Decode)**: Genre = discursive (balanced argument). Audience = SEC examiner reading a sustained, controlled response. Register = formal but not stiff. Length = 400 to 600 words. Required = reference to relevant examples.
- **Step 2 (Plan)**:
  - Opening: a hook that signals the debate (a recent news story about online harms, or a personal observation about how social media has changed a peer's behaviour).
  - Paragraph 1: the strongest argument on the "harm" side (mental health impacts, particularly on adolescents; spread of misinformation; cyberbullying).
  - Paragraph 2: the strongest argument on the "good" side (connection across distance; raising voices for marginalised groups; rapid response in emergencies).
  - Paragraph 3: a synthesis - social media is a tool whose effects depend on how it is used, and the harms are real but not inherent; regulation and digital literacy matter more than blanket acceptance or rejection.
  - Closing: a deliberate final sentence that links back to the opening - "Whether social media does more harm than good depends less on the technology itself than on the choices we make about how we use it."
- **Step 3 (Draft with deliberate craft)**:
  - Open with a specific example: "When the young people in my community organised a clean-up of the local park through a single WhatsApp group, social media felt like a clear force for good."
  - Move to the "harm" paragraph: cite the research linking heavy social media use to anxiety and depression in adolescents; mention the spread of misinformation during a recent election; the cyberbullying that has driven some young people to change schools.
  - Move to the "good" paragraph: cite the rapid coordination of mutual aid during a recent weather event; the platform for marginalised voices; the maintenance of cross-border friendships.
  - Synthesise: the technology is neutral; the design choices (algorithmic amplification of outrage, infinite scroll) and the user choices (passive consumption vs active creation) determine the effect.
  - Close with the deliberate sentence linking to the opening.
- **Step 4 (Verify)**: 400 to 600 words (count: approximately 480 in the draft). Register consistent (formal but not stiff). Both sides represented. Synthesis paragraph present. Specific examples included. Closing sentence links back to the opening.
- **Conclusion**: The response meets all five of the SEC mark scheme's primary criteria - purpose, audience, structure, craft, and relevance to the brief.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Advanced variations and high-difficulty edge cases

At Distinction level, Writing rewards a distinctive voice and confident handling of complex briefs. Practice the four highest-leverage patterns:

- **Transactional forms with rhetorical sophistication**: A letter to the editor that opens with a hook, names the issue in the second paragraph, marshals evidence in the third, addresses counter-arguments in the fourth, and closes with a call to action - this structure signals a confident writer.
- **Reflective writing with metacognition**: The CBA portfolio brief values reflection on what the student learned from composing each piece. Strong reflection names a specific craft choice ("I chose to open with a single sentence paragraph to create a slow, deliberate pace"), explains the intention, and evaluates the outcome.
- **Imaginative writing with controlled ambiguity**: A short story that ends on an image or a question rather than a neat resolution signals sophistication. The opening image should echo in the closing image to create thematic unity.
- **Argumentative writing with concession and rebuttal**: The strongest argumentative pieces acknowledge the strongest counter-argument, concede its partial validity, then rebut it. "While it is true that... this argument overlooks... and fails to account for..." is the high-mark sentence pattern.

#### Systematic drill schedule

A four-week Writing-strand pass before the CBA submission window and the written paper:

- **Week 1**: Daily sustained writing - one 400 to 600 word piece per day across a different genre each day (Monday personal, Tuesday imaginative, Wednesday discursive, Thursday argumentative, Friday transactional). Time each to 40 minutes.
- **Week 2**: CBA portfolio development - compose or revise at least one portfolio piece per day, with a 100-word reflection on what was learned. Build a habit of reflection rather than leaving it to the submission window.
- **Week 3**: Register and craft practice - take a single piece of writing and rewrite it in three different registers (formal, conversational, persuasive). Notice the vocabulary, sentence structure, and tone shifts required.
- **Week 4**: Past SEC specimen papers on the Writing strand. Mark strictly against the rubric on examinations.ie and identify which marks are for purpose/audience, which are for structure, and which are for craft.
