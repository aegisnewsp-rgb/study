# Elasticity

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Elasticity** measures the responsiveness of one economic variable to a change in another, expressed as a ratio of percentage changes. The four core measures for TNPSC are **Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)**, **Income Elasticity of Demand (YED)**, **Cross Elasticity of Demand (XED)**, and **Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)**.

- **PED** = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP; values > 1 elastic, < 1 inelastic, = 1 unit elastic, = 0 perfectly inelastic, = ∞ perfectly elastic.
- **YED** classifies normal (>0), inferior (<0), luxury (>1), and necessity (0–1) goods.
- **XED** sign tells substitutes (+) from complements (−).
- **Total Revenue Test**: revenue rises when price falls if PED > 1, falls if PED < 1, and is maximised when PED = 1.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Formulas

| Measure | Formula | Variable Notes |
|---|---|---|
| PED | (ΔQ/Q) ÷ (ΔP/P) | Q = quantity demanded, P = price |
| YED | (ΔQ/Q) ÷ (ΔY/Y) | Y = consumer income |
| XED | (ΔQ<sub>A</sub>/Q<sub>A</sub>) ÷ (ΔP<sub>B</sub>/P<sub>B</sub>) | Measures interdependence between goods A and B |
| PES | (ΔQs/Qs) ÷ (ΔP/P) | Qs = quantity supplied |
| Point Elasticity | (dQ/dP) × (P/Q) | Infinitesimal change at a point on the curve |
| Arc Elasticity | [(Q₂−Q₁)/((Q₂+Q₁)/2)] ÷ [(P₂−P₁)/((P₂+P₁)/2)] | Used for discrete, finite changes |

#### Degrees of Elasticity

Five standard cases govern every PED MCQ: **perfectly elastic** (horizontal curve, E = ∞, any tiny price rise wipes out demand), **perfectly inelastic** (vertical curve, E = 0, as with insulin for a diabetic), **unit elastic** (E = 1, revenue at its peak), **elastic** (E > 1, quantity responds more than price), and **inelastic** (E < 1, quantity responds less than price).

#### Determinants

**PED determinants** — availability of close substitutes (more substitutes ⇒ more elastic), proportion of income the good absorbs (salt vs housing), necessity-versus-luxury status, the time horizon (longer ⇒ more elastic), and how narrowly the market is defined. **PES determinants** include spare capacity, stock of raw materials, storability, factor mobility, and crucially the time period: supply is most inelastic in the very short run (market period) and most elastic in the long run.

#### Total Revenue Test

Link price changes to revenue direction using the rule **TR = P × Q**:

- If PED > 1 (elastic): price and revenue move in **opposite** directions.
- If PED < 1 (inelastic): price and revenue move in the **same** direction.
- If PED = 1: revenue is at its **maximum** for that demand curve.

This test is the single most-tested application of elasticity in TNPSC descriptive and interview rounds, especially when discussing fuel pricing or agricultural MSP policy.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and the Slope–Elasticity Trap

Elasticity is **not** the same as slope. A steep demand curve can still be elastic, and a flat one inelastic, because slope depends on the units of P and Q. Always compute the ratio (ΔQ/Q)÷(ΔP/P), never assume visual steepness equals inelasticity.

#### Sign Conventions Examined

PED is conventionally reported as a **positive** number because the demand curve's downward slope gives a negative ratio; examiners ignore the sign. **YED and XED retain their signs**, which carry economic meaning: a negative YED flags an inferior good (e.g., coarse cereals when income rises), while a positive XED confirms substitutes (tea and coffee).

#### Point vs Arc Elasticity

Use **point elasticity** when the change is infinitesimally small or when the exact point on the curve is given. Use **arc elasticity** for discrete between two observed price–quantity pairs, and remember to divide by the **average** (Q₂+Q₁)/2, not the original Q₁. Using Q₁ in the denominator produces asymmetric values when price rises versus falls — a frequent MCQ trap.

#### Policy Applications

Tax incidence falls more heavily on the **more inelastic** side of the market, which is why fuel taxes in India are politically viable but food-grain taxes are not. Under oligopoly, the **kinked demand curve** assumes elastic demand above the kink (rivals will not match price rises) and inelastic demand below it (rivals will match price cuts), creating sticky prices. The marginal-revenue relation **MR = P(1 − 1/|E|)** is the formal link examiners love: setting MR = MC and substituting gives the profit-maximising price once elasticity is known.

#### Practice Prompts

1. When the government raises the petrol excise by ₹2 per litre and revenue collection rises, what does this imply about PED of petrol in the short run? Justify using the total revenue test.
2. Two goods show XED of +0.6 and −0.8 respectively. Classify each and state one Indian-market example for each category.
