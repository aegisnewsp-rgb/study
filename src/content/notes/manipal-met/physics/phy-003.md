### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

**Newton's three laws.**
- First law (inertia): a body at rest stays at rest, a body in uniform motion stays in uniform motion, unless acted on by a net external force. Defines inertial frames.
- Second law: $\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = m\vec{a}$ — net force equals mass times acceleration. The acceleration is in the direction of the net force.
- Third law: for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. The action-reaction pair acts on different bodies — never subtract them when summing forces on a single body.

**Momentum and impulse.** Momentum $\vec{p} = m\vec{v}$. Impulse $\vec{J} = \vec{F}\Delta t = \Delta \vec{p}$. For variable force: $\vec{J} = \int \vec{F}\, dt$.

**Conservation of linear momentum.** When $\vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = 0$ on a system, total momentum is conserved. Applies to isolated systems and to systems where the time scale is short enough that external impulses are negligible (collisions). Two-body result for an isolated collision: $m_1 \vec{v}_1 + m_2 \vec{v}_2 = m_1 \vec{v}_1' + m_2 \vec{v}_2'$.

**Equilibrium of concurrent forces.** A body is in equilibrium when the net force on it is zero. Three forces in equilibrium must satisfy Lami's theorem: $\dfrac{F_1}{\sin\alpha_1} = \dfrac{F_2}{\sin\alpha_2} = \dfrac{F_3}{\sin\alpha_3}$ where $\alpha_i$ is the angle opposite the $i$-th force.

**Friction.** Static friction adjusts up to a maximum $f_s^{\max} = \mu_s N$, where $\mu_s$ is the coefficient of static friction and $N$ is the normal force. Once sliding starts, kinetic friction takes over: $f_k = \mu_k N$ with $\mu_k < \mu_s$ typically. Rolling friction is much smaller than sliding friction.

**Banking of roads.** For a vehicle moving on a circular banked road of angle $\theta$ and radius $r$ at the design speed $v_0$, no friction is needed: $\tan\theta = \dfrac{v_0^2}{rg}$. With friction, the safe-speed range expands to $\dfrac{r g (\tan\theta - \mu_s)}{1 + \mu_s \tan\theta} \le v^2 \le \dfrac{r g (\tan\theta + \mu_s)}{1 - \mu_s \tan\theta}$.

### 🟡 Standard — Exam Prep (3d-3w)

**Worked Example 1 — Atwood machine with frictionless pulley.**
Two masses $m_1 = 5\,\text{kg}$ and $m_2 = 3\,\text{kg}$ hang from a light inextensible string over a frictionless pulley. Find the acceleration and the string tension.
Take $m_1$ heavier, so it descends. The system accelerates with $a = \dfrac{(m_1 - m_2)g}{m_1 + m_2} = \dfrac{2 \cdot 9.8}{8} = 2.45\,\text{m/s}^2$. Tension: $T = m_2(g + a) = 3 \cdot (9.8 + 2.45) = 36.75\,\text{N}$. Check on $m_1$: $m_1 g - T = 5 \cdot 9.8 - 36.75 = 49 - 36.75 = 12.25\,\text{N} = m_1 a = 5 \cdot 2.45 = 12.25\,\text{N}$. Consistent.

**Worked Example 2 — Block on incline with kinetic friction.**
A $4\,\text{kg}$ block rests on a $30^\circ$ incline. The coefficient of kinetic friction is $\mu_k = 0.25$. A horizontal force $P = 30\,\text{N}$ pushes the block up the incline. Find the acceleration.
Resolve along the incline (positive up-slope) and perpendicular to it. With the incline surface tilted at $30^\circ$, the horizontal force $P$ has components $P\cos 30^\circ$ along the slope (up) and $P\sin 30^\circ$ perpendicular (into the slope). Normal force: $N = mg\cos 30^\circ + P\sin 30^\circ = 4 \cdot 9.8 \cdot 0.866 + 30 \cdot 0.5 = 33.95 + 15 = 48.95\,\text{N}$. Kinetic friction opposes motion, so along-slope: $f_k = -\mu_k N = -0.25 \cdot 48.95 = -12.24\,\text{N}$. Net force along slope: $F_{\text{net}} = P\cos 30^\circ - mg\sin 30^\circ + f_k = 25.98 - 19.6 - 12.24 = -5.86\,\text{N}$. The negative sign means the block actually accelerates down-slope: $a = F_{\text{net}}/m = -5.86/4 = -1.47\,\text{m/s}^2$, i.e. $1.47\,\text{m/s}^2$ down the incline. The horizontal push was insufficient to overcome friction plus the gravitational component.

**Worked Example 3 — Banked road with friction.**
A circular road of radius $50\,\text{m}$ is banked at $15^\circ$. The coefficient of friction between tyres and road is $\mu_s = 0.3$. Find the maximum safe speed.
Maximum safe speed formula: $v_{\max}^2 = \dfrac{r g (\tan\theta + \mu_s)}{1 - \mu_s \tan\theta}$. Compute $\tan 15^\circ = 0.268$. Numerator: $50 \cdot 9.8 \cdot (0.268 + 0.3) = 50 \cdot 9.8 \cdot 0.568 = 278.3$. Denominator: $1 - 0.3 \cdot 0.268 = 0.920$. So $v_{\max}^2 = 278.3/0.920 = 302.5$, $v_{\max} \approx 17.4\,\text{m/s}$ (about $62.6\,\text{km/h}$).

**Practice Set.**
1. A $2\,\text{kg}$ ball moving at $6\,\text{m/s}$ collides head-on with a stationary $4\,\text{kg}$ ball and they stick together. Find the common velocity. ($v = 2\,\text{m/s}$, momentum conserved.)
2. A $1000\,\text{kg}$ car rounds a flat circular track of radius $100\,\text{m}$ at $20\,\text{m/s}$. Find the required centripetal force. ($F = mv^2/r = 1000 \cdot 400/100 = 4000\,\text{N}$.)
3. A horizontal force of $20\,\text{N}$ just starts to move a $5\,\text{kg}$ block on a flat surface. Find $\mu_s$. ($\mu_s = F/N = 20/(5 \cdot 9.8) \approx 0.408$.)

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (1mo+)

**Pseudo forces in non-inertial frames.** In an accelerating reference frame (lift, car, rotating turntable), Newton's second law holds only after adding a pseudo force $-m\vec{a}_{\text{frame}}$. For a car braking hard, passengers feel thrown forward — the pseudo force points opposite to the car's deceleration. In a rotating frame, the centrifugal pseudo force $m\omega^2 r$ acts outward, balanced by real forces (string tension, normal force) for circular motion. In a MET NAT, "the apparent weight in a lift accelerating upward at $a$" is $m(g + a)$, not $mg$.

**Constraint relations.** Connected bodies (Atwood, blocks-pulled-by-string-on-incline, wedge systems) require constraint equations to relate accelerations. A common case: a block on a frictionless wedge of angle $\theta$ slides while the wedge can move horizontally. Conservation of horizontal momentum plus constraint $|\vec{a}_{\text{block, horiz}}| = |\vec{a}_{\text{wedge, horiz}}|$ gives the answer. The MET frequently uses these in the form of a fixed string over a pulley, where the string length is constant so the magnitudes of acceleration of the two ends are equal.

**Friction angle and the third-law subtlety.** The friction angle $\phi = \arctan \mu$ is the angle the contact force makes with the normal. Three concurrent forces in equilibrium, one of which is a contact force on a surface with friction, must lie within the friction cone or the body will slip. A common student error is to use Newton's third law to "transfer" the contact force back to the body without flipping its direction. The action-reaction pair acts on different bodies; the third law doesn't change the analysis of one body — it adds a reaction on a different body.

**Why $f = ma$ for variable mass.** A rocket ejects mass at high velocity; its mass decreases with time. The correct equation is $m\dfrac{dv}{dt} = F_{\text{ext}} + v_{\text{rel}}\dfrac{dm}{dt}$ where $v_{\text{rel}}$ is the velocity of the ejected mass relative to the rocket (negative for ejection backwards, positive thrust). The Tsiolkovsky rocket equation integrates this for thrust in free space. MET questions sometimes reduce to $v = v_0 + v_{\text{ex}} \ln(m_0/m)$ — the classic rocket-velocity formula.

**Circular-motion energy.** A particle moving in a vertical circle of radius $r$ at speed $v$ has minimum-velocity condition at the top: $v_{\min}^2 = gr$ (gravity alone supplies centripetal force). At any other point, energy conservation relates speed and height: $\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + mgh = \text{const}$. A common NAT asks for the tension at the lowest point — combine centripetal force $T - mg = mv^2/r$ with the energy equation.

## Common traps

1. **Adding Newton's third-law pair to one body's free-body diagram.** The forces on a single body sum to $m\vec{a}$ — they include gravity, normal, friction, applied force, and tension. The reaction from the body onto something else never appears on this body's FBD. Sketching both action and reaction on the same diagram double-counts.
2. **Using $\mu_s$ when the block is sliding, and $\mu_k$ when it is at rest.** The static coefficient applies up to the maximum just before motion; once the block slides, kinetic friction takes over. The MET almost always states which phase the block is in.
3. **Mis-resolving forces on an incline.** Gravity $mg$ points straight down, not along the slope. Along-slope component is $mg\sin\theta$; perpendicular component is $mg\cos\theta$. Using $mg\sin\theta$ as the perpendicular force flips signs and gives nonsensical answers.
4. **Forgetting that pseudo forces only exist in the non-inertial frame.** "A ball on a smooth table in a train accelerating at $a$" — in the lab frame, only the real horizontal push (a string tension, say) accelerates the ball; in the train frame, add $-ma$ as a pseudo force. Mixing the two frames breaks the second law.
5. **Sign confusion in momentum problems.** When two bodies collide and one reverses direction, the post-collision velocity is negative along the chosen axis. Students often write the magnitude only and miss the sign convention when computing common velocity after a sticking collision.
