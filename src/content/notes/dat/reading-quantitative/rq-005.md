# Pacing Across a Five-Hour Examination

The ADA states plainly that the Dental Admission Test "total administration time is five hours and 15 minutes". Four sections share it: Survey of the Natural Sciences (100 items, 90 minutes), Perceptual Ability Test (90 items, 60 minutes), a scheduled 30-minute break, Reading Comprehension (50 items, 60 minutes) and Quantitative Reasoning (40 items, 45 minutes), with an optional 15-minute tutorial at the start and an optional post-test survey at the end.

That schedule tells you something no amount of content revision will. Reading Comprehension and Quantitative Reasoning come after the break, at the three-hour-fifteen-minute mark, and they are the two sections a candidate is most prepared for and least able to perform well on. Fatigue, not ignorance, is what caps the score for a substantial share of candidates.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

**The five-hour fifteen-minute day is not four equal sections.** Dividing each section's minutes by its item count gives the real rate, and two of the four sections are *slower* than one item a minute — the two that come last.

| Section | Items | Minutes | Seconds per item | Position |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Survey of the Natural Sciences | 100 | 90 | **54** | first |
| Perceptual Ability | 90 | 60 | **40** | second |
| Reading Comprehension | 50 | 60 | **72** | after the break |
| Quantitative Reasoning | 40 | 45 | **67.5** | last |
| **Whole paper** | **280** | **255** | **54.6** | — |

**Memory hook — SET the cut-off before you SIT it.** S-set the per-item cut-off in advance, E-estimate is not calculate on items where estimation settles it, T-take the break for food and water only. The cut-off must be written down before the day, because it is a decision about your future self made while your past self is tired.

**The three facts that matter most:**

1. 255 of the 315 minutes are item time; the rest is tutorial, break and survey.
2. Reading Comprehension is the slowest section at 72 seconds an item — slower than Quantitative Reasoning.
3. Perceptual Ability is the fastest at 40 seconds, and it is the section most likely to blow a single item to three minutes.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

### The arithmetic of the day

Per section the budget works out differently in each case, and the spread is wider than most candidates assume. Natural Sciences allows 54 seconds an item and Perceptual Ability 40. Reading Comprehension allows 72 seconds and Quantitative Reasoning 67.5 — the two slowest sections are the two that come after the break, and the overall average of 54.6 seconds hides that completely.

The break is not free time and it is not a reward for surviving the first half. It falls at the 3:15 mark, and everything the candidate does with those thirty minutes decides whether the prose that follows is read or merely survived. Perceptual Ability has items where a candidate will spend three minutes on a keyhole problem, and Reading Comprehension has passages long enough that a single question can absorb a minute. Both eat into the sections that follow.

So make the pacing decision in advance, not in the moment. Decide the per-section cut-off now, write it next to the section name, and hold to it. A section left with six unanswered items because you ran out of time is a worse outcome than the same section with six items answered in the first half of the allotted minutes and guessed quickly at the end.

### The five hours fifteen minutes, itemised

The headline figure is not five hours of testing.

| Component | Minutes | Cumulative |
|---|---|---|
| Optional tutorial | 15 | 0:15 |
| Survey of the Natural Sciences | 90 | 1:45 |
| Perceptual Ability Test | 60 | 2:45 |
| Scheduled break | 30 | 3:15 |
| Reading Comprehension | 60 | 4:15 |
| Quantitative Reasoning | 45 | **5:00** |
| Optional post-test survey | ~15 | 5:15 |

Item time is 90 + 60 + 60 + 45 = **255 minutes**, not 315. The hour that is not testing is the hour that decides whether you reach Quantitative Reasoning alert. Note where Reading Comprehension begins: at 3:15 elapsed, not at four-and-a-half hours — the tutorial, the Natural Sciences section and Perceptual Ability together are two and three quarter hours, and the break is the last thing between you and prose.

### Treat the break as part of the plan

The 30-minute break falls after Perceptual Ability and before Reading Comprehension.

Reset attention. If the final Perceptual Ability block was grinding, the first two Reading passages will be read badly, and that cost is larger than the last few Perceptual Ability items would have been worth. Decide in advance that the break is spent on food and water and nothing else.

Eat and hydrate. The advice is banal and it is also worth marks, because a blood-sugar drop in hour four costs more than an extra thirty minutes of revision would have bought.

### Section order, since you cannot change it

You cannot reorder the sections, so what you control is what you bring into each one.

Perceptual Ability is the section that most often breaks a candidate's rhythm. Whatever concentration is left after the Natural Sciences arrives at Perceptual Ability, and whatever remains after that arrives at Reading. Practise the whole sequence rather than the sections individually, because the transition between a spatial section and a prose section is a real cost.

Reading Comprehension is where preparation has the least effect on pace. You cannot read faster than you read. What you can do is practise abandoning a passage that is not yielding, because that decision costs five seconds and the alternative costs a whole passage.

Quantitative Reasoning is last and the shortest. It is also where a tired candidate makes unit errors, so protect it hardest — by not over-investing in Natural Sciences.

### Rehearse the length

Two full-length sittings is the minimum, and they should be back to back so the second is taken on fatigue rather than on preparation. Nothing else replicates what the fifth hour feels like.

Sit them in one block with no pause beyond the scheduled break, because a break you take when the test does not have one is one you will not get back. The rehearsal exists to find out which section you would abandon at hour four, while it is still cheap to change your mind.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

### Where the time actually goes wrong

Take a candidate who averages 70 seconds an item — a respectable rate, and faster than the Reading Comprehension budget.

| Section | Budget | Time at 70 s/item | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Natural Sciences | 90 min | 100 items × 70 s = 117 min | **27 min over** — 23 items unanswered |
| Perceptual Ability | 60 min | 90 × 70 = 105 min | **45 min over** — 38 items unanswered |
| Reading Comprehension | 60 min | 50 × 70 = 58 min | 2 min under budget |
| Quantitative Reasoning | 45 min | 40 × 70 = 47 min | 2 min over |

The arithmetic is the argument for rehearsing the *sequence*. A 70-second pace is fine for Reading and Quant and catastrophic for the two sections before them. Perceptual Ability alone overruns by 45 minutes, which is more than the entire scheduled break. The break is not a luxury; it is the only mechanism the exam provides for absorbing exactly this overrun.

### Worked item — the pacing decision

**Situation.** You are 40 minutes into Perceptual Ability with 50 items still to go before the break.

**Step 1 — compute the real rate.** 50 items in the remaining 20 minutes is 24 seconds each. The section average is 40 seconds, so this requires a 40% speed-up.

**Step 2 — apply the keyhole method.** Volume count first, coordinate comparison second. On a keyhole item that is roughly 15 seconds if the count eliminates the option and 30–40 seconds if it does not.

**Step 3 — decide with the arithmetic, not with nerve.** If more than about a third of the remaining items are keyhole items whose counts *match*, 24 seconds is unreachable. The correct decision is to attempt the volume count on every item, and to mark-and-move on any item that survives the count with under 15 seconds left.

> ⚡ **Examiner Trap.** The trap in a pacing situation is treating an unanswered item as a neutral event. It is a certain zero, and a certain zero cannot be converted into a probable one by spending ninety seconds on it. Two keyhole items carried to three minutes each cost six minutes — which is nine further items at the section's own 40-second rate. The conversion rate is fixed and it is brutal: every minute lost on one item is worth one and a half items elsewhere.

### The cut-off table

Write this before the exam day and hold to it under fatigue.

| Section | Minutes | Items | Cut-off behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|
| Natural Sciences | 90 | 100 | Hard single concept → flag, return if time remains |
| Perceptual Ability | 60 | 90 | Hard keyhole after count → mark and move, never re-visit |
| Reading Comprehension | 60 | 50 | Unproductive passage → abandon after one full read |
| Quantitative Reasoning | 45 | 40 | Estimation possible → select and move, never recompute |

The pattern: the two sections where items are *self-contained* (Perceptual Ability, Quantitative Reasoning) should be abandoned hardest, because a partially-solved item there scores nothing. The two where items sit inside a shared passage or a shared topic (Reading, Natural Sciences) can absorb a return visit, because the context is already loaded.

### Fuel and the fourth hour

The scheduled break sits at the 3:15 mark. That is the point at which most candidates are least prepared for it, because the session still feels like it has half to run.

- **Eat.** The last meal before Perceptual Ability is typically three or more hours earlier. The drop lands in Reading Comprehension, where reading slows before accuracy visibly falls.
- **Hydrate.** Dehydration presents as fatigue and is usually misread as a concentration problem.
- **Do not revise.** The break is 30 minutes and no topic survives a useful review in it. The candidates who try lose the recovery they were supposed to take.
- **Do not attempt to bank Perceptual Ability time.** Finishing early does not buy extra minutes for Reading; the clock is per section.

### Booking, because an unbooked test is a lost test

The ADA recommends contacting Prometric at least 60 to 90 days before your desired test date, because schedules fill up. This is the only item on the page that costs nothing and cannot be fixed on the day: a candidate who books late takes a date far enough away to be a different preparation plan.

### ⚡ Quick Exam Checklist

- Per-section cut-offs written down **before** the exam day
- Perceptual Ability treated as the overrun risk — 40 s/item, not 70
- Keyhole method: count, then coordinates; never three minutes on one item
- Unanswered items marked, not left in place
- Break used for food, water and nothing else
- No revision attempted in the 30-minute break
- Two full-length sittings rehearsed back to back, whole sequence, no extra pauses
- Test date booked 60–90 days ahead
- Whole sequence practised, not isolated sections
