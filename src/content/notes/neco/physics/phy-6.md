# Heat Energy and Temperature Measurement

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Heat is energy in transit between bodies at different temperatures, measured in joules (J). Temperature is the degree of hotness or coldness of a body, read off a thermometer on the Celsius, Kelvin or Fahrenheit scale. The two are not interchangeable: heat is energy, temperature is a state property.

Two formulas carry most NECO SSCE marks in this topic. **Specific heat capacity** uses $Q = mc\Delta\theta$, where $Q$ is the heat absorbed in joules, $m$ is the mass in kg, $c$ is the specific heat capacity in J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, and $\Delta\theta$ is the temperature change in K or °C. **Latent heat** uses $Q = mL$, where $L$ is the specific latent heat of fusion or vaporisation in J kg⁻¹.

- The Celsius, Kelvin and Fahrenheit scales convert as $T_K = \theta_C + 273$, $\theta_F = \tfrac{9}{5}\theta_C + 32$.
- Ice point (0 °C) and steam point (100 °C) are the fixed points used to calibrate liquid-in-glass thermometers.
- Water has its maximum density at 4 °C — it contracts from 4 °C down to 0 °C, the anomaly behind lake turnover.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Heat vs temperature
Heat is energy that flows because of a temperature difference; once absorbed it becomes part of the body's internal energy. Temperature tells you which way heat will flow on contact: from the hotter body (higher temperature) to the colder one (lower temperature) until thermal equilibrium is reached.

#### Thermometers and thermometric properties
A thermometer uses a property that changes measurably with temperature — length of a liquid column, resistance of a metal wire, or e.m.f. of a thermocouple. Calibration uses the ice point and steam point of water as the lower and upper fixed points.

#### Specific heat capacity
The heat needed to raise 1 kg of a substance by 1 K. Water has $c \approx 4200$ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, much higher than most metals. The **method of mixtures** determines unknown $c$: a hot solid is dropped into cold water in a calorimeter, and $\theta$ is balanced so that heat lost by the solid equals heat gained by the water and calorimeter, with heat losses to surroundings taken as negligible (or corrected by a cooling-correction term).

#### Latent heat
Heat absorbed or released during a change of state with no temperature change. Latent heat of **fusion** ($L_f$) occurs at the melting point; latent heat of **vaporisation** ($L_v$) occurs at boiling point. $L_v$ for water is ~2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹, far larger than $L_f$ (3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹), because vaporisation fully breaks intermolecular bonds.

| Concept | Key point |
| --- | --- |
| Heat (Q) | Energy in transit, measured in J |
| Temperature (θ or T) | Degree of hotness, measured in K or °C |
| Specific heat capacity (c) | J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹; for water ≈ 4200 |
| Specific latent heat (L) | J kg⁻¹; differs for fusion vs vaporisation |
| Ice / steam point | 0 °C and 100 °C — calibration fixed points |

- Mercury thermometers: range about −39 °C to 357 °C, opaque, do not wet glass, used as the laboratory standard.
- Alcohol thermometers: range about −115 °C to 78 °C, cheaper, used where mercury would freeze.
- Conduction, convection and radiation are the three modes of heat transfer; metals conduct well, still air and vacuum insulate.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Expansivity and the water anomaly
Linear expansivity is $\alpha = \Delta L / (L_0 \Delta\theta)$, in K⁻¹. For an isotropic solid, the area and volume (cubical) expansivities are $\beta \approx 2\alpha$ and $\gamma \approx 3\alpha$. Unlike most substances, water **contracts** when heated from 0 °C to 4 °C and only expands above 4 °C, so it has maximum density at 4 °C. This keeps ice floating and lets deep lakes stay liquid under a frozen surface — a fact NECO sometimes tests as a comprehension item.

#### Edge cases and exam traps
A common NECO trap mixes °C and K inside gas-law or specific-heat equations. Temperature *differences* are numerically equal in K and °C, but an absolute temperature on the Kelvin scale must use $T_K = \theta_C + 273$. Another trap is the sign on the conversion: the correct relation is $\theta_F = \tfrac{9}{5}\theta_C + 32$, never $\theta_F = \tfrac{5}{9}\theta_C + 32$.

In the method of mixtures, neglect of heat absorbed by the calorimeter (heat capacity of the calorimeter, $W$) and heat lost to the surroundings accounts for most systematic error. A cleaner balance equation is:

$$m_1 c_1 (\theta_1 - \theta_2) = (m_2 c_2 + W)(\theta_2 - \theta_3)$$

where subscripts 1, 2, 3 refer to the hot solid, the cold water, and the initial water temperature, and $\theta_2$ is the final equilibrium temperature.

#### Practice prompts
1. A 0.5 kg block of aluminium at 200 °C is dropped into 1.0 kg of water at 20 °C in a copper calorimeter of heat capacity 800 J K⁻¹. Take $c_{Al} = 900$ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ and $c_w = 4200$ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Find the final temperature.
2. How much heat is required to convert 2.0 kg of ice at 0 °C completely to steam at 100 °C? (Use $L_f = 3.34 \times 10^5$ J kg⁻¹, $L_v = 2.26 \times 10^6$ J kg⁻¹, $c_w = 4200$ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.)

| Latent heat of fusion | L_f | 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹ |
| Latent heat of vaporisation | L_v | 2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹ |
| Temperature of maximum density | θ | 4 °C |
