# Microorganisms and Nutrient Cycling — Leaving Certificate (Established) Biology Notes

Unit 3.2 is the unit where the material most often feels like two unrelated halves — microorganisms, and biogeochemical cycles — bolted together. They are in fact one idea. **Decomposers close the loop.** Dead matter and waste are broken down by bacteria and fungi, the mineral nutrients released are taken back up by plants, and carbon and nitrogen are returned to the atmosphere. Without microbial decomposition the nutrient cycles in unit 3.1's food webs simply stop, and matter would move one way only. Learn that sentence and the two halves hold together.

The specification is specific about what to be able to do: distinguish bacteria from fungi on structure, nutrition and cellular nature; investigate factors affecting microbial growth; discuss industrial importance; outline microbiomes; model the carbon and nitrogen cycles; and link atmospheric CO₂ and methane to climate change with an evaluation of strategies to reduce them. That final evaluation is the highest-demand item in the unit and the one students skip.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Bacteria vs fungi — the three axes the specification names.**

| Feature | Bacteria | Fungi (e.g. *Rhizopus*, bread mould) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cellular nature | **Prokaryotic** — no membrane-bound nucleus | **Eukaryotic** — has a membrane-bound nucleus |
| Cell wall | Peptidoglycan | Chitin (and glucans), never peptidoglycan |
| Structure | Single cells, may form chains or colonies | Hyphae growing into a branching **mycelium**; spore-bearing **sporangia** |
| Nutrition | Heterotrophic, autotrophic (photosynthetic bacteria, cyanobacteria) or **saprophytic** | **Saprophytic** on dead organic matter, or parasitic; never autotrophic |
| Reproduction | **Binary fission** — no spindle, no stage | Sexual spores and/or asexual **spore dispersal** from sporangia |
| Resistance | Some form endospores surviving harsh conditions | Walls and spores tolerate desiccation |

- **Nutrition types.** *Autotrophic* — makes its own food. *Heterotrophic* — obtains food from others. *Saprophytic* — feeds on dead organic matter, decomposing it. *Parasitic* — feeds on a living host to which it is harmful.
- **Binary fission** — the bacterium replicates its circular chromosome, the membrane and cell wall grow inward, and the cell splits into two genetically near-identical daughter cells. Under ideal conditions this can happen every 20 minutes, so a single cell could theoretically give rise to a very large population overnight. **Worked question:** starting from one cell dividing every 20 minutes, how many cells after 5 hours (15 divisions)? *Answer: 2¹⁵ = 32,768 cells.* This arithmetic is standard in examinations.
- **Factors affecting growth of microorganisms** (specification list): **pH, nutrients, water, external solute concentrations, temperature, presence of antibacterial or antifungal chemicals.** Note that high external solute concentration or low water availability **plasmolyses** the cell — the same osmosis mechanism as unit 2.6.
- **Growth curves have four phases.** **Lag** — no division; cells adapt to the new medium and synthesise the enzymes they need. **Log (exponential)** — constant maximum rate of division. **Stationary** — nutrients exhausted and waste accumulates, so the birth rate equals the death rate and the total viable count plateaus. **Decline (death)** — viable numbers fall as conditions become hostile.
- **Industrial uses of microorganisms** the specification names: **pharmaceutical** (insulin, antibiotics, vaccines), **agricultural** (animal feed supplements, biofertilisers), **medical** (diagnostics, e.g. PCR enzymes), **food production** (bread, yoghurt, cheese, soy sauce, ethanol). Knowledge of the growth curve is used to **maximise yields**: harvest during late log or stationary phase, because harvesting in decline loses product.
- **Microbiome** — the community of microorganisms living in a particular environment. In the **gastrointestinal tract** microbiomes contribute to **digestion**, to **immune signalling**, and to **metabolic health** (synthesising vitamins, fermenting otherwise indigestible fibre). In **soils**, microbiomes cycle multiple nutrients and respond to climate warming, which alters their functioning and hence nutrient availability to plants.
- **The carbon cycle**: carbon is fixed by **photosynthesis** into glucose and then biomass, moved through food webs, released again by **respiration**, returned to soil by **decomposition** (decomposers respire dead matter), stored long-term in **carbon sinks** (plants, algae, phytoplankton, **bogs**, peat, forests, marine sediments, fossil fuels), and released on a geological timescale by **fossil fuel combustion**. Photosynthesis is a **carbon sink**; respiration and combustion are carbon sources. The system is currently out of balance because humans are adding carbon faster than sinks can take it up.
- **The nitrogen cycle**: **nitrogen fixation** converts atmospheric N₂ into ammonia/ammonium (by nitrogen-fixing bacteria in legume root nodules and free-living forms); **nitrification** converts ammonium to nitrite and then to nitrate (by nitrifying bacteria); **assimilation** is plants taking up nitrate to build amino acids and proteins; **decomposition** returns organic nitrogen to ammonium; **denitrification** converts nitrate back to N₂, returning it to the atmosphere. A note: the specification says **names of microorganisms are not required** for these cycles — describe the processes.
- **Climate link**: atmospheric **CO₂ and methane (CH₄)** are greenhouse gases. Biogenic strategies to reduce atmospheric levels include reducing ruminant numbers and improving manure management (reducing CH₄), reducing fossil fuel use, afforestation and peatland restoration (increasing carbon sinks), and using anaerobic digesters to capture methane from waste.
- **Ethical and sustainability issues** around nutrient cycling: intensive agriculture adds artificial fertiliser, which boosts crop yield but runs off into waterways causing **eutrophication** (excess nutrients, algal blooms, oxygen depletion and fish kills) and can contribute to nitrous oxide release; pesticides disturb soil microbiomes; organic and regenerative farming relies on fixing carbon and nitrogen through rotations, legumes and cover crops.

#### Examiner traps

- **Calling fungi prokaryotic, or bacteria eukaryotic.** The single most basic error in the unit.
- **Saying bacteria have a nucleus or mitochondria.** They do not.
- **Confusing binary fission with budding or spores.** Bacteria divide by binary fission; fungi produce spores.
- **Naming nitrogen-cycle microorganisms when the specification says they are not required.** Describing *what happens* to the nitrogen is the mark; naming *Rhizobium* is optional credit.
- **Writing "decomposition" without stating what it releases.** Mineral ions returned to the soil for plants to reuse is the point.
- **Placing the growth curve phases in the wrong order or stating that all cells divide in lag.** Lag is adaptation; log is division.
- **Omitting methane from a greenhouse gas answer.** CO₂ is the obvious one and the one students write; CH₄ is the one that earns the second mark.

#### 30-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Can you produce the bacteria-versus-fungi comparison on structure, nutrition and cellular nature?
- [ ] Can you compute the number of cells after a given number of binary-fission divisions?
- [ ] Can you name all four growth-curve phases and say which one an industrial process harvests from?
- [ ] Can you write the four nitrogen-cycle processes and the arrow direction for each?
- [ ] Can you name two carbon sinks and explain why bogs are one of them?

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### Modelling the nitrogen cycle — the four transformations

For a diagram question, draw the cycle with **nitrogen gas in the atmosphere at the centre** and four named transformations. For each, state what changes chemically, which form nitrogen moves between, and what drives it.

1. **Nitrogen fixation.** Atmospheric N₂ (78% of the air, chemically inert because of the triple bond) → **ammonia/ammonium**, converted to nitrite and nitrate in soil. Driven by nitrogen-fixing bacteria, notably in legume root nodules, and by industrial Haber production. This is the only process that adds nitrogen to the soil.
2. **Nitrification.** Ammonium → **nitrite → nitrate**. A two-step oxidation by nitrifying bacteria, needing oxygen, and therefore slowed in waterlogged soil.
3. **Assimilation.** **Nitrate → organic nitrogen** (amino acids, proteins, nucleic acids) as plants absorb nitrate and reduce it. Animals obtain their nitrogen by eating plants.
4. **Denitrification.** **Nitrate → N₂ gas**, by denitrifying bacteria, typically in anaerobic conditions such as waterlogged soil. It returns nitrogen to the atmosphere.

Plus **decomposition** running continuously: dead organisms and faeces → **ammonium**, by decomposers, which is what links this cycle to the carbon cycle, since the same decomposers respire the carbon as CO₂.

*Worked question.* A farmer ploughs a field in spring, which aerates the soil. Explain the effect on the nitrogen cycle.
*A.* Ploughing introduces oxygen to the soil, which **increases nitrification**, converting more ammonium to nitrate, so more nitrate is available to plants by assimilation. At the same time it discourages **denitrification**, which requires anaerobic conditions, so less nitrate is lost to the atmosphere as N₂. This is a real and measurable benefit of cultivation: ploughed land retains more usable soil nitrogen. The caveat is that the extra nitrate makes leaching into waterways more likely, and nitrate reaching water causes eutrophication.

#### Modelling the carbon cycle

Draw four pools and four fluxes:

- **Atmosphere → biomass** by **photosynthesis** (the main carbon sink; plants, algae and phytoplankton).
- **Biomass → atmosphere** by **respiration** (plants, animals, decomposers) — the largest natural return flux.
- **Biomass → soil → atmosphere** by **decomposition and decomposition respiration**.
- **Carbon stored in sinks**: **bogs and peat** (waterlogged, oxygen-poor, so decomposition is extremely slow and carbon accumulates over millennia — the reason Irish blanket bog is a significant carbon store), forests, marine sediments, and **fossil fuels** formed over geological time.
- **Fossil fuels → atmosphere** by combustion, the flux currently running at roughly the same order of magnitude as natural photosynthesis uptake, which is why atmospheric CO₂ is rising.

The evaluative conclusion the specification invites: photosynthesis is currently a carbon sink but cannot keep pace with the extra atmospheric carbon from combustion; the biological strategies available are afforestation, peatland restoration and conservation of existing sinks, plus reduction of methane emissions from ruminants and landfill.

#### Investigating factors affecting microbial growth

The specification asks you to investigate, so build the full plan.

1. **A suitable culture.** *E. coli* or *Bacillus subtilis* on sterile nutrient agar plates are standard in secondary school practical work; growing cultures requires sterile technique — sterilise the medium and inoculating loop, work near a flame, and do not open a plate unnecessarily.
2. **Independent variable.** One factor only, changed deliberately: temperature, pH, nutrient concentration, or the concentration of an antibacterial substance such as penicillin.
3. **Dependent variable.** Number of colonies on the plate after a set incubation time, counted with a **colony counter** or a grid marked on the plate base.
4. **Controlled variables.** Volume of inoculum, agar volume, incubation time, and the identity of the medium. The one you most often forget is the **volume and dilution of the inoculum** — equal volumes must be used or the counts mean nothing.
5. **Serial dilution.** A single plate cannot hold more than a few hundred colonies usefully, so dilute the culture through a series of known factors and plate known volumes from each step. This is what makes the count quantitative rather than qualitative.
6. **Repeats.** Several plates per condition; count the mean.
7. **Ethical and safety requirements.** No culture work on an open bench; cultures must be incubated at a temperature safe for students; plates are sealed and autoclaved before disposal, and cultures are never left where an unregulated person can handle them. An answer that omits safety loses marks and, in a real laboratory, is simply wrong.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Worked Higher-level questions with answers

**Q1.** Explain why a decomposer is essential to an ecosystem rather than merely useful.
*A.* Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) break down dead organic matter and waste, converting it into **mineral ions** — ammonium, nitrate, phosphate — that plants can absorb. Without them, nutrients would be locked permanently into dead bodies and faeces, the soil would gradually run out of usable nitrogen and phosphorus, and plant productivity would fall until the ecosystem collapsed. Their respiration also returns CO₂ to the atmosphere, which closes the carbon loop. They are the only route by which matter returns to the abiotic pool, so an ecosystem without decomposers is finite in nutrients rather than self-sustaining. That is why a "clean" food chain is never actually clean — detritus and decomposers underpin every trophic level above them.

**Q2.** A student's culture shows growth over 24 hours. Sketch the curve and label the four phases, stating what is happening in each and which phase an industry would harvest in.
*A.* A **lag** phase with no net increase, because cells are adapting and synthesising the enzymes needed for the new medium. A **log** phase of constant maximum rate of exponential increase, limited only by available nutrients and space. A **stationary** phase where the viable total plateaus because the birth rate has fallen to equal the death rate, as nutrients are depleted and metabolic waste accumulates. A **decline** phase where the viable count falls sharply as conditions become lethal. Industry harvests **late log to early stationary**: in late log the cells are growing most rapidly and so produce most product per unit time, while product accumulated in the stationary phase is not being broken down. Harvesting in decline risks losing both viable biomass and accumulated product. For a batch culture this is a direct application of the curve to yield.

**Q3.** Explain why a blanket bog is described as a carbon sink.
*A.* A blanket bog is waterlogged, so oxygen does not reach the lower layers. Aerobic decomposers cannot work without oxygen, so decomposition there is extremely slow, and the carbon from dead sphagnum and other plant material is only partly broken down and released as CO₂. The carbon that is not respired is locked into peat. Over millennia this accumulates into peat soils that store very large quantities of carbon. If the bog is drained — for peat extraction, forestry or agriculture — oxygen reaches the peat, decomposition accelerates, stored CO₂ is released, and the sink becomes a source. This is why bog restoration is treated as a climate measure and why the Irish State has committed to peatland conservation under European and international climate obligations.

**Q4.** Evaluate the claim that increasing artificial fertiliser use is the sustainable way to feed a growing population.
*A.* The evidence for it is real: nitrogen and phosphorus fertilisers raise crop yields substantially, and they do so on land that is already cleared, so the yield gain avoids habitat conversion and land sparing. The limitations are that much applied nitrogen is not taken up by crops — it is lost by **denitrification** as N₂ (with some N₂O, a powerful greenhouse gas) and by leaching as nitrate into waterways, where it drives **eutrophication** — algal blooms, shading of the water, oxygen depletion as the algae decompose, and fish kills. Run-off also reaches coastal waters. Biological and soil fertility is degraded, and fertiliser manufacture involves significant energy use and emissions. A considered answer recommends a combined approach: use fertiliser more efficiently, with timing and placement matched to crop need, combined with rotations including **legumes that fix nitrogen biologically**, cover crops that capture soluble nutrients, and organic matter inputs that support soil microbiomes. The evaluative judgement is that artificial fertiliser is a necessary input but not a sustainable one in its current form.

**Q5.** Explain the role of the human gut microbiome in health.
*A.* The gut hosts a dense community of bacteria, archaea and fungi that live in a mutually dependent relationship with the host. They contribute to **digestion** by fermenting fibre and other carbohydrates that human enzymes cannot break down, producing short-chain fatty acids that the colon absorbs and uses for energy. They contribute to **immune signalling**: the gut-associated lymphoid tissue is continuously exposed to these organisms, and the microbiome shapes the development and regulation of the immune system — which is why antibiotics can cause immune-related effects such as Clostridioides difficile colitis. They contribute to **metabolic health** by synthesising vitamins, including vitamin K and several B vitamins, and by influencing the metabolism of bile acids and other host compounds. The specification frames the microbiome as significant in all three, and an answer that names only digestion loses the other two.

#### How this unit pays elsewhere

- **Unit 3.1** supplies the food webs, trophic levels and decomposer role that these cycles complete.
- **Unit 2.6** supplies osmosis — the mechanism behind plasmolysis and the preservation of food by high external solute concentration.
- **Unit 2.2** supplies photosynthesis and respiration as the carbon fluxes.
- **Unit 2.4** supplies the bacterial pathogens and antibiotic resistance discussed in industry and emerging disease.
- **Unit 3.3** applies everything: industrial-scale fermentation for pharmaceuticals, PCR enzymes from thermophilic bacteria, and microbial production of the enzymes used in genetic engineering.
- **Biology in Practice Investigation** (40% of the grade) — the effect of temperature or pH on bacterial growth, or the effect of an antibacterial substance, is a directly assessable project. It requires sterile technique, primary data, an ethical treatment and safety provisions, all of which are scored.

#### Mistakes students actually make

1. **Swapping prokaryotic and eukaryotic.** Learn it once, as a pair: bacteria prokaryotic, fungi eukaryotic.
2. **Naming microorganism species in a nitrogen-cycle answer.** The specification says names are not required; describe the chemical conversion instead.
3. **Writing "decomposers give plants nutrients" without naming them.** Ammonium, nitrate and phosphate are the marks.
4. **Confusing lag and log phase.** Lag is no division; log is maximum division.
5. **Forgetting that the carbon cycle and nitrogen cycle are linked by decomposers.** That is what makes them one system.
6. **Treating the S-curve in unit 3.1 as a growth curve here.** S-curves are populations; these are microbial batch growth. Mixing them loses the distinguishing mark.
7. **Omitting safety and ethics from a practical plan.** Sterile technique and safe disposal are named requirements, not optional extras.
8. **Writing only CO₂ in a climate answer.** Methane, from ruminants, landfill and anaerobic digesters, is explicitly in the specification.
