# Percentages and Ratios

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **percentage** expresses a number as a fraction of 100, written with the % sign. A **ratio** compares two or more quantities of the same kind, showing how many times one is contained in the other. The two work hand-in-hand because every ratio can be turned into a percentage by multiplying the fraction form by 100.

The three conversion formulas to memorise first are: **Part = (Percentage × Whole) ÷ 100**, **Whole = (Part × 100) ÷ Percentage**, and **Percentage change = ((New − Old) ÷ Old) × 100**. For ratios, always reduce a:b to its simplest form by dividing both terms by their HCF.

In NCEE Mathematics (4% weight), expect 2–4 questions testing: percentage increase/decrease, sharing an amount in a given ratio, profit percent = (Profit ÷ CP) × 100, and simple interest SI = (PRT) ÷ 100. Always divide profit, loss, and interest by the **cost price**, never the selling price.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Definitions

A **percentage** is a fraction whose denominator is 100. So 35% means 35/100 = 0.35. To convert any fraction p/q to a percentage, multiply by 100: (p/q) × 100. To convert a decimal to a percentage, shift the decimal point two places right; to convert a percentage back to a decimal, shift two places left.

A **ratio** a:b means the first quantity is to the second as a is to b, equivalent to the fraction a/b. Ratios are dimensionless (units cancel only if both quantities share the same unit) and must be reduced to **lowest terms** by dividing both terms by their HCF. The ratio 18:24 simplifies to 3:4, not 6:8.

#### Sharing in a Ratio

To divide a quantity Q in the ratio a:b, add the parts (a + b = total parts) and apply the **unitary method**: one part = Q ÷ (a + b), then the first share = a × (one part), the second share = b × (one part). For three-way ratios a:b:c, total parts = a + b + c.

#### Percentage Change

**Percentage increase** = ((New − Old) ÷ Old) × 100. **Percentage decrease** = ((Old − New) ÷ Old) × 100. To apply a percentage increase, multiply by (1 + r/100); for a decrease, multiply by (1 − r/100).

#### Commercial Arithmetic

| Quantity | Formula | Base used |
|---|---|---|
| Profit % | (SP − CP) ÷ CP × 100 | Cost Price |
| Loss % | (CP − SP) ÷ CP × 100 | Cost Price |
| Discount % | (Discount ÷ Marked Price) × 100 | Marked Price |
| Simple Interest | (P × R × T) ÷ 100 | Principal |
| VAT | (% rate ÷ 100) × marked price | Pre-tax price |

#### Direct and Inverse Proportion

Two quantities are in **direct proportion** (y = kx) when one increases, the other increases in the same ratio — doubling x doubles y. They are in **inverse proportion** (xy = k) when one doubles, the other halves. Speed ∝ 1/time and workers ∝ 1/days are classic inverse-proportion setups.

#### Typical NCEE Question Patterns

1. "Express 45 out of 180 as a percentage" → 25%.
2. "Divide N12,000 between A and B in ratio 3:5" → A gets N4,500, B gets N7,500.
3. "A trader bought an article for N800 and sold it for N920. Find the profit percent" → 15%.
4. "If 8 workers finish a job in 15 days, how many workers finish it in 10 days?" → Inverse proportion: 8 × 15 = x × 10, so x = 12 workers.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Subtle Distinctions

**Percentage points vs. percent change** are different. If a tax rises from 5% to 7%, the absolute rise is 2 percentage points, but the relative rise is (7 − 5)/5 × 100 = 40% — an NCEE trap.

**Successive percentage changes do not add.** A 10% rise followed by a 10% fall does not return to the original value. Apply the multipliers: 1.10 × 0.90 = 0.99, so the final value is 99% of the original — a 1% net loss, not zero. Use the chained multiplier (1 + a/100)(1 + b/100) for any number of successive changes.

**Reverse percentages** ask: "after a 20% increase the price is N600, what was the original?" Divide by the multiplier: 600 ÷ 1.20 = N500, not 600 × 0.20 subtracted.

#### Ratio Connection to Probability and Maps

Ratios scale. Map scale 1:50,000 means 1 cm on the map equals 50,000 cm (500 m) on the ground. Three-term ratios a:b:c used in probability yield equally likely outcomes when a, b, c are in lowest terms.

#### Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Dividing profit by selling price instead of cost price — always use CP as the base for profit% and loss%.
- Adding ratios a + b without first summing to get total parts.
- Mixing time units: simple interest uses T in **years**, so convert months by dividing by 12.
- Treating percentage decrease as 100 − r instead of (Old − New)/Old × 100 when the new value is given.
- Comparing percentages taken from different wholes — 30% of N50 is N15, but 30% of N200 is N60.

#### Worked Example

A shopkeeper marks a television at N48,000 and offers a 10% discount. VAT of 7.5% is then added to the discounted price. Find the final amount paid.
Discount = 10% × 48,000 = N4,800. Discounted price = 48,000 − 4,800 = N43,200.
VAT = 7.5% × 43,200 = N3,240. Final amount = 43,200 + 3,240 = **N46,440**.

#### Practice Prompts

1. A sum of N25,000 yields N3,750 simple interest over 3 years. Find the rate per annum. *(Answer: 5%)*
2. Three partners share a profit of N540,000 in the ratio 2:3:4. How much more does the third receive than the first? *(Answer: N108,000)*
