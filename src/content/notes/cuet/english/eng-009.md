# Foreign Phrases

Foreign phrases are borrowed expressions that English never finished absorbing. A French or Latin phrase arrives in an English sentence, keeps its original words, and settles into a fixed meaning that no longer matches its literal translation. That gap between the literal words and the settled meaning is the whole topic. When a question says "he was appointed **ex officio** chairman", the words in front of you are Latin for "from the office" and the settled meaning is "because of the office he holds" — you cannot derive the second from the first, which is exactly why the phrase has to be learned as a unit.

The practical consequence is that foreign phrases are among the cheapest marks in an English paper and among the most commonly lost. They are cheap because the list is finite: a few dozen phrases carry almost all the questions. They are lost because candidates try to translate instead of recall. If you attempt *prima facie* by thinking "first face", you will get it wrong; it means "at first sight, until proved otherwise". If you recall it, you will get it right every time.

There are three families to master, and they behave differently under exam conditions.

- **Abbreviations** — *i.e., e.g., etc., viz., et al., cf., ibid., fl.* These are short, they carry punctuation rules, and they are the most commonly tested group.
- **Unassimilated phrases** — *ad hoc, bona fide, per se, prima facie, status quo, in situ, vice versa*. These keep italics in careful writing and hold precise senses that the literal words do not predict.
- **Naturalised phrases** — *per capita, per annum, vice versa, in medias res, in toto*. These have entered general English and are no longer italicised.

A note on the exam itself before the content. CUET UG is a computer-based test with modular sections, and the language paper in Section I is objective — reading comprehension, vocabulary and grammar in the chosen language. The exact number of questions and the time block for any given paper are fixed in that year's NTA information bulletin, so check the bulletin for the year you are sitting rather than trusting any figure quoted in a coaching video or a previous year's paper. What is stable across years is the *kind* of item you will meet, and that is what this chapter trains: a phrase appears in a sentence and you must pick the closest meaning, or it appears in a flawed sentence and you must spot the error.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

**The abbreviations you must know without hesitation.** Learn the expansion, because the expansion is what tells you the function, and the function is what the question tests.

| Written form | Full form | Literal meaning | Job it does in a sentence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **i.e.** | *id est* | "that is" | Restates or clarifies what was just said |
| **e.g.** | *exempli gratia* | "for example" | Introduces one instance of a wider class |
| **etc.** | *et cetera* | "and the rest" | Continues a list of like things |
| **et al.** | *et alii* | "and others" | Adds further people to a name list |
| **viz.** | *videlicet* | "namely" | Names the members of a group already mentioned |
| **cf.** | *confer* | "compare" | Points the reader to a parallel statement |
| **ibid.** | *ibidem* | "in the same place" | Cites the immediately preceding source again |
| **fl.** | *floruit* | "flourished" | Gives approximate dates of life |
| **vs.** | *versus* | "against" | Marks opposition in a comparison |
| **c.** or **ca.** | *circa* | "about" | Approximates a date or figure |
| **op. cit.** | *opere citato* | "in the work cited" | Points back to an already-cited source |
| **pro rata** | — | "in proportion" | Distributes something by share |

The single trap that catches everyone is **i.e. against e.g.** *i.e.* restates. *e.g.* introduces. If a sentence says "The scheme failed in three districts, **i.e.** Gaya, Bhagalpur and Muzaffarpur", the *i.e.* is a paraphrase of what came before and is redundant — that is an error-spotting item in waiting. If it says "Three districts were affected, **e.g.** Gaya", the *e.g.* introduces a member of a larger unnamed class, which is correct.

**The high-yield phrase list.** If you memorise nothing else from this topic, memorise these.

| Phrase | Meaning | The trap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *ad hoc* | For this particular purpose only | Not "temporarily", not "informally" |
| *bona fide* | Genuine, in good faith | Adjective only — it modifies a noun |
| *per se* | In itself, by its own nature | Not "by itself" as a loose synonym for "alone" |
| *prima facie* | At first sight, until disproved | Not "first rate" or "first of all" |
| *status quo* | The existing state of affairs | Not simply "current situation" |
| *in situ* | In the original place | Not "in the situation" |
| *vice versa* | With the order reversed | Not "vice versa" as "in the same way" |
| *mutatis mutandis* | With the necessary changes made | Not "everything stays the same" |
| *in toto* | In its entirety, completely | Not "in total" as a numeric sum |
| *a priori* | Based on reasoning before evidence | Opposite of *a posteriori* |
| *de facto* | In fact, by practice | Opposite of *de jure* |
| *ceteris paribus* | Other things being equal | The examiner's favourite in economics passages |

**Two conventions worth memorising because they are cheap marks.**

*Italicisation.* A phrase that English has not fully naturalised is set in **italics** in careful prose: *per se, prima facie, mutatis mutandis, bona fide, in situ*. A phrase that has been naturalised is not: *per capita, per annum, vice versa, in medias res, in toto, ad hoc* in most modern house styles. Native dictionaries also disagree at the margin — *bona fide* is now often printed without italics — so treat italics as a convention to respect rather than a rule with a single right answer.

*Punctuation.* A short parenthetical abbreviation takes a full stop before the bracket: "the scheme covers four states (viz. Bihar, Jharkhand, Assam and Odisha)." An abbreviation that is part of the running sentence takes a comma before it: "Several districts, e.g. Gaya, reported a decline." Getting this wrong is a *usage* error, and usage errors are exactly what a language-ability paper tests.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

**Where the phrases come from, and why it matters.** The source language predicts the register you will meet the phrase in, which tells you which phrases to prioritise.

- **Latin** dominates legal, academic, scientific and formal administrative English: *per se, prima facie, mutatis mutandis, in toto, in re, ex officio, bona fides, ergo, inter alia, ceteris paribus, non sequitur*.
- **French** dominates diplomacy, cuisine, fashion and polite social usage: *carte blanche, coup de grâce, fait accompli, force majeure, pièce de résistance, rapport, déjeune, entrée, résumé, déjà vu, bon appétit*.
- **Greek** supplies scientific and philosophical vocabulary, usually via Latin: *in medias res, per annum* (through Latin), *apocalypse, paradox, thesis, synthesis, criterion, status*.
- **Italian** supplies musical and art terms: *intermezzo, crescendo, diminuendo, allegro, staccato, fiasco, replica, fresco*.
- **German** is a smaller but real source: *kindergarten, zeitgeist, fernweh, doppelgänger, panzer*.

In a CUET language paper drawn from news, social science and general-awareness material, **Latin and French between them supply the large majority of testable phrases**. If your time is short, learn those two source-lists first.

**The four distinctions examiners actually build items from.** Every hard question in this topic is one of these four pairs.

**1. Reasoned before, as opposed to observed after: *a priori* vs *a posteriori*.** An *a priori* claim rests on reason or assumption, before evidence arrives. An *a posteriori* claim rests on experience and observation, drawn after the fact. "It is *a priori* absurd to expect a purely digital currency to eliminate cash" is reasoning from first principles. "Sales fell *a posteriori*, once the figures came in, so the forecast was wrong" is learning from outcomes. The pair is standard in economics and philosophy passages.

**2. In practice, as opposed to by law: *de facto* vs *de jure*.** *De jure* means by legal right, by statute. *De facto* means by actual practice, whether or not it is legal. A regime can be *de jure* banned while remaining *de facto* prevalent — this is the most common use, and it appears constantly in current-affairs passages on regulation, taxation and education policy.

**3. In itself, as opposed to in its place: *per se* vs *in situ*.** *Per se* is about intrinsic character, detached from circumstance. *In situ* is about location, "in the original position". A specimen is *in situ* when it is examined where it was found; an argument is *per se* when it is being judged on its own logic. A rare confusion worth knowing: *in vitro* means "in an artificial laboratory environment" and *in vivo* means "within a living organism" — both are usually printed without italics in scientific prose.

**4. The state, as opposed to the state before: *status quo* vs *status quo ante*.** *Status quo* is the existing condition, whatever it is. *Status quo ante* is explicitly the condition as it stood **before** some change — the phrase's whole point is the comparison. "The committee restored the *status quo ante*" means it undid a change. "The report simply documented the *status quo*" means it described what already was. Substituting one for the other reverses the direction of the argument, which is why it is a favourite trap.

**The Latin abbreviation set used in academic writing, with the punctuation each one wants.**

| Abbreviation | Use it when you need to… | Punctuation model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *e.g.* | give one instance of a class | "Two causes dominate, e.g. rainfall and run-off." |
| *i.e.* | restate a claim in shorter or plainer form | "The scheme is revenue-neutral, i.e. it costs nothing." |
| *et al.* | add unnamed co-authors or co-workers | "Sen, Kumar et al. reported a similar effect." |
| *cf.* | direct the reader to a parallel claim | "Compare the monsoon data, cf. Table 4." |
| *viz.* | name the members of a group just defined | "Three organs are involved, viz. liver, kidney and spleen." |
| *ibid.* | cite the previous footnote again | "Ibid., p. 214." |
| *et seq.* | show the reference continues | "See the discussion, 41 et seq." |
| *fl.* | date a person's life | "Ibn Battuta, 1304–1368 fl." |

A useful discipline for the writing component: **if you use a Latin abbreviation in a formal essay, expand it once and then use the abbreviation.** First mention: "that is, *that is* (i.e. contrary to the official figure)". Later mentions: *i.e.* This is standard academic practice and it is the safest possible handling when you are not certain of the punctuation.

**How the items are actually set.** Four templates account for almost everything you will face.

1. **Closest meaning.** One sentence, one italicised phrase, four English paraphrases. The distractor is usually a *near*-literal translation — the phrase that reads right from the Latin but is wrong in English.
2. **Error in the sentence.** The phrase is used where the wrong abbreviation or the wrong sense sits, and you must identify the error.
3. **Fill in the blank.** The logic of the sentence demands one function — restatement or example — and only one abbreviation supplies it.
4. **Reordering or substitution.** A phrase must be swapped for an English equivalent that keeps the meaning *and* the register.

In every template, the skill is the same: **identify the function the sentence needs, then match the phrase to the function.** Never start from the phrase.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

**The naturalisation gradient, and why italicisation is not arbitrary.** Phrases enter English in a rough order — specialist field first, then general circulation, then dictionary listing, then no italics at all.

Take *per capita*. It begins in Latin demographic and economic writing, passes into English technical prose, and is now printed upright in newspapers. *Per se* is still in wide circulation in formal and legal English and is still italicised in most style guides. *Mutatis mutandis* is used mainly in translated legal and diplomatic material and keeps its italics. The rule that follows from this is a practical one: **if you have only ever seen the phrase in a dictionary that italicises it, italicise it; if you have seen it running in an ordinary book or a newspaper, print it upright.** Never agonise — this is a convention, and a defensible convention is worth more than a coin-flip.

**The harder Latin set, decoded.** You can learn this family by structure rather than by rote, because Latin is a morphologically regular language and the pieces recur.

- **In + ablative, meaning "into" or "in the state of":** *in situ* (in place), *in vitro* (in glass), *in vivo* (in life), *in re* (concerning the matter), *in toto* (wholly), *in limine* (at the threshold), *in medias res* (into the midst of things).
- **Per + ablative, meaning "by means of" or "with respect to":** *per se* (by itself), *per capita* (by the head), *per annum* (by the year), *per diem* (by the day), *per annum* is the one to write in formal budgets, not "per year" if you want the register.
- **Ex + ablative, meaning "from" or "out of":** *ex officio* (by virtue of office), *ex parte* (from one side only), *ex post facto* (after the fact).
- **Cum and its relatives:** *non conveniens* (not fitting), *mutatis mutandis* (with the necessary changes).
- **Abbreviations built the same way:** *c.* from *circa* (about), *fl.* from *floruit* (flourished), *et seq.* from *et sequentes* (and the following).

Once you see the pattern, an unfamiliar phrase becomes readable. "*Pro tanto*" — by the ablative of *quantus*, "so much" — means "to that extent" and shows up in accounting and diplomatic texts as a concessive: we concede a point only so far. "*In re*" is the standard opening of a legal matter's title. "*Sub judice*" means "under judicial consideration", that is, before a court.

**The French register, and why the English calque misleads.** French phrases often fail under literal translation in a way Latin ones do not, because English has taken the French idioms at their word.

- *Force majeure* — "greater force". In English it means **a force majeure clause suspends contractual obligations** when an extraordinary event beyond control prevents performance. Candidates often answer "great strength" or "the main authority". The trap is translating the words instead of recalling the sense.
- *Carte blanche* — "blank paper". It means **unconditional authority to act**, not "a blank cheque" in the literal financial sense.
- *Coup de grâce* — "blow of grace". It means **the finishing stroke that ends a suffering or a comeback**, not a "graceful blow".
- *Fait accompli* — "accomplished fact". It means **a thing already done and therefore hard to undo**, used to describe a fait accompli policy.
- *Pièce de résistance* — "resistance piece". It means **the centrepiece of a performance or a course**, the show-stopping item. The literal "resistance" misleads badly.
- *Rendez-vous* — French for a meeting, used in English as an appointment, and as a verb it means **to arrange to meet**.
- *Déjà vu* — "already seen", used in English for the feeling of recognising something already met. It is a real English word now, usually unitalicised, and it is not a synonym for *memory* loss, which is the common misreading.

**Bona fides, and the plural that matters.** *Bona fides* is a **noun** meaning "good faith" or, in the plural sense, "credentials, qualification". *Bona fide* is an **adjective** meaning "genuine, made in good faith". These are the two items in this topic that change grammatical class, which is why they appear in usage and error-spotting items so often.

- "The police verified his **bona fides**." — noun: his credentials. Correct.
- "The police could not verify his **bona fides**." — noun, plural sense, but still correct: the credentials were not established.
- "He is a **bona fide** journalist." — adjective, modifies the noun. Correct.
- "He was appointed **bona fide**." — wrong; the adjective has no noun to modify.

Read the sentence, find the word class the slot demands, and the phrase decides itself.

**The register trap, which is bigger here than in any other topic.** Foreign phrases mark formality. A passage written in journalistic register uses *says* and *shows*; a passage in academic register uses *posits* and *demonstrates*. If a question asks you to replace a foreign phrase with an English equivalent, the correct answer is not always the most accurate one — it is the one that keeps the register. Replacing *carte blanche* with "full authority to act" is accurate; replacing it with "a free pass" destroys the register and is wrong. Replacing *et cetera* with "and other things" is accurate in the supermarket and wrong in a report.

**A drill that actually fixes the topic.** Foreign phrases fail because recognition is weak under time pressure, not because the list is impossible. Do this for two weeks, fifteen minutes a day.

1. **Five minutes, one list.** Write from memory as many foreign phrases as you can, with meanings. Do not check answers yet.
2. **Five minutes, check and mark.** Each one you missed goes into three groups: *never heard*, *heard, meaning blurred*, *meaning right, usage wrong*.
3. **Five minutes, one sentence each.** For the *usage wrong* group only, write a correct sentence and an incorrect sentence. This is the group that actually costs marks, because it is the one that cannot be fixed by memorising harder.

Group three is where the marks are. Group one just needs exposure.

- **"Restate is *i.e*, example is *e.g*."** The single sentence that settles the most common error in the topic. If it repeats what you just said, it is *i.e*. If it shows one of several, it is *e.g*.
- **"Function before phrase."** Every item is solved by asking what the sentence needs done, then choosing the phrase. Starting from the phrase is how students invent wrong meanings.
- **"Pre-first word, the meaning."** *a priori* is reasoned first, *a posteriori* is reasoned after. Same skeleton, opposite timing, and the Latin adverb itself tells you which is which: *priori* is the comparative of *prior*.
- **"*De facto* is what happened; *de jure* is what the law says."** If the sentence contrasts with a law, the phrase is *de facto*.
- **"*Per se* asks about the thing; *in situ* asks about the place."** Neither has anything to do with "by itself" as a casual phrase.
- **"Translate once, then stop."** *Force majeure* is not "greater force". *Coup de grâce* is not "grace blow". Once you accept that the words do not predict the sense, memorisation becomes the only method and you stop losing marks to clever guesses.
- **Flashcard Q&A:**
  - *Difference between "et al." and "etc."?* → **et al.** adds unnamed **people**; **etc.** continues a list of **things**.
  - *What does *bona fides* mean that *bona fide* does not?* → It is a **noun**: good faith, or in the plural, credentials.
  - *Is *per capita* italicised?* → No. It is naturalised; *per se* usually is.
  - *Restate this in Latin: "that is, the opposite of the official figure."* → *i.e.*, and it must restate, not introduce.
  - *Which phrase means "in the original place"?* → *in situ*.

### 🎯 Exam Traps & Error Log

1. **Using *e.g.* to restate.** This is the highest-frequency error in the topic. A restatement needs *i.e.*; *e.g.* introduces an example and creates a meaning the writer did not intend. In a comprehension passage it flips the author's claim, so the question keyed to that line becomes unanswerable.
2. **Translating instead of recalling.** *Force majeure* read as "greater force" and *coup de grâce* read as "blow of grace" produce confident, fluent, wrong answers. Confidence is the danger — the wrong option sounds better than the right one.
3. **Treating *status quo* as "the current situation".** It is the existing state of affairs, the state you are arguing about changing or preserving. *Status quo ante* is the state **before** a change, and the two are opposites in direction.
4. **Confusing *a priori* with *a posteriori*.** One is before the evidence, one is after it. Both are common in social-science and economics passages, which is exactly where the question is set.
5. **Confusing *de facto* with *de jure*.** A practice may be *de facto* without being *de jure*. Reading these as synonyms in a passage about regulation reverses the argument.
6. **Using *bona fide* as an adverb or a bare predicate.** It is an adjective and needs a noun: *a bona fide offer*. "He was appointed bona fide" is a usage error, and it is a clean one to spot.
7. **Mistaking *et al.* for *etc.*,** or *viz.* for either. *Et al.* is for people, *etc.* for things, *viz.* for naming a group just introduced. All three are in a language paper and all three are confused with each other.
8. **Ignoring the punctuation of the abbreviation.** A parenthetical abbreviation takes a full stop before the bracket; a mid-sentence one takes a comma. Error-spotting items reward spotting this.
9. **Swapping a phrase for an accurate English equivalent of the wrong register.** Replacing *carte blanche* with "a free pass" is semantically fine and formally wrong. When you substitute, match the register, not just the meaning.
10. **Italicising a naturalised phrase or romanising an unassimilated one.** Style guides differ at the margin, but the broad split holds: *per capita, vice versa, in toto, in medias res* upright; *per se, prima facie, mutatis mutandis* italicised.
11. **Learning the expansion without the function.** Knowing that *e.g.* is *exempli gratia* is not the point unless you also know it introduces an instance. The function is what the question asks about.

### 🧪 Self-Test — 8 Questions with Worked Answers

Answer these on paper before reading the solutions. Each one is built from a template that appears in the topic.

1. **Choose the closest meaning: "The committee was constituted *ad hoc* to examine the admission irregularities."**
   *Options: (a) temporarily / (b) for this specific purpose only / (c) informally / (d) permanently.* The phrase is doing one job: explaining why a committee with no standing mandate exists at all. **For this specific purpose only** is the answer. *Temporarily* is the classic distractor — it describes duration, and *ad hoc* describes purpose. *Informally* confuses it with *ad interim* or "off the cuff". *Permanently* is the exact opposite and exists only as a trap.

2. **Spot the error: "The scheme covers four districts, e.g. Gaya, Bhagalpur, Muzaffarpur and Katihar."**
   The error is **e.g. for i.e.**, or possibly *e.g.* for *viz.* — both point at the same failure. The sentence names all four districts of a group it has already fixed as four. It is therefore **restating and enumerating an already-specified set**, which is *i.e.* or *viz.*, not introducing an example of a wider class. *E.g.* would be right only if the sentence implied there were more districts than the four named. Marking keywords: *example wrongly used for restatement*, or *abbreviation misapplied*. In a rewrite: "The scheme covers four districts, **viz.** Gaya, Bhagalpur, Muzaffarpur and Katihar."

3. **Choose the correct sentence.**
   (a) He was appointed *bona fide*.
   (b) His *bona fides* as a researcher were never verified.
   (c) The committee accepted his *bona fide* credentials.
   (d) She works *bona fide* with the archive.
   **(b) is correct.** *Bona fides* is a noun — here in the plural sense, "credentials" — and takes a plural verb. (a) and (c) are wrong because *bona fide* is an **adjective** and needs a noun to modify: a *bona fide* appointment, a *bona fide* researcher. (d) is doubly wrong — the adjective cannot function as an adverb. The whole item is decided by word class, not by meaning, which is the point of testing it this way.

4. **Fill in the blank: "The argument rests on assumptions made *a priori*, before any data were collected." Which phrase here means the opposite?**
   The phrase in the sentence is ***a priori*** — reasoning that precedes the evidence. Its opposite is ***a posteriori***, reasoning drawn from experience after the fact: "the model's predictions were assessed *a posteriori*, once the outcomes were known." The pairing is regular in Latin — *priori* and *posteriori* are the comparatives of *prior* and *posterior*, and the Latin literally carries the timing. If an option says "a posteriori", the sentence must be talking about what came after evidence arrived.

5. **Choose the closest meaning: "The ban remained *de facto* illegal for two years."**
   *Options: (a) officially illegal by statute / (b) actually in force, whatever the law said / (c) theoretically illegal / (d) declared illegal in court.* **(b) is right.** *De facto* means by actual practice. The sentence's own construction proves it: it contrasts *de facto* with a legal status, so the phrase is the term for practice as opposed to *de jure*, by law. (a) is *de jure*. (c) inverts the pair, since "theoretical" is the domain of *a priori* reasoning, not *de facto*. (d) introduces a court, which the phrase does not imply.

6. **Correct this: "The original manuscript was found *in toto*, and the missing pages were restored."**
   *In toto* means **in its entirety, in the whole**. The sentence uses it as though it meant "in its original place", which is ***in situ***. The rewrite is: "The original manuscript was found **in situ**, and the missing pages were restored." If the author really meant that the manuscript was recovered whole, the correct sentence is "The original manuscript was recovered **in toto**." The item tests whether you can separate the *in +* family members, which is the largest and most confused group in Latin borrowings: *in situ* (place), *in vitro* (glass), *in vivo* (life), *in toto* (whole), *in re* (concerning), *in medias res* (midway into).

7. **Which is the better substitute for "The governor was given *carte blanche* to reorganise the department", and why is the other rejected?**
   **"The governor was given full authority to reorganise the department"** is correct. It keeps both the meaning — unconditional permission to act — and the formal register of the original. **"The governor was given a free pass to reorganise the department"** is rejected on **register, not meaning**: it is a colloquial phrase that belongs to informal speech, and a formal administrative sentence cannot absorb it. This is the substitution template in its purest form. Under it, the exam gives you a phrase and four English equivalents that differ in meaning *and* register; the register is often what separates the last two survivors, exactly as connotation does in a vocabulary item.

8. **Rewrite this correctly and explain the error: "The report lists the affected crops, id est, rice, wheat and sugarcane."**
   The corrected form is: "The report lists the affected crops, **i.e.**, rice, wheat and sugarcane." The error is using the expanded Latin ***id est*** in running text where the settled English abbreviation ***i.e.*** belongs, and — more importantly — the punctuation must match that form. A mid-sentence abbreviation takes a comma before it, and this one is expanded, so a second comma is required before the closing bracket or before the list. Two distinct lessons in one sentence: keep the conventional abbreviation rather than the museum-piece Latin, and let the position in the sentence decide the comma. A parenthetical short form such as "(*i.e.* rice)" would instead take a full stop before the bracket.

### 💡 Pro Tips

1. **Never translate on the question page.** Give yourself two seconds of recall first; only then check whether the literal words support your answer. The literal words are frequently a trap, and reading them first plants the trap.
2. **Learn the function before the expansion.** "*E.g.* introduces an instance" is the fact you will use; "*exempli gratia*" is only trivia that helps you remember it. Learn the job first, the etymology second.
3. **Group by source language, not alphabetically.** Latin for legal and academic prose, French for formal and diplomatic prose. Two focused lists beat one long list, because each list sits in a register you will meet.
4. **Practise the *bona fides* pair specifically.** It is the only entry here that changes grammatical class, and class-changing entries are the easiest things in the topic to build an error-spotting item around.
5. **Watch the Latin adverb pairs.** *A priori / a posteriori*, *de facto / de jure*, *in vitro / in vivo*, *per se / in situ*, *status quo / status quo ante*. Every one is a two-item set, and every set is worth one guaranteed mark if you know it.
6. **In the writing component, expand once and abbreviate after.** "that is (*i.e.*)" on first use, *i.e.* thereafter. It is correct academic practice and it protects you when you are unsure of punctuation.
7. **Keep a one-page "wrong sense" list.** Every time you meet a phrase in a passage and misread it, write the phrase, your wrong answer, and the right one. That page is a far better use of twenty minutes than adding twenty more phrases to a memorised list, because it targets the exact entries you are weak on.
8. **For a passage, list every phrase before answering.** A single comprehension passage can contain four or five of these, and the questions about them are clustered. You are not learning the phrases in advance; you are collecting them in one place, once, while you have the context that makes them memorable.
