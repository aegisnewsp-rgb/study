# Apply the geometry of conic sections in solving problems (91573) — NCEA Level 3 Calculus Notes

Achievement Standard 91573 is the first of fifteen NCEA Level 3 Mathematics (Calculus) standards. It is internally assessed, carries 3 credits, and tests the geometry of conic sections: circles, ellipses, parabolas, and hyperbolas. Conic sections appear throughout physics (orbital mechanics, projectile motion), engineering (lens design, satellite dishes), and first-year university mathematics. Most schools schedule 91573 in Term 1.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **3 credits**, internally assessed by the school, NZQA moderated.
- Topics: circles, ellipses, parabolas, hyperbolas; standard equations; foci, directrices, eccentricities; intersections of lines and conics.
- **Merit** requires relational thinking — identifying the conic from its equation, using the geometric properties to solve problems.
- **Excellence** requires extended abstract thinking — connecting algebraic form to geometric features, justifying method choice.

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing the foci of an ellipse with the directrices (foci are points inside the curve; directrices are lines outside).
- Sign errors in the standard forms of hyperbolas.
- Forgetting the discriminant condition for a conic (B² − 4AC determines the type for general quadratic curves).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Circle

Standard form: (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r²

Centre (h, k), radius r. The simplest conic; has constant sum of distances from any point on the circle to two fixed points (the foci, which coincide at the centre).

#### Ellipse

Standard form (horizontal): x²/a² + y²/b² = 1, where a > b > 0.

Centre (0, 0), vertices at (±a, 0), co-vertices at (0, ±b). Foci at (±c, 0) where c² = a² − b².

For a vertical ellipse: x²/b² + y²/a² = 1 (swap a and b).

Eccentricity e = c/a. The ellipse is more elongated as e approaches 1 and more circular as e approaches 0.

#### Parabola

Standard form (vertical, opening up): y = a(x − h)² + k

Vertex (h, k), axis of symmetry x = h, focus at (h, k + 1/(4a)), directrix y = k − 1/(4a).

The parabola has constant distance from any point on it to the focus (a point) and the directrix (a line). It is the shape of a satellite dish and the trajectory of a projectile (in the absence of air resistance).

#### Hyperbola

Standard form (horizontal): x²/a² − y²/b² = 1

Centre (0, 0), vertices at (±a, 0). Foci at (±c, 0) where c² = a² + b². Asymptotes y = ±(b/a)x.

For a vertical hyperbola: y²/a² − x²/b² = 1.

Eccentricity e = c/a. The hyperbola is more "open" as e approaches 1.

#### Identifying a conic from its equation

For the general quadratic Ax² + Bxy + Cy² + Dx + Ey + F = 0, the discriminant B² − 4AC determines the type:

- B² − 4AC < 0: ellipse (or circle if A = C and B = 0).
- B² − 4AC = 0: parabola.
- B² − 4AC > 0: hyperbola.

For Level 3 standard 91573, the equations are usually in standard form without the xy term, so B = 0 and the classification is simpler.

#### Geometric properties

**Foci** — fixed points such that the sum (ellipse) or difference (hyperbola) of distances is constant. For parabola, there is one focus.

**Directrix** — a fixed line such that the ratio of distance to focus to distance to directrix is the eccentricity e.

**Eccentricity** — e = c/a for ellipse and hyperbola; e = 1 for parabola; e = 0 for circle.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Why conics?

Conic sections are curves formed by intersecting a plane with a double cone. Each type corresponds to a different angle of the plane:

- Perpendicular to the axis: circle.
- Oblique to the axis (single intersection): ellipse.
- Parallel to a generator: parabola.
- Through both nappes: hyperbola.

The unifying algebraic feature: a general quadratic equation in x and y (with no higher powers) defines a conic.

#### Applications

- **Orbital mechanics:** planetary orbits are ellipses with the Sun at one focus (Kepler's first law).
- **Projectile motion:** the trajectory of a projectile (in the absence of air resistance) is a parabola.
- **Optics:** parabolic mirrors focus parallel rays to a single point (used in telescopes, headlights).
- **Satellite dishes:** parabolic shape focuses radio waves onto the receiver at the focus.
- **Hyperbolic cooling towers:** the hyperbola's properties make it an efficient shape for natural-draught cooling towers.

#### Method selection for intersections

To find the intersection of a line and a conic:

1. Substitute the line equation into the conic equation.
2. Solve for one variable.
3. Substitute back into the line equation to find the other.

The result is a quadratic (for ellipse/circle/hyperbola) or linear (for parabola), with 0, 1, or 2 solutions corresponding to the number of intersections.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Ellipse and circle are different." A circle is a special case of an ellipse with equal axes.
- "Parabola is y = x²." Yes for the standard form y = x², but parabolas can open in any direction and have any scale.
- "Hyperbola has no curve." It has two branches, one opening left/right, the other up/down.
- "Eccentricity is always less than 1." For ellipse, yes; for hyperbola, e > 1; for parabola, e = 1; for circle, e = 0.
