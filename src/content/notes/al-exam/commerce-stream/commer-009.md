# Banking and Financial Institutions

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Banking and Financial Institutions** in the A/L Commerce syllabus covers the structure of Sri Lanka's financial system: the **Central Bank of Sri Lanka (CBSL)**, **commercial banks** (Licensed Commercial Banks — LCBs, and Licensed Specialised Banks — LSBs), **development banks**, and **non-bank financial institutions (NBFIs)** such as Licensed Finance Companies (LFCs), leasing firms and insurance companies.

#### Must-know facts

- **CBSL** was established under the **Monetary Law Act No. 58 of 1949**. It issues currency, acts as banker to the government and to banks, regulates the system, and conducts monetary policy through the **Statutory Reserve Ratio (SRR)**, **Open Market Operations (OMO)**, the **Bank Rate** and the **Repo Rate**.
- **Commercial banks** accept deposits, advance loans, create credit and provide agency/transfer services.
- **Credit creation formula**: Total Credit = Initial Deposit × ((1 − LRR) / LRR); the **money multiplier** m = 1 / LRR.

> **Exam pointer:** Name Sri Lankan examples — BOC, People's Bank, HNB, NTB, DFCC, NDB — and cite the **Banking Act No. 30 of 1988** and **Finance Business Act No. 42 of 2011** when relevant.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### The financial-system structure in Sri Lanka

Sri Lanka's financial system has four tiers: (1) the **CBSL** at the apex as regulator and monetary authority, (2) **Licensed Commercial Banks (LCBs)** such as Bank of Ceylon, People's Bank, HNB and NTB that take demand deposits, (3) **Licensed Specialised Banks (LSBs)** such as DFCC Bank and National Development Bank that focus on development/term finance, and (4) **NBFIs** including LFCs, leasing companies, insurance companies and capital-market intermediaries. Only institutions licensed under the Banking Act may use the word "Bank".

#### Functions of commercial banks

| Function | Example |
|---|---|
| Accepting deposits | Current, savings, fixed deposits |
| Advancing loans | Overdraft, term loans, cash credit |
| Credit creation | Multiplier expansion of deposits |
| Agency services | Cheque collection, bill payment |
| Transfer of funds | SLIPS, CEFT, RTGS |
| Discounting bills | Trade-bill discounting |
| Issuing letters of credit | Import-export trade |

#### Role of the Central Bank of Sri Lanka

The CBSL operates under the **Monetary Law Act No. 58 of 1949**. Its core roles are: sole issuer of currency, banker to the government, **lender of last resort** to banks, supervisor of LCBs/LSBs/LFCs, custodian of foreign reserves, and conductor of monetary policy. It sets the **SRR** on rupee deposits, conducts **OMO** through government securities, and signals the policy stance via the **Repo Rate** and **Bank Rate**.

#### Credit creation (worked relationship)

When a new deposit of `D` enters a banking system with legal reserve ratio `LRR`, the **single-bank maximum credit** is `D × (1/LRR)`, while the **total system credit expansion** is `D × ((1 − LRR)/LRR)`. The **money multiplier** m = 1/LRR shows how many rupees of deposits one rupee of reserves supports. Worked: with D = LKR 1,000 and LRR = 0.20, total new credit = 1,000 × (0.80/0.20) = LKR 4,000, and the multiplier m = 5.

#### NBFIs and capital-market institutions

NBFIs include **Licensed Finance Companies** under the Finance Business Act No. 42 of 2011, **specialised leasing companies**, **pawnbrokers**, **insurance companies** (e.g. LIC, NLI), and unit trusts. Capital-market institutions are the **Colombo Stock Exchange (CSE)** (primary and secondary market) and the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** as regulator.

> **Common trap:** Students confuse CBSL with a commercial bank. CBSL does **not** accept deposits from the public and does **not** lend for trade — it lends only to banks and the government.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Commercial banks vs development banks

The two are distinguished by **funding horizon and clientele**, not by size alone. Commercial banks rely on **demand and short-term deposits**, advancing working-capital loans, overdrafts and trade finance. Development banks — such as **DFCC Bank** (originally the Development Finance Corporation of Ceylon, 1955) and the **National Development Bank (NDB)** — raise medium- and long-term funds through debentures, term loans and syndicated credit to finance projects in agriculture, infrastructure, manufacturing and SMEs. Today DFCC operates as an LSB under CBSL supervision; NDB is a licensed commercial-style development bank listed on the CSE.

#### Monetary-policy instruments in detail

#### Instruments

- **Open Market Operations (OMO):** CBSL buys/sells government securities to inject or absorb rupee liquidity permanently or temporarily.
- **Repo Rate:** the rate at which CBSL lends to banks against government securities; primary policy signal.
- **Bank Rate:** the rate at which CBSL discounts eligible bills; acts as a ceiling.
- **SRR:** the percentage of rupee deposits LCBs/LSBs must hold with CBSL; changing it directly alters the money multiplier (m = 1/LRR).

#### Recent reforms (high-yield for essays)

The **Banking Act No. 30 of 1988** was amended in 2021 to introduce **minimum core-capital ratios**, tighter governance and a resolution framework. The **Sri Lanka Deposit Insurance Scheme** (SLDIS) was strengthened, and CBSL consolidated dozens of weak LFCs, revoking licences of non-viable firms under the **Finance Business Act No. 42 of 2011**. These reforms are routinely tested as "recent developments" in essay questions.
