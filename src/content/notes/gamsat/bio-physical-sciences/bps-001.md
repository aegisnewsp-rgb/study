# Data Analysis and Graph Interpretation

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review

The Biological and Physical Sciences section is 75 multiple-choice questions, and ACER describes it as assessing "the ability to identify knowledge in new contexts, and analyse and interpret data". Read that wording literally. The section is not asking whether you memorised a fact — it is handing you a figure you have never seen and asking what it means.

Most lost marks here come from answering the question the graph was not asking. So spend the first few seconds on the frame before you read a single data point. Work out what sits on the x-axis and what sits on the y-axis, because questions deliberately swap the intuitive direction and a line that rises over time looks very different once "after treatment" is the vertical axis. Then read the units. Millimetres of mercury is not kilopascals, millimoles per litre is not milligrams per decilitre, and a proportion is not a percentage. Finally, check the scale, because a truncated axis exaggerates a small difference into a dramatic one, and a logarithmic axis means each equal step is an equal ratio rather than an equal amount.

**Before you read a single data point, fix the frame in this order.** Title, x-axis, y-axis, units, scale. Questions deliberately invert the intuitive direction, so a line rising over time reads completely differently once "after treatment" is the vertical axis.

| Frame check | What goes wrong if you skip it |
|---|---|
| Title | You answer the question the figure is not asking |
| x vs y axis | A rising line gets read as "more over time" when time is vertical |
| Units | mmHg read as kPa; mmol/L read as mg/dL; a proportion read as a percentage |
| Scale | A truncated axis inflates a small difference into a dramatic one |
| Log scale | Each equal step is an equal ratio, not an equal amount |

**Six shapes carry most of the section.**

- Straight line — constant rate of change; once the slope bends, the linear model buys nothing
- Steepening curve — exponential; doubling time, growth rate, compounding versus a fixed addition
- Curve flattening to an asymptote — saturation, and the shape that matters most in physiology
- Two peaks, or a step — something categorical, not continuous: two mixed populations, or a threshold
- Fall slowing toward a floor — decline flattening; asymptotic at zero, or a model pushed outside its range
- Error bars — precision versus uncertainty; heavy overlap means the groups have not been shown to differ

**The saturation shape is the one to know cold.** Enzyme rate against substrate concentration plateaus when active sites are occupied, so more substrate buys nothing and the limiter becomes enzyme concentration, temperature, pH or cofactor availability. Competitive inhibitors shift that curve right; non-competitive inhibitors lower the maximum rate. Oxygen transport, receptor saturation and drug absorption all show the same shape.

**Correlation is not mechanism.** Before accepting a causal reading, ask what else is driving both arms (salt intake and blood pressure both rise with age), whether causation runs the other way (a drug cleared slowly reaches a higher level *and* causes the toxicity that slowed its clearance), and whether the mechanism is even physically possible in the time available. If it could not produce the effect, the correlation is wrong however clean the lines look.

**Discard any option that assumes a variable the graph does not show.** You cannot have concluded something from a figure that does not display it.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study

#### The six patterns that cover most questions

A straight line means a constant rate of change. If the slope alters, the relationship has stopped being linear and the straight-line model buys you nothing beyond that point.

A curve that steepens is exponential, and doubling time, growth rate and the question of whether a population is compounding or being added to at a fixed number all live in that shape.

A curve that flattens toward an asymptote is saturating. This is the one that matters most in physiology. Enzyme reaction rate plotted against substrate concentration is the classic case, and the plateau means the enzyme is saturated and further substrate buys nothing.

Two peaks, or a step rather than a curve, tells you something categorical rather than continuous. A bimodal distribution means two populations are mixed together. A step marks a threshold or a boundary — the thing being measured does not fade in, it switches on.

A fall that slows over time and tends towards a floor is a decline that flattens. If it approaches zero, it is asymptotic. If it crosses and keeps heading negative, the model has been pushed outside the range where it holds and the right answer is usually that the model no longer applies.

Error bars are about honesty rather than height. A tight error bar means the estimate is precise; a wide one means the estimate is uncertain no matter how far it sits from a reference line. Two groups whose error bars overlap heavily have not been shown to differ, however tidy the separation of their means looks.

#### Correlation is not mechanism

Two curves rising together tells you the variables are associated. It does not tell you why, and the exam will not reward you for skipping that step.

Before accepting a causal reading, ask what else is going on. A third variable can drive both arms of the relationship — salt intake and blood pressure both rise with age, which says nothing about salt. Or the causation can run the other way entirely: a drug that is cleared slowly reaches a higher measured level and also causes the toxicity that slowed its clearance in the first place. And when the mechanism is physically impossible, the correlation has to be wrong no matter how clean the lines look. If the proposed cause could not possibly produce the proposed effect in the time available, reject it.

#### Worked pattern: the physiology question you will meet

A question shows reaction rate rising steeply as substrate concentration increases, then levelling off. What does the plateau tell you?

- The enzyme is saturated — essentially all active sites are occupied.
- Adding more substrate will not increase the rate.
- Something other than substrate is now limiting: enzyme concentration, temperature, pH, or cofactor availability.
- This is also why competitive inhibitors shift the curve right (more substrate needed to reach the same rate) while non-competitive inhibitors lower the maximum rate instead.

You will meet this shape repeatedly. Reaction rate against substrate concentration is one, but so is oxygen transport, receptor saturation and drug absorption. Learn the shape once and it turns up in every section.

#### A five-step routine

Read the title, then the axes, then the units, then the scale, in that order — before the question. Describe the shape in a single sentence. Ask what the shape physically means rather than what it happens to correlate with, and only then look at the options, discarding anything that contradicts the shape you described. Finally, if an option assumes a variable that is not on the graph, that option is wrong: you cannot have concluded something from a figure that does not show it.

#### Common mistakes

The usual failure is reading the direction of the line instead of its shape, so a saturating curve gets treated as if it were linear and you extrapolate a plateau that will never lift. The second is confusing "no significant difference" with "no difference measured". The third is reading a truncated axis as evidence of a large effect.

Beyond those, it is easy to accept a causal claim because a graph shows two things moving together, and easy to spend so long admiring the figure that you miss the units and answer confidently in the wrong scale.

### 🔴 Extended — Mastery

#### A truncated axis is a claim, not a formatting choice
The same data drawn on a broken axis produces a dramatic-looking separation that reverses as soon as the axis is restored. When an option rests on "these groups differ markedly", test whether the gap survives the axis. Two lines that look far apart on a truncated scale are frequently overlapping once the scale is honest. This is also why a log axis confuses ratio with amount: on a log scale equal vertical steps are equal ratios, so a doubling reads as one step whether it is 1 to 2 or 100 to 200.

#### Error bars answer a different question from the mean
A tight error bar means the estimate is precise; a wide one means it is uncertain no matter where the mean sits relative to a reference line. The examable consequence is that two groups whose error bars overlap heavily have not been shown to differ, however tidy the separation of their means looks. Students routinely read a wide bar as "a big effect, just noisy", which reverses the meaning entirely.

#### The saturation plateau is the same physics in every subject
Once you recognise that a plateau means the system has run out of capacity, the same reading transfers across sections:

| System | What is saturating | What the plateau means |
|---|---|---|
| Enzyme kinetics | Active sites | Substrate is no longer the limiting factor |
| Receptor binding | Binding sites | Occupancy is near maximum; effect plateaus |
| Oxygen transport | Haemoglobin loading | Delivery is capped by carrying capacity |
| Drug absorption | Transporter capacity | Absorption rate no longer scales with dose |

Because the same shape recurs, learning it once in enzyme kinetics pays for itself in physiology, pharmacology and biology without further revision.

#### Where the shape is genuinely ambiguous, say what it rules out
Two of the six shapes are often mistaken for each other under exam pressure. A curve that flattens toward a floor approaching zero is an asymptotic decline, whereas a fall that crosses and keeps heading negative has been pushed outside the range where the model holds — in that second case the correct answer is usually that the model no longer applies, not a numerical extrapolation. Bimodal distributions similarly carry two populations rather than one variable with high variance, which is why a bimodal figure cannot be summarised by a single mean.

#### Five steps, and the one that saves the most marks
Read the title, then the axes, then the units, then the scale, in that order, before the question. Describe the shape in one sentence. Ask what the shape physically means rather than what it happens to correlate with. Only then read the options, discarding anything that contradicts the sentence you wrote. Finally, discard any option that assumes a variable not on the graph. Under time pressure the commonest failure is skipping to the options before describing the shape, which is why so many marks are lost to confident answers in the wrong scale.
