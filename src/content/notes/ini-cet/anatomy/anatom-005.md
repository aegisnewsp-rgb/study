# Upper Limb Anatomy — Bones, Joints, Muscles, Nerves and Clinical Correlations

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

The upper limb contains **30 bones** per side — clavicle + scapula (shoulder girdle), humerus, radius (lateral/thumb side) + ulna (medial), 8 carpals arranged in two rows, 5 metacarpals, and 14 phalanges. The **brachial plexus (C5–T1)** organises as 5 roots → 3 trunks → 6 divisions → 3 cords → 5 terminal branches (musculocutaneous, axillary, radial, median, ulnar), and AIIMS questions routinely test which cord yields which nerve.

> Mnemonic for cords → nerves: **"LML / M / P"** — Lateral cord = Musculocutaneous + Lateral root of median; Medial cord = Ulnar + Medial root of median; Posterior cord = Axillary + Radial.

- **Surgical neck humerus fracture** → axillary nerve (C5–C6) → deltoid paralysis, regimental badge anaesthesia.
- **Midshaft humerus fracture** → radial nerve (spiral groove) → wrist drop.
- **Medial epicondyle fracture** → ulnar nerve → claw hand.
- **Scaphoid fracture (FOOSH)** → tenderness in anatomical snuffbox → risk of AVN of proximal fragment.
- **Erb's palsy (C5–C6)** → "waiter's tip" deformity; **Klumpke's (C8–T1)** → claw hand.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Skeletal framework and joints
The shoulder girdle (clavicle + scapula) articulates with the axial skeleton only at the **sternoclavicular joint** (the only true synovial joint connecting upper limb to trunk). The **glenohumeral joint** sacrifices bony congruence for mobility — its stability depends on the rotator cuff, glenoid labrum, and capsular ligaments. The elbow is a compound joint: **humeroulnar (hinge) + humeroradial (gliding) + proximal radioulnar (pivot)**, with a carrying angle of ~5–10° in males and ~10–15° in females.

| Region | Bones | Key joint | Nerve at risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shoulder girdle | Clavicle, scapula | Sternoclavicular, acromioclavicular | Suprascapular n. |
| Arm | Humerus | Glenohumeral | Axillary (surgical neck), Radial (spiral groove) |
| Forearm | Radius (thumb side), ulna | Humeroulnar + radioulnar | Median (ant. interosseous) |
| Wrist/hand | Scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum, pisiform; trapezium, trapezoid, capitate, hamate; 5 metacarpals; 14 phalanges | Radiocarpal, midcarpal, CMC, MCP, IP | Median (carpal tunnel), Ulnar (Guyon's) |

#### Brachial plexus and clinical correlations
The plexus travels from the posterior triangle through the axilla. The **lateral cord** gives musculocutaneous and the lateral root of median; the **medial cord** gives ulnar and medial root of median; the **posterior cord** gives axillary and radial. The long thoracic nerve (C5–C7) arises *before* the trunks and innervates serratus anterior — injury produces a **winged scapula** on pushing against resistance.

> Cubital fossa contents (medial → lateral): **M**edian nerve, **B**rachial artery, **B**iceps tendon — Mnemonic "**M**arilyn **B**urns the **B**acon."

- Erb-Duchenne palsy (C5–C6) follows shoulder traction/birth injury; limb hangs adducted, internally rotated, forearm pronated — "waiter's tip."
- Klumpke's palsy (C8–T1) follows upward traction (e.g. grabbing a branch); produces intrinsic hand wasting and **claw hand**.
- Carpal tunnel syndrome: median nerve compressed under flexor retinaculum; **recurrent (motor) branch is spared** because it lies outside the retinaculum — thenar eminence is therefore preserved in pure CTS (adductor pollicis, supplied by ulnar, also spared).

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge cases and trap questions
INI CET examiners reward candidates who distinguish look-alike structures. The **recurrent branch of the median nerve** lies *extrinsic* to the flexor retinaculum and innervates the three thenar muscles — its preservation in CTS explains why severe cases show thenar wasting only late, never as the presenting sign. The **anatomical snuffbox** has abductor pollicis longus + extensor pollicis brevis radially and extensor pollicis longus ulnarly, with the **scaphoid** as floor; a missed scaphoid fracture on initial X-ray is a classic litigation scenario.

| Injury / lesion | Site | Deformity / sign | Nerve or root |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Surgical neck humerus fracture | Surgical neck | Deltoid paralysis, regimental-badge anaesthesia | Axillary n. (C5–C6) |
| Midshaft humerus fracture | Spiral (radial) groove | Wrist drop | Radial n. |
| Supracondylar fracture (child) | Supracondylar ridge | Hand of benediction / anterior interosseous palsy | Median n. |
| Medial epicondyle fracture | Cubital tunnel | Claw hand (4th–5th digits) | Ulnar n. |
| FOOSH on outstretched hand | Scaphoid waist | Snuffbox tenderness, AVN of proximal pole | Scaphoid |
| Erb's palsy | Upper trunk C5–C6 | Waiter's tip | Upper trunk |

#### Mechanism in plain steps
A **FOOSH** drives the scaphoid waist, but because the **scaphoid blood supply enters distally** (dorsal carpal branch of radial artery), the proximal fragment is devascularised and at risk of non-union. For rotator cuff testing, examiners map each muscle to a movement: supraspinatus (empty-can, first 15° of abduction), infraspinatus and teres minor (external rotation, lag sign), subscapularis (lift-off / belly-press test). **Teres major is NOT in the cuff**, although both teres muscles are external rotators — this is a frequent MCQ trap.

> High-yield AIIMS framing: image-based questions show cubital fossa, anatomical snuffbox, or a deformity (wrist drop, claw hand, waiter's tip) and ask for the affected nerve/root — answer in one line with the root value.

#### Practice prompts
1. A 22-year-old falls from a motorbike onto his right shoulder. Examination reveals inability to abduct the arm beyond 15° and loss of sensation over the lower deltoid. Identify the injured nerve and the most likely fracture site.
2. A newborn delivered with shoulder dystocia holds the affected upper limb adducted, pronated, with the palm facing backwards. Which roots are injured, and what is the eponymous name of this deformity?
