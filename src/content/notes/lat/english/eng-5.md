# Sentence Structure and Grammar

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Sentence structure and grammar tests how words combine into phrases, clauses, and sentences to produce precise meaning — the spine of the English section in LAT (HEC Pakistan). Every LAT English MCQ rewards clarity, parallelism, and correct agreement, so locking the five basic patterns and four sentence types removes most guesswork.

- **Five patterns**: S–V, S–V–O, S–V–IO–DO, S–V–O–OC, S–V–S — identify each before choosing a correction.
- **Four structural types**: simple, compound, complex, compound-complex — each demands a specific conjunction behaviour.
- **High-yield rules**: subject–verb agreement (ignore intervening phrases), pronoun–antecedent agreement, parallelism, modifier placement, and punctuation (comma, semicolon, colon, apostrophe).

| Pattern | Example | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| S–V | *The court adjourned.* | Intransitive statement |
| S–V–O | *Lawyers argue cases.* | Action on object |
| S–V–IO–DO | *She gave the clerk the file.* | Indirect + direct object |
| S–V–O–OC | *They called him guilty.* | Object + complement |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Parts of Speech and Sentence Frames

Every word in a LAT sentence belongs to one of eight parts of speech: noun, pronoun, verb, adjective, adverb, preposition, conjunction, or interjection, with determiners (a, the, this) functioning as noun markers. The sentence's backbone is the **subject** (what the sentence is about) and the **predicate** (what is said about it). Recognizing the finite verb — the one carrying tense and agreement — is the fastest way to locate the subject.

#### Four Sentence Types by Structure

Simple sentences carry one independent clause. Compound sentences join two independent clauses with a coordinating conjunction (and, but, or, nor, for, yet, so) or a semicolon. Complex sentences add a dependent (subordinate) clause introduced by a subordinating conjunction (because, although, while, if). Compound-complex sentences combine both — at least two independent clauses plus at least one dependent one.

#### Agreement and Reference

The finite verb must match its subject in number and person, regardless of phrases that intervene. "The box of chocolates is on the table" is correct because *box* controls the verb. Pronouns must agree with their antecedents in number, gender, and person, and their reference must be unambiguous — a frequent LAT error-spotting target.

#### Parallelism and Modifiers

Coordinated elements coordinated by *and*, *or*, *both…and*, *either…or*, or *not only…but also* must share the same grammatical form. A misplaced or dangling modifier produces a logically absurd meaning, often the only diagnostic clue in a tricky MCQ.

| Error Type | Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subject–verb agreement | *The list of items are long.* | *The list of items is long.* |
| Dangling modifier | *Walking to court, the rain started.* | *Walking to court, she felt the rain start.* |
| Comma splice | *He appealed, the judge dismissed it.* | *He appealed; the judge dismissed it.* |

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Voice, Tense, and Legal Precision

LAT English passages favour the active voice because legal arguments depend on identifying the agent: "The counsel objected" is sharper than "An objection was made by the counsel." Passive voice is acceptable only when the agent is unknown, irrelevant, or deliberately obscured. Tense consistency across clauses is equally important; shifting mid-sentence between past and present simple weakens the reasoning and is treated as an error in sentence-improvement items.

#### Punctuation as Meaning

A comma joins coordinate elements or sets off non-restrictive modifiers; a semicolon links two independent clauses without a conjunction; a colon introduces a clause, list, or explanation that completes the preceding independent clause; an apostrophe marks possession or the omission in contractions. Misusing any of these corrupts meaning — a common trap in jumbled-sentence rearrangement.

#### Edge Cases and LAT Traps

1. **Collective nouns** (*jury, committee, board*) take a singular verb when the group acts as a unit and a plural when members act individually.
2. **Who vs. whom** — *who* is the subject form; *whom* is the object form. In legal English, *whom* often follows a preposition.
3. **Correlative conjunctions** require the same grammatical structure after each half: *not only a lawyer but also a scholar* (not *not only a lawyer but also being a scholar*).
4. **Reduced relative clauses** (e.g., *The lawyer appointed yesterday…*) can dangle if the implied subject is wrong.

#### Worked Micro-Example

**Identify the error:** *"Each of the witnesses have testified, and their accounts differs."*
**Correction:** *"Each of the witnesses has testified, and their accounts differ."*
The subject of the first clause is the singular pronoun *each*, so the verb must be *has*; the second clause treats *accounts* as a plural subject, so *differ* is required, not *differs*.

#### Practice Prompts

1. Reclassify the following as simple, compound, complex, or compound-complex, and justify with one clause marker: *"Although the counsel argued passionately, the bench reserved its judgment, and the audience waited in silence."*
2. Rewrite the sentence fixing agreement, parallelism, and voice: *"The report was written by the junior associate, it lacked clarity, and was not only incomplete but also being inaccurate."*
