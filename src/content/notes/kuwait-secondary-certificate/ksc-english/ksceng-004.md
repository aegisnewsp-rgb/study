# Unit 10 — Pushing the Limits — Kuwait Secondary Certificate English Notes

Unit 10 of the Kuwait MOE Grade 12 "Over To You" textbook (second term, 29 March to 16 April 2026) is "Pushing the Limits" — a thematic unit on human endurance, achievement, and surpassing boundaries. The reading input is a report about a Kuwaiti climber. The reading includes an interview. The unit's grammar focus is the past perfect simple and continuous. Vocabulary covers phrasal verbs with "come" and adjectives beginning with "a". The writing focus is a response to an advert (persuasive writing). Speaking focus is making difficult choices, explaining a choice, being persuasive, and agreeing and disagreeing.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Past perfect simple:** "I had finished the climb by the time the sun set." Used for an action completed before another past event.
- **Past perfect continuous:** "I had been climbing for six hours when the storm hit." Used for a continuous action leading up to another past event.
- **Phrasal verbs with "come":** come up (arise), come across (find by chance), come up with (think of), come back (return), come out (emerge), come over (visit), come into (inherit), come around (recover, change opinion).
- **Adjectives beginning with "a":** ambitious, adaptable, affable, agile, attentive, authentic, awesome, austere, avid, aware.
- **Persuasive writing structure:** hook, claim, three reasons with evidence, call to action.
- **Language of agreeing/disagreeing:** "I see your point, however..." / "That's a valid argument, but..." / "I respectfully disagree because..."

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing past simple and past perfect — past perfect only when one past event is before another.
- "Come" vs "go" in phrasal verbs — "come up with an idea" (think of), "go ahead" (proceed). They are not interchangeable.
- Overusing intensifiers in persuasive writing ("very," "extremely," "absolutely") weakens the argument.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Past perfect simple vs past perfect continuous

Both describe actions completed before a reference point in the past. The past perfect simple focuses on the completion of an action; the past perfect continuous focuses on the duration.

- "I had read the book (before the exam)." (simple — completed action)
- "I had been reading the book (for three hours when the phone rang)." (continuous — duration up to a point)

Choose the simple form when:

- The action is completed and the result matters.
- The action happens once or at a specific time.
- The action is sudden or brief.

Choose the continuous form when:

- The duration of the action matters.
- The action is ongoing up to the reference point.
- The action is interrupted by another event.

For combined use: "I had been reading for an hour when I had finished the chapter." (continuous for duration, simple for completion).

#### Phrasal verbs with "come"

Each phrasal verb with "come" is a fixed multi-word verb whose meaning is idiomatic. Learn each one as a unit:

- "Come up" — arise, happen (a problem came up).
- "Come across" — find by chance (I came across an old photo).
- "Come up with" — think of, propose (She came up with a great idea).
- "Come back" — return (He came back the next day).
- "Come out" — emerge, be published (The truth came out).
- "Come over" — visit casually (Come over for dinner).
- "Come into" — inherit (She came into a fortune).
- "Come around" — recover, change opinion (He came around to my view).
- "Come about" — happen (How did this come about?).

The verb is intransitive (no direct object) in most of these — passives construction with "be" is needed: "The problem came up" → "A problem was come up with" is wrong; it is "A problem came up" or "We came up with a problem" (transitive with "come up with").

#### Adjectives beginning with "a"

This vocabulary group is examined on Kuwait terminal English papers. Memorise a working set:

- **ambitious** — having strong desire to succeed.
- **adaptable** — able to adjust to new conditions.
- **affable** — friendly, easy to talk to.
- **agile** — able to move quickly and easily.
- **attentive** — paying careful attention.
- **authentic** — genuine, real.
- **austere** — severe, lacking luxury.
- **avid** — very enthusiastic.
- **aware** — having knowledge of.

Each has its own register and collocations. Use them in speaking and writing when the meaning matches — avoid forcing them into contexts where a more common word fits better.

#### Persuasive writing structure

A persuasive text (advert response, opinion article, letter to the editor) has the following structure:

1. **Hook** — a striking fact, question, or quote that engages the reader.
2. **Claim** — state your position clearly and confidently.
3. **Reason 1** — first supporting reason, with evidence (a statistic, an example, an expert opinion).
4. **Reason 2** — second supporting reason, with evidence.
5. **Counter-argument + rebuttal** — acknowledge the opposing view fairly, then refute it.
6. **Call to action** — tell the reader what to do, think, or feel next.

Use persuasive language: rhetorical questions, repetition, parallelism ("We must act. We must decide. We must lead."), direct address ("you," "we"), and strong verbs. Avoid name-calling and unsupported assertions — they weaken the argument.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Where Unit 10 appears across the terminal English paper

- **Reading comprehension:** an interview, report, or biographical text on an achievement or challenge, with vocabulary items drawn from the unit.
- **Grammar items:** past-perfect tense transformations (past simple → past perfect, or past perfect continuous).
- **Writing item:** a persuasive letter or advert response.
- **Vocabulary items:** phrasal verbs with "come" in context, adjectives beginning with "a".

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Past perfect always means before now." No — past perfect means before a reference point in the past, not before the present.
- "Phrasal verbs can be understood word-by-word." No — they are idiomatic. "Come across" does not mean "come + across the street".
- "Persuasive writing needs flowery language." No — clarity, evidence, and structure make a persuasive text. Long adjectives and adverbs often weaken the argument.

#### Exam technique

- For past-perfect transformations, identify the earlier action first — that becomes the past perfect; the later action stays as past simple.
- For phrasal-verb items, consider the surrounding context — the meaning is rarely derivable from the verb + particle alone.
- For persuasive writing, plan before writing — list the three reasons and the evidence for each before drafting paragraphs.
