# MM7 Integration

MM7 is the reverse of MM6, and the specification says so in a way worth reading twice. It sets out seven requirements: the area interpretation and the warning that a definite integral is not automatically an area, integration of $x^n$ for rational $n \ne -1$ after any needed simplification, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus in two forms, combining integrals with equal or contiguous ranges, the trapezium rule with its error direction, and solving differential equations of the form $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = f(x)$.

Two things the specification rules out are worth stating up front, because they tell you where not to spend time. It never asks for a numerical value of an integral, and it does not require partial fractions — MM7.2 stays with powers of $x$ and sums and differences of them, simplified first if necessary.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> Integration on this paper is: find an antiderivative, then evaluate at the two limits and subtract.

| Symbol | Name | What it means | Where it bites |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\displaystyle\int$ | Indefinite integral | A family of antiderivatives | Needs $+C$; drop it and mark is lost |
| $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ | Definite integral | A signed number | $F(b) - F(a)$, in that order |
| $F'(x) = f(x)$ | FTC part 1 | Antiderivatives differ by a constant | $F$ any one antiderivative |
| $\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt = f(x)$ | FTC part 2 | Differentiating an upper limit | Upper limit becomes the variable |
| trapezium rule | Numerical area | $\tfrac{h}{2}\big[f_0 + 2f_1 + \cdots + 2f_{n-1} + f_n\big]$ | Direction of the error |

**The rule you need on a loop.** For rational $n \ne -1$:

$$\int x^n \, dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C$$

Raise the power by one, divide by the new power. That is the whole of MM7.2 once the expression has been tidied.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### A definite integral is not automatically an area

This is the specification's own warning in MM7.1 and it catches people every year. Take $y = x^2 - 4x$ between $x = 0$ and $x = 2$:

$$\int_0^2 (x^2 - 4x)\,dx = \left[\frac{x^3}{3} - 2x^2\right]_0^2 = \frac{8}{3} - 8 = -\frac{16}{3}$$

The curve lies **below** the axis on that interval, so the answer is negative. The geometric area between the curve and the axis is $\tfrac{16}{3}$. Signed area cancels; area does not. Whenever the question says *area*, split the integral at every point where the curve crosses the axis and take the absolute value of each piece.

#### Integration after simplification

MM7.2 says expressions may require simplification before integrating, and fractions are the usual culprit. The job is to get every term into the shape $x^n$, or $1/x$, before you integrate anything term by term:

$$\int \frac{x^3 - 4x}{x^2} \, dx = \int \left(x - \frac{4}{x}\right) dx = \frac{x^2}{2} - 4\log x + C$$

Rational powers also collapse. $\int 8x^{-1/3}\,dx = 8 \cdot \frac{x^{2/3}}{2/3} = 12x^{2/3}$. The $n = -1$ case is the one exception the specification flags, and on this paper it belongs to the logarithmic family rather than to the power rule.

#### The Fundamental Theorem, both ways

**Part one.** If $F'(x) = f(x)$ then $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)$. For $\int_1^3 2x\,dx$, take $F(x) = x^2$: $F(3) - F(1) = 9 - 1 = 8$.

**Part two.** $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\right] = f(x)$. The power of the FTC that surprises people: the integrand is written in a dummy variable $t$, and the result substitutes the upper limit back in. Evaluate $\int_0^x t^2\,dt = \dfrac{x^3}{3}$ and differentiate: $x^2$. Which is exactly what you must have expected.

#### Combining integrals

MM7.4 gives you the two identities you need, and both fall out of the meaning of a definite integral as an accumulation.

Equal ranges add: $\int_2^5 f(x)\,dx + \int_2^5 g(x)\,dx = \int_2^5 \big(f(x) + g(x)\big)\,dx$.

Contiguous ranges join up, provided the limits are in the right order. Reversing the limits changes the sign, which is the step that makes this work:

$$\int_4^2 f(x)\,dx + \int_3^4 f(x)\,dx = -\int_2^4 f(x)\,dx + \int_3^4 f(x)\,dx$$

Now $\int_2^4 f = \int_2^3 f + \int_3^4 f$, so the two terms cancel and what is left is $-\int_2^3 f = \int_3^2 f$. The specification writes this as $\int_3^2 f(x)\,dx$, and the identity is worth being able to run in either direction.

#### The trapezium rule and its error direction

Split $[a,b]$ into $n$ equal strips of width $h = \tfrac{b-a}{n}$ and read off the values $f_0, f_1, \dots, f_n$ at the division points:

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx \frac{h}{2}\left[f_0 + 2f_1 + 2f_2 + \cdots + 2f_{n-1} + f_n\right]$$

The interior values are doubled because each one is shared by two strips. Check it on $\int_0^2 x^2\,dx$ with $n = 2$, so $h = 1$ and $f_0 = 0, f_1 = 1, f_2 = 4$:

$$\frac{1}{2}\left[0 + 2(1) + 4\right] = 3$$

The exact value is $\tfrac{8}{3} \approx 2.67$, so $3$ is an **overestimate**. That direction is not a coin toss. A curve bending upwards (concave up) sits above its chords, so the trapezium rule overestimates; a curve bending downwards gives an underestimate. Linear functions are the fixed point — every trapezium is exact.

#### Solving $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = f(x)$

MM7.6 is the smallest strand and the easiest to forget. Integrate both sides — the constant of integration becomes the unknown you solve for using the given condition. If $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 3x^2$ and $y = 1$ when $x = 2$:

$$y = x^3 + C, \qquad 1 = 8 + C, \qquad C = -7$$

Check by differentiating: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^3 - 7) = 3x^2$ ✓. Nothing about this strand needs separation of variables, because the specification restricts it to $f(x)$ alone.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (3mo+)

#### Why the antiderivative exists at all

The whole family rests on MM7.3: if you can differentiate, some function has you as its derivative, and every other antiderivative differs from it by a constant. That is why $C$ appears once and not once per term, why definite integrals can drop it entirely, and why the trapezium rule converges to the definite integral as the strip width shrinks — the error per strip is proportional to how much the curve bends, and the bend is $O(h^2)$ per strip while the number of strips grows like $1/h$.

#### Symmetry is worth checking before integrating

The contiguous-ranges identity has a pleasant consequence. If $f$ is symmetric about $x = c$ on $[a,b]$, then $\int_a^b f = 2\int_c^b f$, which halves the work. And if $f$ is odd about the midpoint, $\int_a^b f = 0$ with no integration at all. Recognising this is the difference between a two-line answer and a four-line one.

#### Where the routine runs out

Three situations break the standard template. An integrand that needs rewriting — long division, or splitting a fraction, or cancelling a factor before the powers become integratable — will defeat anyone who applies the power rule immediately. An area question where the curve crosses the axis twice defeats anyone who integrates once. And a numerical area question where exact integration is impossible defeats anyone who does not know the trapezium rule; conversely, a question where the trapezium rule is *prohibited* by context defeats anyone who reaches for it when the exact answer was available.

### Common mistakes

Forgetting $+C$ on an indefinite integral.

Writing $F(a) - F(b)$ instead of $F(b) - F(a)$.

Reporting a negative value as an *area* when the curve lies below the axis.

Forgetting that reversing the limits changes the sign, which is what makes contiguous ranges work.

Dropping the factor of 2 on the interior ordinates in the trapezium rule, or using $h/2$ when the strip count is $n$ rather than $n-1$.

Asserting the trapezium rule always overestimates — it depends on which way the curve bends.

Forgetting the constant when solving $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = f(x)$ and then being unable to use the given condition.

Trying to integrate $1/x$ with the power rule.
