# Craft and Structure — Words in Context — SAT R&W Notes

Words in Context is part of the SAT R&W Craft and Structure content domain. It tests the candidate's ability to determine the meaning of high-utility academic words and phrases in context, including figurative and ambiguous language, connotation, and the effect of word choice on tone and meaning.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Vocabulary in context:** determining the meaning of a word from the surrounding text.
- **Connotation:** the emotional or evaluative load of a word (positive, negative, neutral).
- **Tone:** the author's attitude (serious, humorous, ironic, etc.).
- **Figurative language:** words used non-literally (metaphor, simile, personification).
- **Skills tested:** vocabulary, nuance, tone, figurative meaning.

#### Examiner traps

- Choosing a dictionary definition instead of the contextual meaning.
- Confusing connotation (positive/negative) with denotation (literal meaning).

---

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Vocabulary in context

To determine the meaning of a word in context:

1. Read the sentence containing the word.
2. Read the surrounding sentences for clues.
3. Identify the context (positive, negative, formal, informal).
4. Choose the meaning that fits the context.

#### Connotation vs denotation

- **Denotation:** the literal meaning of a word.
- **Connotation:** the emotional or evaluative load.

Example: "skinny" (negative connotation) vs "slim" (positive connotation) vs "thin" (neutral denotation). All three have similar denotations but different connotations.

#### Tone

The author's tone is their attitude toward the subject. To identify tone:

1. Look for evaluative language ("unfortunately," "remarkably," "I believe").
2. Consider the connotations of key words.
3. Choose the tone that fits the passage.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Where Words in Context appears across the SAT R&W section

Words in Context items appear across the SAT Reading and Writing section. They test vocabulary and nuance at a level higher than simple recall.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "The dictionary definition is always right." No — the contextual meaning is what matters.
- "Connotation is the same as tone." No — connotation is a property of words; tone is a property of the passage.

#### Exam technique

- For vocabulary-in-context questions, read the surrounding sentences.
- For connotation questions, compare the emotional load of options.
- For tone questions, look for evaluative language.
