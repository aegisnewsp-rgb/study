# Basic Chemistry

The Department of Examinations schedules Science in a split form in the 2026 timetable: Paper II from 08:30 to 11:40 and Paper I from 13:00 to 14:00. The long paper comes first in the day and the short paper in the afternoon, which is worth knowing in advance because the two papers behave differently — the 3 hour 10 minute paper rewards coverage, the one-hour paper rewards precision on a narrow set of facts.

Basic chemistry is the highest-density block in that syllabus. Almost every other topic in O/L science depends on being able to read a formula, write a balanced equation and reason about a particle.

## Matter, and the particle model

Solids, liquids and gases are distinguished by particle arrangement and by the strength of the forces between particles, not by particle size. Melting breaks some forces, boiling breaks all of them, and dissolving separates particles without breaking them at all — which is why a solution is not a new substance.

A change of state is physical; a new substance is chemical. That test alone sorts most of the classification questions.

## Elements, compounds and mixtures

An element contains one type of atom. A compound contains atoms of two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio, and it has entirely different properties from its elements — that is the classic demonstration of which students misremember. A mixture keeps the properties of its parts and can be separated physically.

Writing formulas: the number in a formula is a ratio, not an amount of substance. $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ are different compounds with different properties, and the difference is one oxygen atom per molecule.

## Equations and the reactivity series

Balance by changing coefficients only, never subscripts — putting a coefficient inside a polyatomic ion that cancels is the shortcut that saves time until it does not.

The reactivity series orders metals by how readily they lose electrons. Higher means more reactive, and the consequences run consistently: a metal higher in the series displaces a metal below it from its salt solution; a metal above hydrogen reacts with dilute acid to give hydrogen; a metal above carbon needs electrolysis to extract from its oxide.

The opposite error is the commonest: saying a metal reacts with water when it needs steam, or saying iron reacts with dilute sulfuric acid when it displaces copper. Read the specific conditions.

## Acids, alkalis and salts

Neutralisation is acid plus base to give salt and water, and it is double displacement in every case: swap the partners. The pH scale runs 0 to 14 with 7 neutral at ordinary temperature, and the strength of an acid or alkali is not the same as its pH — a dilute strong acid can have a higher pH than a concentrated weak one.

Titration gives you the concentration of one solution from another. Read the question carefully about which is in the flask and which is in the burette, because the roles reverse the arithmetic direction, and average the titres from the concordant runs rather than using the first.

## Common mistakes

Confusing a mixture with a compound. If the properties of the parts survive, it is a mixture.

Treating the formula number as a mass. $\text{CuSO}_4\cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$ is one formula unit, not five.

Using the reactivity series without the conditions — steam versus water, and the position of hydrogen in the series, are what decide whether a reaction happens at all.

Forgetting the sign and unit on a titration answer.

Calling neutralisation a combination reaction. It is double displacement; combination is the one where one product forms.

Skipping the indicator or using the wrong one at the endpoint in a titration question.
