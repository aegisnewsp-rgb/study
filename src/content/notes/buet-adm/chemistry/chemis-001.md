# Safe Use of Laboratory — BUET Admission Test

Safe laboratory use is the practical side of chemistry: reading labels, selecting PPE, controlling heat and ignition, segregating incompatible reagents, and choosing a safe waste route. NCTB HSC Chemistry approaches it through chemical symbols, GHS pictograms, and the acidic, basic, oxidising, or reducing behaviour of common reagents. First-year chemistry syllabus and laboratory work add dilution of concentrated acids, flammable-liquid storage, residue disposal, and emergency routines. In BUET preparation, this material sits inside the compulsory written quiz and any chemistry practical or viva component, not as a separate block. It earns its place through a handful of single-mark accuracy questions, where one careless word about a pictogram or extinguisher can lose the mark.

> Verify the live syllabus, paper pattern, and any in-year changes on https://buet.ac.bd/ before planning around the figures below.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

- **Nine GHS pictograms:** exploding bomb, flame, flame over circle, gas cylinder, corrosion, exclamation mark, skull and crossbones, health hazard, and environment.
- **Signal words:** DANGER marks more severe hazard classifications; WARNING marks less severe classifications. The classified category decides the word, not the bottle’s size.
- **H and P codes:** H codes describe hazards. H224 means “extremely flammable liquid and vapour”; H302 means “harmful if swallowed”; H314 means “causes severe skin burns and eye damage”; H400 means “very toxic to aquatic life”.
- **Precautionary P codes:** P210 says to keep the chemical away from heat, sparks, flames and other ignition sources, with no smoking. P280 requires suitable gloves, protective clothing, eye protection and face protection.
- **Legacy R/S phrases:** Old bottles may retain the earlier European system. R11 means highly flammable; S36, S37 and S39 call for suitable protective clothing, gloves, and eye or face protection.

| Fire class | Typical material | Usual extinguishing agent |
|---|---|---|
| A | Wood, paper, ordinary solids | Water, foam or ABC powder |
| B | Petrol, ethanol, organic solvents | Foam, dry powder or CO₂; no plain water |
| C | LPG and other flammable gases | Isolate supply; dry powder normally |
| D | Sodium, magnesium and similar metals | Special Class-D powder only |

- **Universal dilution order:** Always add **acid to water**, never water to acid. Concentrated H₂SO₄ has density $1.84\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, so it sinks and heats the surrounding water during dilution.
- **LD₅₀:** The median lethal dose kills 50% of test organisms under a stated species, route and time condition. It is reported in mg of substance per kg of body mass; a smaller LD₅₀ means greater acute lethal toxicity.

> **Bench tip:** Splash goggles are only one part of PPE. Add a lab coat, closed shoes, and chemical-compatible gloves; use a face shield when splashing or scale-up is possible.

### 🟡 Standard — Exam Prep (3d-3w)

#### Why water into acid is the classic accident

Water poured onto concentrated acid can float briefly as a thin, easily heated layer. Concentrated H₂SO₄ at $1.84\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ then sinks into it, releases dilution heat, and traps the water near a hot interface. In a poor, uncooled addition, a very small trapped portion can flash to boiling in about a second and throw hot acid; the precise time depends on volumes and mixing.

The temperature rise follows $q=mc\Delta T$, where $q$ is heat absorbed by the mixture in J, $m$ is mixture mass in g, $c$ is specific heat capacity in $\mathrm{J\,g^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, and $\Delta T$ is temperature rise in K. Local heating can be much harsher than the average value.

Use this order:

1. Place the measured water in a heat-resistant vessel and prepare goggles, gloves, coat and cooling bath.
2. Add acid slowly in small portions while stirring and cooling.
3. After cooling, transfer the solution to a volumetric vessel and dilute to the required final volume.

#### Fire classes and extinguisher matching

For an energised electrical fire, isolate power if safe and use CO₂ or dry powder rated for electrical service. Water and ordinary aqueous foam conduct electricity and can turn one spark into a broader accident.

Never use water on an alkali-metal fire:

$$
\mathrm{2Na + 2H_2O \rightarrow 2NaOH + H_2}
$$

The reaction releases heat, and the hydrogen may ignite. CO₂ is not a safe blanket choice either because hot metals can reduce it:

$$
\mathrm{2Mg + CO_2 \rightarrow 2MgO + C}
$$

Use a special Class-D agent. For a small solvent fire, foam, dry powder or CO₂ may be suitable; for a burning gas, isolate the supply and use the approved dry-powder procedure.

#### Storage, segregation and incompatibility

Use designated acid-above-base shelving with separate trays and secondary containment. Store oxidisers away from flammables: concentrated HNO₃ contains a strong oxidising pair that can react violently with ethanol, acetone and similar organic fuels.

Keep heat-sensitive liquids in a cool, dark desiccator. Peroxidisers such as diethyl ether need separate fire-resistant storage because storage can form shock-sensitive peroxides. Anhydrous reagents need dedicated dry cabinets or tightly closed desiccators rather than an open shelf.

#### Working numbers

For dilution, use

$$
C_1V_1=C_2V_2
$$

where $C_1$ and $C_2$ are initial and final amount concentrations in $\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, while $V_1$ and $V_2$ are aliquot and final volumes in mL.

To prepare $100\ \mathrm{mL}$ of $0.100\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ H₂SO₄ from $1.00\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ stock:

$$
V_1=\frac{C_2V_2}{C_1}
=\frac{(0.100\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}})(100\ \mathrm{mL})}
{1.00\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}}
=10.0\ \mathrm{mL}
$$

Use $10.0\ \mathrm{mL}$ of stock and dilute to a final volume of $100\ \mathrm{mL}$.

For concentration,

$$
c=\frac{m}{V}
$$

where $c$ is mass concentration in $\mathrm{mg\,L^{-1}}$, $m$ is dissolved mass in mg, and $V$ is solution volume in L. Thus:

$$
c=\frac{250\ \mathrm{mg}}{2.00\ \mathrm{L}}
=125\ \mathrm{mg\,L^{-1}}
\approx125\ \mathrm{ppm}
$$

For a dilute aqueous solution, $1\ \mathrm{mg\,L^{-1}}$ is approximately $1\ \mathrm{ppm}$.

For toxicity,

$$
n_{50}=\frac{L_{50}}{M}
$$

where $n_{50}$ is LD₅₀ in $\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ body mass, $L_{50}$ is LD₅₀ in $\mathrm{mg\,kg^{-1}}$ body mass, and $M$ is molar mass in $\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$.

Using illustrative acute oral-rat values:

$$
3000\ \mathrm{mg\,kg^{-1}}=3.000\ \mathrm{g\,kg^{-1}}
$$

$$
n_{50}=\frac{3.000\ \mathrm{g\,kg^{-1}}}{58.44\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}}
=0.0513\ \mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}
$$

For ethanol:

$$
7060\ \mathrm{mg\,kg^{-1}}=7.060\ \mathrm{g\,kg^{-1}}
$$

$$
n_{50}=\frac{7.060\ \mathrm{g\,kg^{-1}}}{46.07\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}}
=0.153\ \mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}
$$

NaCl has the lower LD₅₀ in both comparisons, so it remains more acutely toxic here. Never transfer such rankings between species or exposure routes.

#### MCQ-style traps

- **DANGER versus WARNING:** severity comes from the hazard classification, not simply the acid concentration.
- **H versus P:** H identifies the hazard; P gives prevention, response, storage or disposal advice.
- **Exploding bomb versus flame:** the bomb indicates explosive or self-reactive behaviour; a plain flame indicates flammability, not necessarily explosion.
- **CO₂ in a fume cupboard:** for a small burning solvent, it cools rapidly and leaves no wet residue; it is not sprayed onto a non-burning spill.
- **Adequate PPE:** splash goggles alone do not protect the clothing, hands, face or shoes during handling.

> **Exam habit:** Read the pictogram, signal word, H statement and P statement separately. They answer four different questions.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (1mo+)

#### Exposure control as a chemistry problem

A fume-hood face velocity around $0.50\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ is a common laboratory design figure, not a BUET rule. Face velocity follows

$$
v=\frac{Q}{A}
$$

where $v$ is face velocity in $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $Q$ is airflow in $\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}}$, and $A$ is open-face area in $\mathrm{m^2}$. Thus, $Q=0.250\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}}$ and $A=0.50\ \mathrm{m^2}$ give $v=0.50\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

Sash height changes opening area and capture distance. Lowering the sash at fixed airflow raises calculated face velocity, but opening it too far allows room air to disturb the contaminated airflow. Keep work near the interior and use the marked sash position.

A TWA is an eight-hour time-weighted average exposure. An STEL concerns a short exposure period, often 15 minutes. Their numerical limits depend on the substance and jurisdiction.

For a uniform-air model at pressure $P$, room volume $V$, temperature $T$, and target $C_p$ expressed numerically in ppmv:

$$
n=\frac{C_pPV}{10^6RT}
$$

Here $n$ is solvent vapour amount in mol, $P$ is pressure in kPa, $V$ is room volume in $\mathrm{m^3}$, $R=8.314\ \mathrm{kPa\,m^3\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, and $T$ is temperature in K.

For $100\ \mathrm{ppmv}$, $10.0\ \mathrm{m^3}$, $101.3\ \mathrm{kPa}$, and $293.15\ \mathrm K$:

$$
n=\frac{(100)(101.3\ \mathrm{kPa})(10.0\ \mathrm{m^3})}
{(10^6)(8.314\ \mathrm{kPa\,m^3\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}})(293.15\ \mathrm K)}
=4.16\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{mol}
$$

For toluene, $M=92.14\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$, so:

$$
m=(4.16\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{mol})(92.14\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}})
=3.83\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm g=3.83\ \mathrm{mg}
$$

This uniform-mixing calculation is not permission to release vapour; a real fume plume is uneven and often becomes a local concentration problem.

#### The 18-volume rule and vapour density

The classical “18 volumes of water” rule estimates total water needed to absorb the heating, partial vaporisation and heat loss from certain nonpolar liquid fires:

$$
V_w\approx18V_f
$$

Here $V_w$ is total extinguishing-water volume in L and $V_f$ is fuel volume in L. Thus, $0.50\ \mathrm L$ of fuel corresponds to an estimated $9.0\ \mathrm L$ of extinguishing water. This is an emergency supply rule of thumb, not permission to throw water across burning solvent, and it is not a universal extinguisher-design formula.

At the same temperature and pressure, vapour density relative to air is:

$$
d=\frac{\rho_v}{\rho_a}=\frac{M_v}{M_a}
$$

Here $\rho_v$ and $\rho_a$ are vapour and air densities in $\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, $M_v$ and $M_a$ are their molar masses in $\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$, and $d$ is dimensionless. For toluene:

$$
d=\frac{92.14\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}}{28.97\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}}=3.18
$$

Toluene vapour is about 3.18 times as dense as air under these assumptions. It often collects near bench level and travels along the floor to a distant spark or hot motor. Airflow can carry lighter mixtures too, so remove ignition sources rather than trusting vapour density.

#### Grignard chemistry as the reason anhydrous exists

Grignard reagents are made by the general reaction:

$$
\mathrm{Mg + R{-}X \rightarrow R{-}MgX}
$$

Here $R$ is an organic group and $X$ is a halogen such as chlorine, bromine or iodine. Water converts the product immediately back into a hydrocarbon:

$$
\mathrm{R{-}MgX + H_2O \rightarrow R{-}H + Mg(OH)X}
$$

The general carbon-carbon bond-forming reaction is:

$$
\mathrm{R{-}MgX + R'C(=O)R'' \rightarrow R'C(OMgX)(R'')R
\xrightarrow{H_3O^+} R'C(OH)(R'')R}
$$

Here $R'$, $R''$ and $R$ are organic groups; the scheme covers reaction with an aldehyde or ketone, followed by acidic work-up.

Magnesium carries an MgO surface film, while humid storage can add hydroxide or carbonate-containing surface species. The oxide may hinder reaction initiation, but moisture and reactive surface sites consume RMgX or divert it towards unwanted products. “Anhydrous” therefore means demonstrably water-free solvent and glassware, not merely a bottle labelled dry.

Prepare and use these reagents on a small scale in a fume hood, with dry inert gas and no open flame. Organolithiums follow similar moisture-sensitivity rules. Ether, hydrogen and reactive metals form a poor combination for laboratory improvising.

#### Practice prompts

1. A label shows H224 and P210. State the hazard and the main precaution.  
   **Hint:** One code classifies; the other tells the handler what to do.

2. Prepare $50.0\ \mathrm{mL}$ of $0.150\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ solution from $0.750\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ stock. What aliquot is required?  
   **Hint:** $V_1=C_2V_2/C_1=10.0\ \mathrm{mL}$.

3. Choose a suitable response for an energised socket and for burning sodium, and justify each choice.  
   **Hint:** Conductivity and the hydrogen-forming sodium reaction decide the answers.

4. A Grignard flask was washed with tap water but used immediately. What is wrong?  
   **Hint:** Adorbed water quenches the organometallic before the intended reaction.

#### Connections

Acid-base equilibrium and Le Chatelier’s principle explain proton transfer and quenching, while ionic and covalent bonding explains solvent polarity, miscibility and organometallic reactivity. Redox and electrode potential connect oxidisers, reductants and combustion; functional groups predict solvent and reagent reactions. Polymer and waste chemistry informs degradation and disposal, titration trains standardisation, and mixing thermochemistry explains why acid dilution demands controlled heat transfer.

### Common traps

1. **“DANGER simply means the bottle contains a high concentration.”** It is assigned to a more severe GHS hazard classification; concentration alone does not decide it.
2. **“Water is safe on every fire.”** Water can spread burning liquids and reacts violently with alkali metals such as sodium.
3. **“P-codes describe hazards.”** H-codes describe hazards; P-codes give precautions, response, storage or disposal instructions.
4. **“Splash goggles are adequate PPE.”** Goggles protect only the eyes; gloves, clothing, footwear and suitable face protection may also be required.
5. **“Water should be poured into concentrated acid for rapid cooling.”** Add acid slowly to a larger amount of water while stirring and cooling.
6. **“CO₂ is the universal safe extinguisher.”** It can be suitable for some electrical or solvent fires, but not for every spill, gas fire or burning metal.
