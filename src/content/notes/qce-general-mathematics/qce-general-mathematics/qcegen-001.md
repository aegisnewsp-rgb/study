# Unit 1: Money, Measurement, Algebra and Linear Equations — QCE General Mathematics (QCAA 2025) Notes

Unit 1 of QCE General Mathematics covers consumer arithmetic, percentages and finance; measurement and error; algebra and linear equations with applications to practical contexts. It is the first Unit of the four-Unit General Mathematics course in the Queensland Certificate of Education.

> Re-check the live specification details on https://www.qcaa.qld.edu.au/senior/senior-subjects/syllabuses/mathematics/general-mathematics before planning revision around these figures.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Consumer arithmetic.** Wages, salaries, commission, GST, discount, mark-up and margin. Solve problems involving money using percentages.
- **Measurement and error.** Absolute error = |measured − true|. Relative error = absolute error / true value. Percentage error = relative error × 100%.
- **Algebra.** Simplify expressions, expand brackets, factorise common factor and quadratic trinomials, complete the square.
- **Linear equations.** Solve ax + b = c. Solve simultaneous equations by substitution or elimination. Graph y = mx + c.

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing discount and mark-up — discount is applied to the original price; mark-up is added to the cost price.
- Mixing up absolute and relative error.
- Treating 5x and x⁵ as the same.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Consumer arithmetic

Salary = annual ÷ 12 (monthly) or annual ÷ 26 (fortnightly) or annual ÷ 52 (weekly). Commission = rate × sales. GST = 10% of the pre-GST price. Discount: sale price = original × (1 − discount%). Mark-up: selling price = cost × (1 + mark-up%). Margin: margin% = (selling − cost) / selling × 100.

#### Measurement and error

Absolute error = |measured − true|. Relative error = absolute error / true. Percentage error = relative error × 100%. Bounds: a measurement of x to the nearest unit lies in [x − 0.5, x + 0.5).

#### Algebra

Laws of indices: aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ; aᵐ / aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ; (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ. Expand: (a + b)(c + d) = ac + ad + bc + bd. Factorise: common factor first, then quadratic trinomial as (x + p)(x + q) where pq = constant and p + q = coefficient.

#### Linear equations

Solve ax + b = c by isolating x: x = (c − b)/a. Two equations with two unknowns: solve by substitution (isolate one variable, substitute) or elimination (multiply to make coefficients equal, subtract).

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Why Unit 1 underpins the rest of General Mathematics

- **Linear equations** are the foundation for linear modelling, simultaneous equations and inequalities in later Units.
- **Measurement and error** is essential for scientific applications in Units 2-4.
- **Algebraic manipulation** is required in every subsequent Unit.

#### Year 11 planning pattern

- **Term 1.** Consumer arithmetic, percentages, money conversions.
- **Term 2.** Measurement, error, bounds.
- **Term 3.** Algebra, factorisation, simultaneous equations.
- **Term 4.** Revision and assessment.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Discount and mark-up are the same." No — discount is subtracted; mark-up is added.
- "5x and x⁵ are the same." No — 5x means 5 × x; x⁵ means x × x × x × x × x.
- "Factorisation always gives a unique answer." Different factorisations are equivalent.

#### Worked example — simultaneous equations by elimination

Solve the system:

```
  2x + 3y = 12          (1)
  x − y  =  1            (2)
```

From (2), x = y + 1. Substitute into (1):

```
  2(y + 1) + 3y = 12
  2y + 2 + 3y = 12
  5y = 10
  y = 2
```

Then x = 2 + 1 = 3.

Check: 2(3) + 3(2) = 6 + 6 = 12 ✓ ; 3 − 2 = 1 ✓. Both equations hold, so the solution (x, y) = (3, 2) is correct.

#### Worked example — mark-up versus margin

A retailer buys a jacket for $80 (the cost price) and sells it for $100 (the selling price).

```
  Mark-up % = (selling − cost) / cost × 100
            = (100 − 80) / 80 × 100
            = 25%

  Margin % = (selling − cost) / selling × 100
           = (100 − 80) / 100 × 100
           = 20%
```

Mark-up is the percentage added on top of the cost. Margin is the percentage of the final ticket price that is profit. A 25% mark-up produces a 20% margin, not 25% — this is the single most common arithmetic slip in consumer-arithmetic questions.

#### Worked example — measurement bounds and percentage error

A rod is measured as 23.4 cm, correct to the nearest millimetre.

```
  Absolute error  ≤ 0.5 × smallest scale division
                  ≤ 0.5 × 1 mm
                  ≤ 0.05 cm

  Relative error  = absolute / true
                  = 0.05 / 23.4
                  ≈ 0.00214

  Percentage error = relative × 100
                   ≈ 0.214%
```

The true length lies in the interval [23.35, 23.45). The percentage error bound, using the lower bound of the interval, is at most 0.05 / 23.35 × 100 ≈ 0.214%.

#### Common marking-scheme mistakes

Markers deduct marks even when the working looks mostly right. Watch for:

- Transposing a term across the equals sign without flipping its sign.
- Using the cost price as the denominator when the question asked for margin. The formula then gives mark-up, not margin.
- Reporting an error as a decimal when the question asked for a percentage.
- Rounding intermediate values to one or two decimals before the last step. The rounding error compounds; keep full precision until the final line.
- Drawing y = mx + c by joining two far-apart points without checking the y-intercept c against the equation.
- Writing the solution to a system as (y, x) instead of (x, y). Markers match coordinates in order.
- Forgetting the absolute value bars around an absolute-error expression. A negative "error" is not a meaningful error.

#### 20-minute recap before you walk in

- Memorise the three error formulas: absolute, relative, percentage. One line each.
- Distinguish discount (subtracted from original price), mark-up (added to cost price) and margin (relative to the selling price).
- Practise simultaneous equations by both substitution and elimination; pick whichever is faster for the coefficients in front of you.
- Always substitute your solution back into both original equations. Thirty seconds of checking can recover a sign error.
- Skim the laws of indices. Five identities cover most questions.
