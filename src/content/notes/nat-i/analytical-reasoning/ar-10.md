# Statement and Assumptions

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **Statement–Assumption** item gives a short proposal, plan, or claim, then lists 2–5 hidden premises; the job is to pick the assumption the author is quietly relying on. The **statement** is what is said; the **assumption** is what must be true for that statement to make sense. Test any option by deleting it: if the statement collapses without it, the option is a **necessary (warranted) assumption**. Watch for action-trigger words — *should, must, ought to, let us, the best way is, I propose* — because these signal a course of action whose hidden justification is the answer. An **inference** follows from the statement; an **assumption** precedes it. In NAT-I, this is a low-weightage but high-accuracy slot (usually 2–3 MCQs in the Analytical Reasoning section), so a clean two-pass strategy is enough.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Definition and Scope
An **assumption** is a *hidden, unstated premise* that the writer takes for granted. It is not an argument and not a conclusion — it is the unexpressed belief without which the stated proposal or claim would lose its force. NTS frames the question as: *"Given the statement, which assumption is implicit in it?"* or *"Which of the following is an assumption underlying the statement?"*

#### The Necessity Test
A valid assumption must pass the **necessity test**:

1. Mentally remove the option.
2. Ask: *Does the statement still hold, still make sense, still justify the proposed action?*
3. If **no**, the option is a necessary assumption and therefore valid.
4. If the statement still works without it, the option is a desirable but **unnecessary** belief — mark it invalid.

#### Course-of-Action Statements
Most NAT-I items in this topic are **proposals** (e.g., "The company should shift to a four-day week"). The hidden assumption almost always concerns one of three things:

- **Motivation** — *Why is this action being suggested?* (e.g., employees want better work-life balance.)
- **Feasibility** — *Can this action actually be carried out?* (e.g., output will not drop.)
- **Likely effect** — *What will the action achieve?* (e.g., productivity will rise.)

If none of the given options is presupposed by the action, the answer is **"None of the above"** — a common NTS distractor.

#### Assumption vs Inference vs Conclusion
| Term | Position relative to statement | Example (for "Schools should ban smartphones") |
|---|---|---|
| Assumption | Believed *before* the statement | Students use phones to cheat in exams. |
| Inference | Reasoned *after* the statement | Exam scores will improve next year. |
| Conclusion | Restated *from* the statement | Smartphone bans are necessary. |

Mixing these up is the single biggest error candidates make.

#### Worked Pattern
Statement: *"Let us switch to online classes during the heatwave."*
Option A: *Temperatures will remain high for the next week.* → **Necessary assumption** (without persistent heat, the proposal is pointless).
Option B: *Online classes improve learning outcomes.* → Not required; the statement only argues for **safety**, not quality.
Option C: *Students own laptops.* → A desirable condition, but the proposal doesn't actually depend on it (mobile phones suffice).

#### Common Traps
- **Paraphrased restatement** of the statement dressed as a new idea.
- **Wishful ideals** the speaker never relied on.
- **Stronger-than-required** beliefs (the statement only needs a *sufficient* premise, not a universal one).

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Nuanced Variants
NTS occasionally introduces subtle shifts:

- **Stating a fact, not proposing action.** A statement like *"The new bridge reduced traffic by 30%"* presupposes that traffic was measured reliably. The hidden assumption is methodological, not motivational — recognise the *type* of statement before you test the options.
- **"Only if" structures.** When the statement contains *only if, cannot, impossible*, the assumption is **necessary and sufficient**, so the answer must be the *exact* enabling condition — any weaker option is a trap.
- **Negative premises.** Statements that deny something (e.g., *"The policy should not be revoked"*) hide **affirmative** assumptions: the policy is working, or revocation would cause harm. Do not pick an option phrased as another denial.
- **"None of the above" items.** In NAT-I, when every option fails the necessity test, the correct choice is often the explicit *"None of these is an implicit assumption"* — slow down and verify all four before selecting it.

#### Connection to Adjacent Topics
Statement–Assumption overlaps directly with:
- **Statement–Conclusion** — same skeleton, but the conclusion must be *supported by* the statement, not *presupposed by* it.
- **Statement–Course of Action** — here you must evaluate *feasibility and likely effect* of the suggested action; the assumption is the *why*.
- **Critical Reasoning (Argument Evaluation)** — the assumption is the *missing link* in the argument, identical in logic to GMAT/GRE "assumption" stems.

#### Common Mistakes (revisited)
1. Treating an **inference** as an assumption because it sounds logical.
2. Choosing an option that **strengthens** the statement rather than one it **depends on**.
3. Assuming the statement is **true** and looking for evidence — NTS asks what is *presupposed*, not what is *proven*.
4. Missing **modal verbs** (*should, must, ought*) that turn a description into a proposal.

#### Practice Prompts
**Prompt 1.** *"The municipality should install CCTV cameras in all public parks."* Which assumption is implicit?
(a) Parks are visited by many people. (b) Cameras deter crime. (c) Surveillance improves safety. (d) The municipality has surplus funds.
→ **Answer: (a)** — without many visitors, the proposal has no scope. (b) and (c) are *inferences*; (d) is desirable but not presupposed.

**Prompt 2.** *"Our institute will not allow mobile phones inside the examination hall."* Identify the hidden assumption.
(a) Phones cause unfair means. (b) Students usually carry phones. (c) Cheating harms merit. (d) Exams are held offline.
→ **Answer: (d)** — if exams were online, the rule would be meaningless. (a) and (c) are *conclusions* drawn from the rule, not presupposed by it.

#### Exam Strategy
Allocate **45–60 seconds per item**. First pass: filter options that *restate* or *contradict* the statement. Second pass: apply the necessity test on the survivors. In NAT-I, this topic contributes roughly **4%** of Analytical Reasoning marks (2–3 questions); accuracy above 85% is achievable with the two-pass method.
