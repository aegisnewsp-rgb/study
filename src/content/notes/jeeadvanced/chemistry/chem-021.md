# Haloalkanes

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Haloalkanes (alkyl halides) are saturated aliphatic compounds in which one or more hydrogen atoms of an alkane are replaced by halogen atoms (F, Cl, Br, I). The carbon bearing the halogen is sp³ hybridised (tetrahedral, 109°28′), and the C–X bond is polar covalent with a partial positive charge on carbon, which drives nucleophilic substitution and elimination pathways.

| Property | Order / Rule | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| C–X bond enthalpy (kJ mol⁻¹) | C–F (~485) > C–Cl (~339) > C–Br (~276) > C–I (~238) | Bond strength falls with halogen size |
| Density of monohaloalkanes | RI > RBr > RCl > RF | Tracks molar mass |
| Boiling point | R–I > R–Br > R–Cl > R–F; branching lowers BP | Van der Waals + surface area |
| SN2 reactivity | CH₃X > 1° > 2° > 3°; also RI > RBr > RCl > RF | Steric + leaving group ability |
| SN1 reactivity | 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl | Carbocation stability |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** "**I leave easily**" — for SN2, leaving group ability follows **I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻** (opposite of C–X bond strength). Pair it with "**More carbons crowd the door**" for the steric order CH₃X > 1° > 2° > 3°.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Nomenclature and Classification
IUPAC names halogen as a prefix (halo-) and number the chain to give the lowest locant; common names use alkyl + halide (e.g., isopropyl bromide). Classification by carbon type gives **primary (1°), secondary (2°), tertiary (3°)** haloalkanes, while positional classification gives **geminal (two halogens on the same carbon)** and **vicinal (halogens on adjacent carbons)** dihalides. Allyl halides (CH₂=CH–CH₂–X) and benzyl halides (C₆H₅–CH₂–X) behave differently from vinyl/aryl halides because of resonance stabilisation of the cation.

#### Nucleophilic Substitution: SN1 vs SN2

The choice between SN1 and SN2 is decided by **substrate structure, nucleophile strength, and solvent**.

- **SN2 (single-step, bimolecular):** rate = k[Substrate][Nucleophile] in mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹. The nucleophile attacks from the backside of the C–X bond, giving **Walden inversion** (100% inverted stereochemistry). Favoured for methyl/1° substrates, polar aprotic solvents, and strong nucleophiles.
- **SN1 (two-step, unimolecular):** rate = k[Substrate] in s⁻¹. Ionisation forms a planar carbocation that is attacked from both faces, giving a **racemic mixture**. Favoured for 3° substrates, polar protic solvents, and weak nucleophiles. Allyl and benzyl substrates react SN1 even at 1° because the cation is resonance-stabilised.

#### Elimination: E1 and E2
β-elimination removes H and X from adjacent carbons to form an alkene. **E2** is concerted, requires an **anti-periplanar** H–C–C–X arrangement, and follows **Saytzeff's rule** (more substituted alkene major) with small bases. **E1** shares the carbocation intermediate with SN1 and gives the same Saytzeff outcome. Bulky bases (e.g., *t*-BuOK) or quaternary substrates lacking β-H give the **Hofmann product** (less substituted alkene).

#### Preparation and Organometallic Reagents
- From alcohols: HCl/ZnCl₂ (Lucas), PBr₃, SOCl₂ (gives SO₂ + HCl by-products).
- From alkenes: HBr/HCl Markovnikov addition; HBr with peroxide gives anti-Markovnikov product (radical mechanism).
- Halogen exchange: **Finkelstein** (RCl + NaI → RI + NaCl, driven by NaCl precipitation in acetone) and **Swarts** (RCl/Br + AgF → RF).
- **Grignard reagents**: R–X + Mg → R–Mg–X in **strictly dry ether**; water or alcohols protonate R–Mg–X back to alkane.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** (R)-2-Bromobutane is treated with aqueous NaOH (dilute, polar protic solvent). Which statement is correct?
(a) Reaction proceeds via SN2 with 100% inversion.
(b) Reaction proceeds via SN1 giving a racemic alcohol.
(c) Reaction proceeds via E2 with anti-periplanar geometry.
(d) The product is but-2-ene as the major organic product.

#### Solution:
1. Substrate is **2°** (2-bromobutane) with a secondary stereocentre.
2. Nucleophile (OH⁻ in water) is **weak** (water dilutes it); solvent is **polar protic** — conditions favour **SN1**.
3. SN1 forms a planar 2° carbocation at C-2; OH⁻ attacks from either face with equal probability → (R)- and (S)-butan-2-ol in equal amounts = **racemic mixture**.
4. E2 is disfavoured because OH⁻ is a weak base in dilute aqueous solution; elimination (E1) would compete but the question asks about the dominant pathway with OH⁻.

**Answer: (b)**.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students see "NaOH" and jump to SN2 with inversion. With a **2° substrate** in **aqueous (polar protic)** medium, OH⁻ is poorly nucleophilic and SN1 dominates — the stereochemical outcome is **racemisation**, not inversion.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Bond Polarity and Dipole Moment
The C–X bond dipole μ = q · d, where q is partial charge (C), d is the charge separation (m), and μ has SI unit C·m (dimension [A T]). For methyl halides the measured order is **CH₃Cl > CH₃F > CH₃Br > CH₃I**, *not* the electronegativity order. The reason: fluorine is so small that the C–F bond is unusually short, reducing d enough that μ(CH₃F) sits below μ(CH₃Cl) despite F being more electronegative.

#### Advanced Traps and Exceptions

1. **Leaving group vs bond strength inversion** — strongest C–X bond (C–F, ~485 kJ mol⁻¹) corresponds to the **worst** leaving group (F⁻). Always rank leaving-group ability by **C–X bond enthalpy descending**, not ascending.
2. **Allyl/benzyl SN1 at 1°** — a 1°-carbon adjacent to a π-system ionises almost as fast as a 3°-alkane because the cation is resonance-stabilised across the allyl/benzyl framework.
3. **Wurtz on mixed halides** — R–X + R′–X (different groups) yields RR + R′R′ + RR′ statistically, so cross-coupling cannot be done cleanly. Use **Corey–House synthesis** (R–X + R′₂CuLi) or **Wurtz–Fittig** (R–X + Ar–X + Na) for selective coupling.
4. **Anchimeric assistance (NGP)** — a neighbouring lone pair or π-bond can participate during ionisation, giving **retention** of configuration (e.g., 2-halopropanoates, *trans*-2-halocyclohexyl sulfonates). Classical SN1 racemisation fails in such substrates.
5. **E2 anti-periplanar constraint in cyclohexanes** — only the **trans-diaxial** β-H is eliminated; this fixes the geometry of the resulting alkene and is a favourite JEE Advanced structural-prediction question.
6. **Grignard incompatibility** — R–Mg–X is destroyed by –OH, –NH, –COOH, C≡C–H, and even by traces of moisture. All glassware must be oven-dried and the solvent freshly distilled over sodium/benzophenone.
7. **Swarts vs Finkelstein thermodynamics** — Finkelstein (Cl → I) is driven by precipitation of NaCl in acetone (Le Chatelier). Swarts (Cl/Br → F) is driven by formation of AgCl/AgBr precipitate when AgF is used.

#### Practice Prompts
1. Predict the major product(s) when 2-bromo-2-methylbutane is treated with (i) NaOEt in ethanol, (ii) *t*-BuOK in DMSO. Explain why the two bases give different regiochemistry despite both promoting E2.
2. (S)-2-Iodooctane reacts with NaI⁻ in acetone and with NaCN in DMSO separately. Describe the stereochemistry of the product in each case and justify using transition-state arguments.
