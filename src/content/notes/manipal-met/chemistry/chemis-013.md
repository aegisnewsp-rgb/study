### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

**Tetravalency and hybridisation.** Carbon forms four bonds. Hybridisation: $sp^3$ (tetrahedral, 109.5°), $sp^2$ (trigonal planar, 120°), $sp$ (linear, 180°). Ethane: $sp^3$; ethene: $sp^2$; ethyne: $sp$. Hybridisation determines geometry, bond length, and the kind of orbital overlap in $\pi$ bonds.

**Functional groups and homologous series.** A homologous series is a family differing by a repeating $-\text{CH}_2-$ unit. Functional groups: $-\text{OH}$ (alcohol), $-\text{CHO}$ (aldehyde), $>\text{C}=\text{O}$ (ketone), $-\text{COOH}$ (carboxylic acid), $-\text{NH}_2$ (amine), $-\text{X}$ (halide, where $\text{X} = \text{F}, \text{Cl}, \text{Br}, \text{I}$). Same functional group → similar chemical behaviour.

**Isomerism.** Structural (constitutional) isomers differ in connectivity. Stereoisomers have the same connectivity but differ in 3D arrangement. Within stereoisomers: geometric (cis-trans around a $>\text{C}=\text{C}<$), optical (mirror-image non-superimposable pairs around a chiral centre).

**IUPAC nomenclature — the suffix system.** Identify the longest carbon chain that contains the principal functional group. Number to give the lowest locant to that group. Replace the "-e" of the alkane name with the suffix for the principal group: -ol (alcohol), -al (aldehyde), -one (ketone), -oic acid (carboxylic acid), -amine (amine). For example, $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}(\text{OH})\text{CH}_3$ is propan-2-ol, not isopropanol.

**Bond fission.**
- Homolytic: bond breaks symmetrically, one electron to each fragment, producing two free radicals. Favoured by UV light, peroxides, high temperature.
- Heterolytic: bond breaks asymmetrically, both electrons to one fragment, producing a cation and an anion. Favoured by polar solvents and polar reagents.

**Reaction intermediates.**
- Free radical: neutral, electron-deficient, $sp^2$ planar, stabilised by hyperconjugation and resonance. Stability: $3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ > \text{methyl}$.
- Carbocation: positively charged, $sp^2$ planar, electron-deficient. Stability order same as radicals.
- Carbanion: negatively charged, $sp^3$ pyramidal, electron-rich. Stability: $1^\circ > 2^\circ > 3^\circ$ (opposite of cation — alkyl groups are weakly electron-donating and destabilise negative charge through inductive donation).

**Electrophile vs. nucleophile.** Electrophile = electron-poor, seeks electrons (e.g. $\text{H}^+$, $\text{BF}_3$, carbocations, $\text{Cl}^+$ sources). Nucleophile = electron-rich, donates electrons (e.g. $\text{OH}^-$, $\text{NH}_3$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{CN}^-$).

**Electronic effects in covalent bonds.**
- Inductive effect: permanent polarisation of a $\sigma$ bond through adjacent atoms. $-\text{I}$ (electron-withdrawing): $-\text{NO}_2$, $-\text{CN}$, $-\text{F}$, $-\text{COOH}$. $+\text{I}$ (electron-donating): alkyl groups like $-\text{CH}_3$, $-\text{C}_2\text{H}_5$. Effect falls off with distance — roughly halves every additional carbon.
- Electromeric effect: temporary polarisation of a $\pi$ bond under attack by a reagent. The $\pi$ electrons shift entirely to one atom. Operates only during the reaction and disappears in the product.
- Resonance: delocalisation of $\pi$ or lone-pair electrons across a conjugated system. The real structure is a hybrid of the contributing structures, with bond orders intermediate between single and double.
- Hyperconjugation: delocalisation of $\sigma$ electrons of an adjacent $\text{C}-\text{H}$ bond into an empty $p$ orbital (radical, cation) or a $\pi$ bond. Number of $\alpha$-hydrogens controls the strength: more $\alpha$-hydrogens → greater stabilisation.

**Four reaction types.**
- Substitution: one group replaces another ($\text{S}_\text{N}1$, $\text{S}_\text{N}2$, free-radical halogenation).
- Addition: two groups add across a multiple bond (electrophilic addition to alkene, nucleophilic addition to carbonyl).
- Elimination: a small molecule ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{HX}$) is removed to form a multiple bond ($\text{E}1$, $\text{E}2$).
- Rearrangement: the carbon framework rearranges to a more stable isomer (e.g. hydride shift, methyl shift in carbocation intermediates).

### 🟡 Standard — Exam Prep (3d-3w)

**Worked Example 1 — IUPAC naming.**
Name $\text{CH}_3-\text{CH}(\text{CH}_3)-\text{CH}_2-\text{CHO}$.
Longest chain containing the $-\text{CHO}$ group: four carbons → butanal. The methyl branch sits on carbon 2 (numbering from the $-\text{CHO}$ end gives the lowest locant to the principal group). Name: 2-methylbutanal.

**Worked Example 2 — Inductive effect ranking of acidity.**
Rank by acidity: $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$, $\text{FCH}_2\text{COOH}$, $\text{ClCH}_2\text{COOH}$, $\text{BrCH}_2\text{COOH}$, $\text{ICH}_2\text{COOH}$.
Acidity of substituted acetic acids correlates with the electron-withdrawing power of the substituent: stronger $-\text{I}$ effect stabilises the carboxylate anion. Electronegativity decreases $\text{F} > \text{Cl} > \text{Br} > \text{I}$. So acidity: $\text{FCH}_2\text{COOH} > \text{ClCH}_2\text{COOH} > \text{BrCH}_2\text{COOH} > \text{ICH}_2\text{COOH} > \text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$. The fluorinated one is the most acidic because fluorine pulls electron density most strongly through the $\sigma$ bond.

**Worked Example 3 — Resonance structures of carboxylate.**
Draw the two main resonance structures of the acetate ion $\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$.
Structure A: $\text{CH}_3-\text{C}(=\text{O})-\text{O}^-$ (negative charge on the singly-bonded oxygen). Structure B: $\text{CH}_3-\text{C}(-\text{O}^-)=\text{O}$ (negative charge on the other oxygen, double bond shifted). Both oxygens are equivalent in the real ion. Bond order of each $\text{C}-\text{O}$ bond is $1.5$. This is why carboxylate anions are much more stable than alkoxide anions, and why carboxylic acids are more acidic than alcohols.

**Practice Set.**
1. How many structural isomers does $\text{C}_4\text{H}_{10}$ have? (Answer: 2 — n-butane and isobutane.)
2. Identify the hybridisation of each carbon in $\text{CH}_2=\text{CH}-\text{C}\equiv\text{CH}$. ($\text{C}_1$, $\text{C}_2$: $sp^2$; $\text{C}_3$, $\text{C}_4$: $sp$.)
3. Rank carbocation stability: $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2^+$, $(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{CH}^+$, $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C}^+$. (Most stable: tertiary > secondary > primary.)
4. Identify the type of bond fission in the reaction $\text{Cl}_2 \xrightarrow{h\nu} 2\text{Cl}^\bullet$. (Homolytic, producing two free radicals.)

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (1mo+)

**Why $sp^3$/$sp^2$/$sp$ hybridisation is real.** Carbon has $2s^2 2p^2$ in its ground state. Promoting one $2s$ electron to the empty $2p$ gives $2s^1 2p^3$ — four unpaired electrons ready to bond. Hybridisation mixes the $2s$ and the $2p$ orbitals into a new set of equivalent hybrids. The geometry is fixed because the hybrids point in tetrahedral / trigonal-planar / linear directions; the leftover unhybridised $p$ orbitals form $\pi$ bonds sideways. MET questions occasionally test the link between hybridisation and bond angle ($sp$: 180°, $sp^2$: 120°, $sp^3$: 109.5°) or between hybridisation and bond length ($sp$ shortest, $sp^3$ longest among single bonds to similar atoms).

**Stability of carbocations in detail.** Methyl cation $\text{CH}_3^+$ has no $\alpha$-hydrogen to hyperconjugate, so it is the least stable primary cation. Each $\alpha$-hydrogen on an adjacent $sp^3$ carbon can donate into the empty $p$ orbital of the cation, lowering energy. Tertiary cations have nine $\alpha$-hydrogens (on three methyl groups) — maximum hyperconjugation. Resonance, when available, dwarfs hyperconjugation: the allyl cation $\text{CH}_2=\text{CH}-\text{CH}_2^+$ is more stable than $t$-butyl cation because the positive charge is delocalised across two carbons.

**Resonance vs. hyperconjugation at a glance.** Resonance requires a conjugated $\pi$ system (alternating single/double bonds or lone pair adjacent to $\pi$); hyperconjugation only needs an adjacent $\text{C}-\text{H}$ $\sigma$ bond. Resonance stabilises by several kcal/mol per contributing structure; hyperconjugation contributes about 1–2 kcal/mol per $\alpha$-hydrogen. In an MET NAT question, count $\alpha$-hydrogens for hyperconjugation effects and identify conjugated systems for resonance effects.

**Hyperconjugation in alkenes and arenes.** For an alkene $\text{R}_2\text{C}=\text{CR}_2$, the number of $\alpha$-hydrogens on substituents correlates with the heat of hydrogenation — more $\alpha$-hydrogens → more stable alkene. The stability order of alkenes by substitution: $\text{R}_2\text{C}=\text{CR}_2 > \text{R}_2\text{C}=\text{CHR} > \text{RCH}=\text{CHR}$ (cis or trans) > $\text{RCH}=\text{CH}_2$ (cis more stable than trans) > $\text{CH}_2=\text{CH}_2$. This is why 2-methylbut-2-ene is more stable than but-1-ene. The same effect explains why toluene is more reactive than benzene in electrophilic substitution: the methyl group hyperconjugates into the ring.

**Reaction-mechanism classification.** Free-radical halogenation of alkanes: initiation (homolysis of $\text{Cl}_2$ under UV), propagation ($\text{Cl}^\bullet$ abstracts $\text{H}$ from alkane, leaving carbon radical; carbon radical abstracts $\text{Cl}$ from $\text{Cl}_2$), termination ($\text{Cl}^\bullet + \text{Cl}^\bullet \rightarrow \text{Cl}_2$, etc.). Electrophilic addition to alkene: $\pi$ electrons attack an electrophile ($\text{H}^+$, $\text{Br}^+$), forming a carbocation intermediate, then the nucleophile attacks. Markovnikov's rule: hydrogen adds to the carbon with more hydrogens, producing the more stable carbocation. Anti-Markovnikov (peroxide effect) applies only to $\text{HBr}$ addition in the presence of peroxides.

**Why nucleophilicity differs from basicity.** Basicity is the equilibrium constant for protonation in water; nucleophilicity is a kinetic rate property in a specific solvent and reaction. In protic solvents (water, alcohols), larger polarisable nucleophiles ($\text{I}^-$, $\text{Br}^-$, $\text{RS}^-$) are stronger nucleophiles than smaller ones ($\text{F}^-$, $\text{Cl}^-$) because the larger ions are less tightly solvated by hydrogen bonds. In aprotic solvents (DMSO, acetone, DMF), the order flips — $\text{F}^-$ becomes a much stronger nucleophile. MET generally tests the protic-solvent ordering.

## Common traps

1. **Confusing the stability order of carbocations and carbanions.** Carbocations: $3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ > \text{methyl}$ (alkyl groups donate electrons inductively). Carbanions: the opposite — $\text{methyl} > 1^\circ > 2^\circ > 3^\circ$. Memorising only one direction and applying it to both is a frequent error.
2. **Counting $\alpha$-hydrogens wrong.** The $\alpha$-carbon is the carbon attached to the positively charged centre or radical. Hydrogens on that carbon are $\alpha$-hydrogens; hydrogens on the next carbon are $\beta$, not $\alpha$. In $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C}^+$, the central carbon is the cation, and the three methyl groups contribute $3 \times 3 = 9$ $\alpha$-hydrogens.
3. **Mixing up Markovnikov's rule for HBr with the peroxide effect.** In the absence of peroxides, HBr adds Markovnikov (hydrogen to the carbon with more H). In the presence of peroxides (e.g. benzoyl peroxide), HBr adds anti-Markovnikov via a free-radical mechanism. The peroxide effect does NOT apply to HCl or HI — only HBr.
4. **Treating hyperconjugation and resonance as the same effect.** They are not. Resonance needs a $\pi$ system and contributes several kcal/mol per structure. Hyperconjugation needs an $\alpha$-$\text{C}-\text{H}$ $\sigma$ bond and contributes 1–2 kcal/mol per hydrogen. Many questions specifically ask which is operating.
5. **Choosing the wrong IUPAC parent chain.** The longest chain that contains the principal functional group (the suffix group) wins, even if there is a longer chain without it. In $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH}(\text{OH})\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_3$, the parent is pentane (5 carbons) → pentan-3-ol, not a hexane-based name. A common error picks the longer alkyl chain instead.
