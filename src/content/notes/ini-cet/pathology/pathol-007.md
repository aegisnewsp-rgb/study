# Immunopathology

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Read first when you have one day left and want the bare minimum to still answer the question.

Disordered immunity — **hypersensitivity**, **autoimmunity**, **immunodeficiency**, **transplant rejection**, and **amyloidosis** — forms a 5-part vertical that recurs in roughly 3% of INI CET Pathology. Gell and Coombs splits hypersensitivity into **Type I (IgE/mast cell)**, **Type II (cytotoxic antibody)**, **Type III (immune complex)**, **Type IV (T-cell mediated)**; getting the prototype disease on each row is worth a free mark.

| Type | Core mechanism | Rapid-fire prototype |
|---|---|---|
| I | IgE → FcεRI → mast cell degranulation | Anaphylaxis, atopic asthma |
| II | IgG/IgM binds cell-surface antigen → MAC, opsonization, ADCC | Goodpasture, myasthenia gravis, hemolytic anemia, hyperacute graft rejection |
| III | Antigen–antibody complex deposits in vessels/kidney/skin | SLE, serum sickness, PSGN, Arthus reaction |
| IV | Th1/cytotoxic T cells, macrophages, granulomas | TB, contact dermatitis, chronic rejection, GVHD |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook — "ACID" classification cue:** **A**llergic/Anaphylaxis = I, **C**ytotoxic = II (think **C**oombs antibodies), **I**mmune complex = III, **D**elayed = IV (T cells take **d**ays). Amyloid stains **Congo red → apple-green birefringence**; **AL** = immunoglobulin **L**ight chain, **AA** = secondary to chronic inflammation (serum **A**myloid A).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> For the candidate who has a few weeks and wants the working model, not just the punchline.

#### Mechanism backbone of each hypersensitivity type

**Type I** requires prior sensitization: plasma cells secrete IgE, which binds FcεRI on mast cells. Re-exposure cross-links the IgE → mast cell degranulation releases histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes C4/D4/E4, prostaglandin D2, and PAF. The early phase (minutes, histamine-driven) is followed by a late phase (hours, leukotriene-driven). Skin-prick testing detects bound IgE; serum tryptase confirms systemic mast-cell activation. Therapy: epinephrine first, then antihistamines, leukotriene modifiers, omalizumab (anti-IgE), and allergen desensitization.

**Type II** destroys cells through three effector arms: complement MAC (membrane attack complex) lysing the membrane, opsonization with C3b driving phagocytosis, and ADCC where NK cells engage IgG via FcγRIII. Antibody-stimulating disorders (Graves — anti-TSHR acting as agonist) do NOT destroy the target cell but hyper-function it — same Type II bucket.

**Type III** injury depends on antigen:antibody ratio. Slight antigen excess produces soluble complexes that deposit in glomeruli, vessel walls, and joints, fix complement (low C3/C4), and recruit neutrophils via C5a. Chronic serum sickness and SLE exemplify this.

**Type IV** is the only antibody-free type. Th1 cells release IFN-γ to activate macrophages (granuloma); CD8+ T cells kill targets directly. Read the case — nickel dermatitis in 48–72 hours = Type IV.

#### Autoimmunity — what tolerance broke

Loss of self-tolerance happens through **molecular mimicry** (rheumatic fever: streptococcal M protein vs cardiac myosin), **epitope spreading**, **bystander activation** of autoreactive T cells during infection, and **release of sequestered antigens** (sperm after vasectomy, lens protein post-trauma). Autoimmune diseases split into **organ-specific** (Hashimoto, Graves, type 1 DM, myasthenia gravis) versus **systemic** (SLE, RA, scleroderma, Sjögren, dermatomyositis). Autoantibody panels map to disease with high specificity — anti-dsDNA and anti-Sm → SLE; anti-CCP → RA; anti-Scl-70 → diffuse systemic sclerosis; anti-centromere → limited/CREST; anti-mitochondrial (AMA-M2) → primary biliary cholangitis; anti-SSA/Ro, anti-SSB/La → Sjögren; anti-TPO + anti-thyroglobulin → Hashimoto; anti-TSHR (stimulating) → Graves.

#### Transplant rejection — the timetable examiners love

| Setting | Timing | Mediators | Histology clue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hyperacute** | Minutes to hours | Preformed anti-ABO/HLA IgG (Type II) | Fibrinoid necrosis + platelet-fibrin thrombi |
| **Acute cellular** | Weeks to months | Host CD8+ T cells on donor MHC (Type IV) | Dense lymphocytic infiltrate + tubulitis |
| **Acute humoral** | Weeks to months | Donor-specific antibodies + complement (Type II) | C4d deposition in capillaries |
| **Chronic** | Months to years | CD4+ Th1 → fibrosis, vascular intimal thickening (Type IV) | Interstitial fibrosis, tubular atrophy, "onion-skin" vessels |
| **GVHD** | Days to months post-BMT | Donor T cells attacking host HLA | Skin rash, hepatic cholestasis, GI diarrhea |

#### Worked clinical vignette

A 30-year-old woman presents 9 days after receiving a deceased-donor renal transplant with rising creatinine and decreased urine output. The graft biopsy shows a dense interstitial mononuclear-cell infiltrate with lymphocytes invading tubular epithelium. C4d staining is negative, and no preformed donor-specific antibodies are detected in serum.

Resolution path: timing (weeks post-transplant), lymphocytic tubulitis, negative C4d — this is **acute cellular rejection**, a **Type IV** reaction driven by recipient CD8+ cytotoxic T cells recognising donor MHC class I mismatches. Treatment escalates calcineurin-inhibitor (tacrolimus) dosing and adds high-dose IV methylprednisolone; anti-thymocyte globulin is reserved for steroid-resistant disease.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Don't reflexively call every post-transplant dysfunction Type IV. If the vignette says **minutes after revascularization**, the answer is **hyperacute / Type II**. If **C4d is positive**, it is **acute humoral (Type II)**, not cellular. The four timing windows map to four mechanisms — read the clock.

#### Comparison matrix — commonly mixed-up pairs

| Feature | Type II | Type III | Type IV |
|---|---|---|---|
| Antibody involvement | Yes (IgG/IgM vs cell-surface) | Yes (IgG/IgM + soluble antigen) | None |
| Primary effector | Complement, ADCC, opsonization | Neutrophils + complement | T cells + macrophages |
| Prototype | Goodpasture, MG, AIHA | SLE, PSGN, serum sickness | TB, contact dermatitis |
| Speed | Hours | Hours–days | 48–72 hours |
| Distinct trap | Graves is II (stimulating Ab) | SLE is III, NOT II despite anti-dsDNA | TB granuloma is IV |

| Immunodeficiency | Defect | Hallmark | Inheritance / Key clue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bruton agammaglobulinemia** | BTK → no mature B cells | Recurrent bacterial infections after 6 months; absent tonsils | X-linked; **no** immunoglobulins on electrophoresis |
| **DiGeorge syndrome** | 22q11.2 deletion → thymic aplasia | T-cell deficiency, hypocalcemic tetany, cardiac anomalies, cleft palate | "CATCH-22" |
| **SCID** | Adenosine deaminase / IL-2Rγ mutations | Failure to thrive, opportunistic + bacterial infections, no thymic shadow | ADA → accumulate adenosine + dATP (toxic to lymphocytes) |
| **Wiskott-Aldrich** | WAS gene on Xp11 | Thrombocytopenia with **small platelets**, eczema, infections | **WIPE**: Wiskott-Aldrich, Immunodeficiency, (small) Platelets, Eczema |

#### Amyloidosis — the protein, not the disease

Amyloid fibrils share a **β-pleated sheet** configuration regardless of precursor protein, which is why every subtype stains Congo red and shows apple-green birefringence under polarized light. Electron microscopy reveals non-branching 7.5–10 nm fibrils.

| Subtype | Precursor protein | Clinical context |
|---|---|---|
| **AL (primary)** | Ig light chain (κ or λ) | Plasma cell dyscrasia, multiple myeloma; deposits in heart, kidney, tongue |
| **AA (secondary)** | Serum amyloid A | Chronic inflammation — RA, TB, osteomyelitis, familial Mediterranean fever; deposits in kidney, liver, spleen |
| **Dialysis-related (Aβ2M)** | β2-microglobulin | Long-term hemodialysis; joints (carpal tunnel) |
| **Familial ATTR** | Mutant transthyretin | Peripheral neuropathy, cardiomyopathy; autonomic dysfunction |
| **Senile (wild-type ATTR)** | Normal transthyretin | Restrictive cardiomyopathy in the elderly |

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Margin-of-mastery material: exceptions, edge cases, and exam-winning detail.

#### Edge cases worth banking

- **Autoantibody that causes disease by stimulation, not lysis — Graves disease.** Anti-TSHR IgG is agonistic; the thyroid is hyper-functional, not destroyed. Euthyroid Graves in pregnancy can transfer stimulating IgG across the placenta to cause neonatal thyrotoxicosis. This is the same Type II slot as Hashimoto's destructive anti-TPO/anti-thyroglobulin antibodies — slot is shared, mechanism diverges.
- **Goodpasture's double-organ attack.** Anti-GBM IgG targets the non-collagenous (NC1) domain of α3 chain of type IV collagen, present in pulmonary alveoli and renal glomeruli. The combination of pulmonary hemorrhage + rapidly progressive (crescentic) glomerulonephritis is the high-yield pattern.
- **Hashimoto straddles two mechanisms.** Anti-TPO and anti-thyroglobulin are Type II (antibody-mediated cellular injury by ADCC/complement), but the *chronic destruction* depends on CD8+ T cells and Th1 cytokine-driven apoptosis — that's Type IV. Most exam keys list Hashimoto as **Type IV with associated autoantibodies**, not Type II; SLE is the question you categorise by deposited complexes (Type III), not by antibody *type*.
- **Mixed cryoglobulinemia (Type II + III) in HCV.** IgM rheumatoid factor + polyclonal IgG against HCV antigen forms immune complexes that deposit in small vessels — palpable purpura, arthralgia, membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis.
- **Arthus reaction vs serum sickness — both Type III, different timelines.** Arthus is local, 4–10 hours, after a localised antigen (e.g., vaccine booster in a previously immunised limb). Serum sickness is systemic, 1–2 weeks after heterologous serum exposure (now seen after rituximab, infliximab, or streptokinase), with fever, urticaria, arthralgia, lymphadenopathy and glomerulonephritis.
- **Type IV hypersensitivity has two flavors.** Granulomatous (Th1 + IFN-γ + macrophages) for TB and sarcoidosis; direct cytotoxicity (CD8+ T cells + perforin/granzyme) for viral clearance, contact dermatitis, and tumour surveillance.

#### Mathematical and quantitative anchors

- **C3 and C4 both drop** in SLE (Type III) and PSGN because the classical pathway is consumed. In post-streptococcal GN specifically, C3 normalises in 6–8 weeks; persistent low C3 should prompt a workup for membranoproliferative GN or lupus nephritis.
- **CD4+ count thresholds in HIV (CD4+ T-cell depletion — secondary immunodeficiency):** opportunistic-infection risk rises steeply below 200 cells/µL; **Pneumocystis jirovecii** prophylaxis is initiated; **Mycobacterium avium complex** prophylaxis becomes relevant below 50 cells/µL. Exact prophylaxis thresholds must be verified in the current AIIMS/NACO protocol on the day of the exam — do not memorise a number that may have shifted.
- **Renal graft biopsy Banff classification** grades acute cellular rejection (IA, IB, IIA, IIB, III) by interstitial infiltrate (i), tubulitis (t), and arteritis (v), and chronic changes (ci, ct, cv, cg) by fibrosis. "i3 t3" signifies severe acute cellular rejection; "cg" indicates transplant glomerulopathy, a chronic antibody-mediated feature.
- **Serum tryptase peaks 60–90 minutes after anaphylaxis** and remains elevated for up to 6 hours — useful for retrospective diagnosis when a patient presents after epinephrine has already been given.
- **Amyloid P component (SAP) is universal across all amyloid types**; serum amyloid P scintigraphy is a research-grade imaging tool to quantify whole-body amyloid load.

#### Advanced practice prompts

1. A 55-year-old man with long-standing rheumatoid arthritis develops proteinuria and hepatomegaly. Renal biopsy shows mesangial deposition of amorphous eosinophilic material that is Congo-red positive. Name the amyloid subtype, the precursor protein, and the organ you would *not* expect to be dominantly involved. *Answer: AA, serum amyloid A, heart (cardiac involvement dominates in AL, not AA).*
2. A 7-month-old boy develops recurrent pneumonia, otitis media, and absent tonsillar tissue. Peripheral smear shows no CD19+ B cells; serum IgG, IgA, and IgM are all undetectable. What enzymatic defect is responsible, what is the inheritance pattern, and why did infections only begin *after* 6 months? *Answer: Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) — X-linked — IgG of maternal origin protected him during the first half-year.*
