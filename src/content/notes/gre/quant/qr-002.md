# Algebra — GRE Quantitative Reasoning Notes

Algebra on the GRE is not a test of symbol manipulation for its own sake. The questions are built so that the fastest route is usually **translating the words into one equation and then solving for what is asked** — often without ever finding the individual variables. A large share of algebra questions in Quantitative Comparison can be settled by manipulating the two quantities directly, which avoids solving entirely.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

**Format facts worth memorising**

- Algebra appears across both **35-minute, 20-question** Quantitative Reasoning sections, inside Quantitative Comparison, Problem Solving and Numeric Entry formats.
- **The calculator does not do algebra.** It cannot solve an equation, factor a quadratic, or graph a line. Algebra questions must be reasoned, not computed.
- Many questions supply **two equations in two unknowns**, but ask for a *combination* such as x + y rather than x or y separately. Add or subtract the equations to get the combination directly.

**The five identities worth knowing cold**

- (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²
- (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²
- (a + b)(a − b) = a² − b²
- a² + b² = (a + b)² − 2ab
- a³ ± b³ = (a ± b)(a² ∓ ab + b²)

The third identity is the highest-yield one on the GRE: any expression of the form *something squared minus something else squared* factors immediately.

**The single most useful habit**

Before solving, write down **exactly what is asked**. Questions that give x + 2y = 11 and 3x + 2y = 19 and then ask for x + y are answered by subtracting the first from the second to get 2x = 8, so x = 4, then y = 3.5, so x + y = 7.5 — but the subtraction alone already revealed the structure. Candidates who solve for both variables fully spend twice the time for the same mark.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

**Linear equations and systems**

- Isolate the variable by doing the same operation to both sides. When a fraction is present, multiply through by the denominator first.
- **Two equations, two unknowns**: add them if the coefficients of one variable are opposite; subtract if they are identical; otherwise multiply one equation to match coefficients, then add or subtract.
- **No solution** when the lines are parallel (same slope, different intercept) — the variables cancel and leave a false statement such as 0 = 5. **Infinitely many solutions** when the equations describe the same line — the variables cancel and leave a true statement such as 0 = 0.
- **Absolute value equations** |ax + b| = c split into two cases, ax + b = c and ax + b = −c. Both must be checked against the original, because squaring can introduce extraneous roots.

**Quadratics**

- Standard form ax² + bx + c = 0. **Quadratic formula**: x = [−b ± √(b² − 4ac)] / 2a.
- **Discriminant** b² − 4ac decides the root structure: positive → two distinct real roots; zero → one repeated root; negative → no real roots. A question asking *how many* solutions is answered by the discriminant alone, with no solving.
- **Factoring by inspection**: find two numbers multiplying to c and adding to b. For x² − 7x + 12, those are −3 and −4, so x² − 7x + 12 = (x − 3)(x − 4).
- **Sum and product of roots**: for ax² + bx + c = 0, the roots sum to −b/a and multiply to c/a. This settles many comparison questions without finding either root.

**Inequalities — the rule that catches people**

Multiplying or dividing an inequality by a **negative number reverses the direction of the sign**. So −2x > 6 gives x < −3, not x > −3. When a question involves a variable that could be negative, this reversal is exactly the mechanism that makes the answer *cannot be determined*.

**Functions and coordinate geometry**

- Function notation: f(x) is the output when x is the input. Composite questions give f(g(x)) — evaluate the inner function first.
- **Slope** = (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁). Parallel lines share a slope; perpendicular lines have slopes multiplying to −1.
- **Line**: y = mx + b, where m is slope and b is the y-intercept. Two points determine the line; a point and a slope do too.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

**Where the marks actually leak**

- **Solving for variables you were never asked for.** If the question wants x + y, manipulate the system to produce x + y. Time saved here is time spent on the hard questions later.
- **Forgetting the inequality reversal.** This is the most common single arithmetic error in GRE algebra and the most common source of a wrong answer on a comparison question.
- **Treating a "no solution" system as a mistake.** Some questions deliberately present parallel lines and expect the answer *cannot be determined* or *no solution*. Recognising the pattern is the skill.
- **Squaring both sides carelessly.** Squaring can create solutions that do not satisfy the original equation. Any radical or absolute-value question must end with a check.
- **Assuming integer answers.** Unless the question says integers, x may be a fraction, a negative, or irrational. Testing only whole numbers is what produces a confident wrong answer on a comparison question.

**Strategy specific to Quantitative Comparison**

Manipulate the two quantities **before substituting**. Add the same expression to both, subtract the same expression from both, or divide both by a positive common factor — the comparison survives, and the algebra often collapses to something obviously true. Never divide both sides by a variable whose sign is unknown; that is precisely the case where the relationship may not be determinable.

**A three-week drill schedule**

- **Week 1 — translation.** Take 20 word problems and, without solving, write the equation each one requires. Translation is the bottleneck, not manipulation.
- **Week 2 — quadratics and inequalities.** 20 questions per sitting restricted to these two families, with a written note on each inequality about whether the sign reversed.
- **Week 3 — mixed algebra under time.** Full 35-minute sections, tracking how many questions you solved fully versus how many you answered by manipulating the target expression directly.
