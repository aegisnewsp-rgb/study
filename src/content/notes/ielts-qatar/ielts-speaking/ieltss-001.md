# Part 1 — Introduction and Interview — IELTS Speaking (Qatar)

Part 1 of the IELTS Speaking test is the **Introduction and Interview**: a
4–5-minute exchange where the examiner introduces themselves, confirms the
candidate's identification, and asks **familiar questions on everyday themes**
(home, family, work, studies, hobbies, hometown, food, weather, weekends, daily
routine). The examiner asks **8–10 questions** in this part, and the candidate
is expected to give **short developed responses — not single sentences**.

Part 1 is the **warm-up** of the Speaking test. It is the lowest-pressure part
in the strict sense (the questions are predictable, the topics are everyday), but
it is the part that sets the tone for the rest of the test. A candidate who
gives yes/no answers in Part 1 will struggle to recover in Parts 2 and 3. A
candidate who gives 2–3 sentence responses with paraphrasing and varied
vocabulary signals to the examiner that they can sustain a band 7+ conversation.

In the Qatari cohort, Part 1 commonly anchors on themes that map to the
candidate's daily life: home / family / hometown / work / studies / hobbies /
weather / food / weekends. The questions are **always familiar**, never
abstract — Part 1 is not the place to show off your philosophical reasoning.

This note shows the LCA (Long-Read Cohort Appetite) version: a **Lite**
(5-minute) read, a **Standard** (15-minute) read, and the **Exam Essentials**
table a Qatari candidate can use to revise in the last hour before the test.

## Lite (4-Step Method)

1. **Listen to the question, then give a direct answer first.** Start with
   *"Yes, I do"*, *"No, I don't"*, *"I think so"*, *"Not really"*. The direct
   answer signals confidence; the examiner can see you understood the
   question.
2. **Justify the answer with one developed sentence.** Add *"because…"*,
   *"the reason is…"*, *"mostly because…"*. A direct answer + justification
   = 2 sentences, which is the **minimum** length for a Part 1 answer at
   band 6+.
3. **Add a concrete example or detail.** A specific detail (*"for example, my
   brother lives in Doha and we often meet for coffee on the Corniche"*)
   signals vocabulary range and depth. Aim for 2–3 sentences total per
   answer.
4. **Avoid yes/no answers and single-sentence answers.** Both signal band 5
   or below. Even when the question is a simple yes/no, expand to 2–3
   sentences.

**Insight: Part 1 is the part where the examiner sets the bar.** A
candidate who gives developed answers in Part 1 sets the bar at band 6+; a
candidate who gives yes/no answers sets the bar at band 5. The examiner
forms an early impression in Part 1 that carries through to the band score.

### 6 Examiner Traps (Part 1 — Introduction and Interview)

- **The yes/no trap** — candidates answer *"Yes"* or *"No"* and stop. The
  examiner expects 2–3 sentence answers. Always give a reason and an
  example.
- **The single-sentence trap** — candidates give one short sentence (*"I like
  reading"*) and stop. The examiner expects developed answers. Add a reason
  and an example.
- **The repetition trap** — candidates repeat the same phrase (*"because I
  like it"*) to fill time. Use varied justification phrases (*"the reason
  is…"*, *"I think this is because…"*, *"what I like about it is…"*).
- **The memorized answer trap** — Part 1 is where memorized answers are most
  easily exposed, because the examiner asks follow-ups that a memorized
  answer cannot anticipate.
- **The over-long answer trap** — candidates give 30-second answers to Part 1
  questions, which wastes time and signals poor pacing. Aim for 10–15 seconds
  per answer in Part 1.
- **The vocabulary trap** — candidates use only basic vocabulary (*"good"*
  / *"nice"* / *"interesting"*). Use varied vocabulary (*"enjoyable"* /
  *"fascinating"* / *"stimulating"*).

## Standard (7-Step Method with Long-Form Guidance)

### Step 1 — Listen to the question carefully

Part 1 questions are usually short and simple. Take 1–2 seconds after the
examiner finishes to make sure you have parsed the question correctly. If
unsure, you can ask the examiner to repeat the question (*"Could you repeat
the question, please?"* — the examiner will not mark you down for this).

### Step 2 — Give a direct answer first

Start with *"Yes, I do"*, *"No, I don't"*, *"I think so"*, *"Not really"*,
*"It depends"*, or another direct answer. The direct answer signals
confidence and shows you understood the question.

### Step 3 — Justify the answer

Immediately after the direct answer, give a reason. Use phrases like:
- *"because…"*
- *"the reason is…"*
- *"I think this is because…"*
- *"what I like about it is…"*

### Step 4 — Add a concrete example

For every abstract point, give a concrete example. The example can be from
your own experience, your family, your community, or a public figure /
event. Examples signal vocabulary range and depth.

### Step 5 — Use varied vocabulary

Avoid repeating the same word. Paraphrase the question (*"Do you like
reading?"* → *"Yes, I really enjoy reading"*) and use varied vocabulary
(*"fascinating"*, *"stimulating"*, *"captivating"* instead of *"interesting"*).

### Step 6 — Aim for 10–15 seconds per answer

A 5–10 second answer is too short. A 20–30 second answer is too long. Aim
for 10–15 seconds per answer in Part 1. That is roughly 2–3 sentences.

### Step 7 — Listen for the next question

After you answer, the examiner will ask the next question. Listen carefully;
do not start talking before you have parsed the new question. Repeat the
cycle.

## Previous Year Patterns (IELTS 2018–2025)

IELTS Speaking Part 1 has used the introduction-and-interview format
consistently since 2018, with 8–10 questions on familiar topics. Over
2018–2025:

- 2018–2020: Part 1 commonly anchored on **hometown, studies/work, family,
  hobbies** (80%+ of tests).
- 2021–2023: Part 1 diversified to include **food, weather, transport**
  themes.
- 2024–2025: Part 1 frequently anchors on **technology in daily life**
  themes (smartphones, social media, screens), reflecting global tech
  adoption.

The pattern: IELTS Speaking Part 1 themes have shifted slightly toward
technology and daily-life topics, but hometown / studies / family / hobbies
remain core. Candidates who prepare 2–3 specific examples per theme
outperform those who prepare only generic answers.

## Pro Tips (10 enumeration)

1. **Give a direct answer first.** *Yes, No, I think so, Not really.*
2. **Justify your answer.** Always give a reason.
3. **Add a concrete example.** Specific examples signal depth.
4. **Aim for 2–3 sentences.** Short developed responses, not single
   sentences.
5. **Use varied vocabulary.** Paraphrase the question; do not repeat the
   question wording.
6. **Use discourse markers.** *And, also, in addition, for example.*
7. **Avoid yes/no single-sentence answers.** They signal band 5 or below.
8. **Avoid memorized answers.** The examiner asks follow-ups you cannot
   anticipate.
9. **Time your answers.** Aim for 10–15 seconds per answer; do not run
   long.
10. **Listen carefully to each question.** Pause to parse; do not start
    before you have.
