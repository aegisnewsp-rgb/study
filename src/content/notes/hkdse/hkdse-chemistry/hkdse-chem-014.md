# XIV. Materials Chemistry (Elective) — HKDSE Chemistry Notes

This topic covers Elective Topic XIV of the HKDSE Chemistry Elective Part. Candidates sit any two of the three electives on Paper 2. The topic addresses metals and alloys; ceramics and glasses; polymers and composite materials; liquid crystals and their applications; nanomaterials and their properties; and green chemistry and sustainability.

> Verify the live HKDSE Chemistry Assessment Framework on https://www.hkeaa.edu.hk/ before planning revision.

---

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Metals and alloys**: pure metals (soft, malleable, conductive); alloys (harder, stronger, less malleable). Examples: brass, bronze, stainless steel, duralumin.
- **Ceramics**: inorganic non-metallic solids (oxides, nitrides, carbides); hard, brittle, heat-resistant. Examples: alumina, silicon carbide, zirconia, porcelain.
- **Glasses**: amorphous solids; transparent. Soda-lime glass (most common), borosilicate glass (heat-resistant, e.g. Pyrex), lead glass (high refractive index).
- **Polymers**: natural (cellulose, silk, rubber, DNA) and synthetic (Topic XI).
- **Composite materials**: two or more phases (matrix + reinforcement). Examples: fibreglass (glass fibre + polymer matrix), carbon-fibre reinforced polymer, concrete (cement + sand + aggregate), reinforced concrete (concrete + steel rebar).
- **Liquid crystals**: states between solid and liquid; orientational order but positional disorder. Applications: LCD displays (twisted nematic, in-plane switching).
- **Nanomaterials**: at least one dimension < 100 nm. Properties differ from bulk materials (high surface-area-to-volume ratio, quantum effects). Examples: carbon nanotubes, graphene, fullerenes, quantum dots, silver nanoparticles.
- **Green chemistry**: 12 principles for designing chemical products and processes that reduce or eliminate the use and generation of hazardous substances. Examples: use of safer solvents, design for degradation, atom economy.

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing glasses with ceramics — glasses are amorphous; ceramics are crystalline.
- Treating "nanomaterial" as a single class — nanomaterials include many types with different properties.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Metals and alloys

See Topic III for extraction and basic properties. Material properties:

- Pure metals: soft, malleable, ductile, good conductors of heat and electricity. The lattice of identical atoms allows planes to slide easily.
- Alloys: the addition of other atoms (different sizes) distorts the lattice and prevents planes from sliding, so alloys are harder and stronger than the parent metal.

Examples:

- **Brass**: Cu + Zn (20-45%). Harder than copper, malleable, corrosion-resistant. Used in musical instruments, fittings, plumbing.
- **Bronze**: Cu + Sn (~10%). Hard, low friction. Used in bearings, sculptures, bells.
- **Stainless steel**: Fe + Cr (≥10.5%) + Ni. Corrosion-resistant due to chromium oxide layer. Used in cutlery, surgical instruments, kitchenware.
- **Duralumin**: Al + Cu + Mg + Mn. Light and strong. Used in aircraft frames.
- **Solder**: Sn + Pb (or Sn + Ag, Ag-free). Low melting point. Used for joining metals in electronics.

#### Ceramics and glasses

**Ceramics**: inorganic non-metallic materials, typically crystalline, with high melting points, hardness, brittleness and chemical resistance. Examples: Al₂O₃ (alumina, used in cutting tools and spark plugs), SiC (silicon carbide, used in abrasives), Si₃N₄ (silicon nitride, used in engine components), ZrO₂ (zirconia, used in dental crowns and fuel cells).

**Glasses**: amorphous solids formed by cooling a melt fast enough to prevent crystallisation. No long-range order. Soda-lime glass (the most common, ~70% SiO₂, ~15% Na₂O, ~10% CaO) is used in windows and bottles. Borosilicate glass (with B₂O₃ replacing some Na₂O and CaO) has a low coefficient of thermal expansion (Pyrex). Lead glass (with PbO replacing CaO) has a high refractive index (used in optical glass, decorative crystal).

#### Polymers and composites

**Polymers** (Topic XI): addition and condensation polymers, natural and synthetic.

**Composites**: combine two or more materials to achieve properties not available in any single component. The matrix binds the reinforcement; the reinforcement provides strength.

Examples:

- **Fibreglass**: glass fibre + polyester resin. Light, strong, corrosion-resistant. Used in boat hulls, car bodies.
- **Carbon-fibre reinforced polymer (CFRP)**: carbon fibre + epoxy resin. Very strong, very light, expensive. Used in aircraft, racing cars, sports equipment.
- **Concrete**: cement + sand + aggregate + water. Compressive strength. Used in buildings, bridges, roads.
- **Reinforced concrete**: concrete + steel rebar. Compressive strength (concrete) + tensile strength (steel). Used in foundations, columns, beams.

#### Liquid crystals

Liquid crystals have properties between those of conventional liquids and crystals: they can flow like a liquid but have some orientational order (molecules tend to point in a particular direction).

Types:

- **Nematic**: molecules aligned in one direction but no positional order.
- **Smectic**: molecules in layers with positional order within layers.
- **Cholesteric (chiral nematic)**: molecules in layers, with the direction of alignment rotating from layer to layer.

Applications: **liquid crystal displays (LCDs)** — the twisted nematic (TN) display uses the optical activity of nematic liquid crystals controlled by an electric field. In-plane switching (IPS) and vertical alignment (VA) are alternatives.

#### Nanomaterials

A nanomaterial has at least one dimension < 100 nm. At this scale, properties differ from the bulk material:

- High surface-area-to-volume ratio (more reactive).
- Quantum effects (different optical, electronic properties).

Examples:

- **Carbon nanotubes**: cylindrical carbon structures with extraordinary strength and electrical conductivity. Used in composites, electronics.
- **Graphene**: single layer of carbon atoms in a hexagonal lattice. Extremely strong, highly conductive. Applications: electronics, sensors, composites.
- **Fullerenes**: spherical carbon structures (C60 buckminsterfullerene). Applications: lubricants, drug delivery, organic solar cells.
- **Quantum dots**: semiconductor nanocrystals that fluoresce in different colours depending on size. Applications: displays, biological imaging.
- **Silver nanoparticles**: antimicrobial. Used in wound dressings, food packaging, textiles.

#### Green chemistry

The 12 principles of green chemistry (Anastas and Warner, 1998):

1. Prevention: better to prevent waste than to treat it after creation.
2. Atom economy: design methods to maximise incorporation of all materials used into the final product.
3. Less hazardous chemical syntheses: design syntheses to use and generate substances with little or no toxicity to humans or the environment.
4. Designing safer chemicals: design chemical products to be effective with minimal toxicity.
5. Safer solvents and auxiliaries: avoid using solvents and auxiliaries where possible.
6. Design for energy efficiency: minimise energy requirements; conduct reactions at ambient temperature and pressure.
7. Use of renewable feedstocks: use raw materials from renewable sources where possible.
8. Reduce derivatives: minimise or avoid unnecessary derivatisation.
9. Catalysis: catalytic reagents are superior to stoichiometric reagents.
10. Design for degradation: design products so they break down to innocuous degradation products.
11. Real-time analysis for pollution prevention: develop analytical methodologies for real-time monitoring.
12. Inherently safer chemistry for accident prevention: minimise potential for chemical accidents.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Cross-topic links

- **III. Metals**: alloys are derived from pure metals.
- **XI. Chemistry of Carbon Compounds**: polymer structure determines properties.
- **XV. Analytical Chemistry (Elective)**: analytical techniques are used to characterise materials.

#### Exam technique

- **For material questions**: identify the material class (metal/alloy, ceramic, glass, polymer, composite, liquid crystal, nanomaterial); state the composition; cite the key property and a specific application.
- **For "compare" questions**: use a table with columns for composition, properties and applications.
- **For green chemistry questions**: cite the relevant principle(s); explain how the design choice implements them.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Ceramics are metals." No — ceramics are inorganic non-metallic solids.
- "All nanomaterials are toxic." Nanomaterials vary widely in toxicity; silver nanoparticles have antimicrobial properties, while some other nanomaterials have unknown biological effects.
- "Green chemistry is just about recycling." Green chemistry is about designing safer, more efficient chemical processes and products; recycling is one outcome.
