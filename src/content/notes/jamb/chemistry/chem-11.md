# Alkyl Halides and Alcohols

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Two functional groups. Halogen replacing H on an sp³ carbon gives an alkyl halide (R–X); hydroxyl on the same carbon gives an alcohol (R–OH). Bond polarity decides almost every property.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"Higher Boiling, More H-Bonds"** — alcohols beat alkyl halides, which beat alkanes of similar mass, because O–H···O hydrogen bonding stacks extra intermolecular force. The Memory Hook word is **HOX** — **H**-bonds for **O**H, **X** for dipole, neat alkanes for none.

| Property | Alcohol (R–OH) | Alkyl halide (R–X) | Alkane (R–H) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functional group | –OH | –F, –Cl, –Br, –I | C–H only |
| Polar bond? | Yes (C–O, O–H) | Yes (C–X) | No (≈ zero dipole) |
| H-bonding? | Yes (O–H···O) | No | No |
| B.p. order (same C number) | Highest | Middle | Lowest |

- Reactivity order of C–X: **R–I > R–Br > R–Cl > R–F** (C–I is weakest, breaks fastest).
- Classification by the carbon bearing –OH or –X: **1°** (one C attached), **2°** (two), **3°** (three).
- Lucas test in 30 seconds: 3° cloudy immediately, 2° in 5–10 min, 1° stays clear at room temperature.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Core mechanisms, the four pathways (SN1/SN2/E1/E2), and the named-test ladder.

#### Naming and Classification

R–X and R–OH are split into **primary (1°)**, **secondary (2°)** and **tertiary (3°)** by counting how many carbon atoms are bonded to the functional carbon. 2-methylpropan-2-ol is 3° because the C–OH carbon is bonded to three CH₃ groups; propan-1-ol is 1°. This single count drives every mechanism choice that follows.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix — Which Pathway Wins?

| Condition | Favours SN2 | Favours SN1 | Favours E2 | Favours E1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Substrate | 1°, methyl | 3°, benzylic, allylic | 3°, 2° | 3° |
| Nucleophile/base | Strong Nu⁻ (I⁻, CN⁻, OH⁻) | Weak Nu, neutral (H₂O, ROH) | Strong bulky base (t-BuOK) | Weak base, heat |
| Solvent | Polar aprotic (DMF, DMSO) | Polar protic (H₂O, ROH) | Polar aprotic | Polar protic |
| Temperature | Lower | Lower | Higher | Higher |
| Kinetics | rate = k[R–X][Nu⁻] | rate = k[R–X] | rate = k[R–X][base] | rate = k[R–X] |
| Stereochemistry | Inversion (Walden) | Racemisation | Anti-periplanar H removed | Carbocation, mixture |

#### Preparation Routes

- Alkyl halide ← alkane via **free-radical halogenation** (UV light, Cl₂ or Br₂).
- Alcohol ← alkene via **acid-catalysed hydration** (H₂O, H₃PO₄) or **oxymercuration** in lab courses.
- Alkyl halide ← alcohol using **HX, PCl₃, PCl₅, SOCl₂** or **P + I₂**.
- Grignard reagent: **R–X + Mg → R–Mg–X** in **dry ether** — trace water gives R–H + Mg(OH)X and ruins the reagent.

#### Alcohol Reactions Worth Memorising

- **Oxidation:** 1° → aldehyde → carboxylic acid (KMnO₄ or K₂Cr₂O₇/H⁺); 2° → ketone; 3° resists simple oxidation.
- **Dehydration:** conc. **H₂SO₄, 443 K**, gives an alkene; reactivity **3° > 2° > 1°** (E1 in textbooks).
- **Esterification:** R–OH + R′COOH ⇌ R′COOR + H₂O (acid catalyst, Fischer).
- **With sodium:** 2 R–OH + 2 Na → 2 R–ONa + H₂↑ (hydrogen gas evolution is the JAMB cue).

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

A student adds anhydrous ZnCl₂ dissolved in concentrated HCl to three test tubes containing butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol at room temperature. Which observation correctly identifies 2-methylpropan-2-ol as a tertiary alcohol?

**Options:** (A) All three turn cloudy within 1 minute. (B) Only butan-1-ol turns cloudy after 5 minutes. (C) The 2-methylpropan-2-ol tube turns cloudy immediately while the others remain clear. (D) No reaction occurs in any tube because water is absent.

**Resolution step by step.** Lucas reagent is ZnCl₂/HCl. ZnCl₂ is a Lewis acid that coordinates to the oxygen of –OH, weakening the C–O bond and ionising it to a carbocation. A 3° carbocation forms readily because three alkyl groups donate electron density and stabilise the positive charge. Cloudiness comes from the **insoluble alkyl chloride** (R–Cl) precipitating out of the aqueous acidic layer.

- Butan-1-ol is 1° → cannot form a stable primary carbocation → no reaction at room temperature.
- Butan-2-ol is 2° → reacts slowly → turbidity only after 5–10 min.
- 2-methylpropan-2-ol is 3° → immediate cloudiness (<1 min).

Correct choice is **(C)**. Option (D) is wrong because the reaction does not require water; ZnCl₂ supplies the Lewis acidity, and HCl supplies Cl⁻.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students pick (A) thinking "alcohols all react the same way" — they forget that the Lucas test discriminates alcohols by carbocation stability, not by the mere presence of –OH.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Edge cases, reactivity inversions, and the mistakes JAMB repeats every year.

#### Reactivity Inversions You Must Not Miss

- **C–X bond strength drops down the halogen group:** C–F is so strong that RF is practically inert toward SN1/SN2; RI is fastest. JAMB frames this as "which halide reacts fastest with KCN?" — answer is the iodide.
- **Aromatic inversion:** chlorobenzene (Ar–Cl) resists SN1/SN2 because the sp² carbon cannot undergo backside attack and a phenyl cation is destabilised. Treating Ar–Cl like an alkyl halide in a hydrolysis question is a guaranteed loss of marks.
- **Phenol ≠ alcohol:** Ar–OH is acidic (pKa ≈ 10) because the phenoxide ion is resonance-stabilised over the ring. Tests for aliphatic alcohols (Lucas, dehydration, Na metal) give different or no results on phenol.

#### Advanced Traps and Exceptions

1. **Grignard + dry ether:** "dry" means **anhydrous**, not merely "no liquid water visible" — ether must be freshly distilled over sodium to remove dissolved moisture and peroxides.
2. **Wurtz coupling:** 2 R–X + 2 Na → R–R + 2 NaX gives symmetric alkanes; mixing two different R–X produces a mixture of three products (R–R, R′–R′, R–R′), so Wurtz is clean only for a single halide.
3. **E2 vs substitution with alc. KOH:** alcoholic KOH favours elimination (E2); aqueous KOH favours substitution (SN2). The same base, different solvents, different products.
4. **SN1 racemisation vs SN2 inversion:** a chiral 2° halide in polar protic solvent gives a racemic mix, not a single enantiomer — examiners test this stereochemical consequence directly.
5. **Dehydration temperature:** concentrated H₂SO₄ at ~443 K gives the alkene; the same acid at lower temperatures (~413 K) gives the ether (Williamson-type condensation) instead.
6. **Isomer recognition:** C₂H₆O can be CH₃CH₂OH (ethanol) or CH₃OCH₃ (dimethyl ether); C₂H₅Cl has no functional isomer with the same formula because chlorine is monovalent. JAMB asks for functional-group isomers, and only alcohols/ethers share the CₙH₂ₙ₊₂O formula.
