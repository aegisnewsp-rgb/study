# Polymers and Natural Polymers

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **polymer** is a giant molecule built by linking many small **monomers** through **polymerisation**. The **degree of polymerisation** is calculated as: molecular mass of polymer ÷ molecular mass of monomer. Two reaction routes exist: **addition polymerisation** (C=C double bonds open and join, no by-product, e.g. ethene → polythene) and **condensation polymerisation** (functional groups react with loss of small molecules like H₂O or HCl, e.g. amino acids → proteins, diamines + dicarboxylic acids → nylon). Natural polymers include **carbohydrates** (starch, cellulose — polymers of glucose), **proteins** (polymers of α-amino acids joined by peptide bonds) and **natural rubber** (polyisoprene). Synthetic polymers include polythene, PVC, nylon and polyester. WAEC WASSCE loves comparing addition vs condensation reactions and naming natural polymer sources.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Definitions
A **monomer** is a small molecule that can chemically bond to identical or similar molecules to form a polymer. The number of repeat units in a polymer chain is the **degree of polymerisation (n)**. The empirical formula of the repeat unit is identical to that of the monomer in addition polymerisation, but differs in condensation polymerisation because small molecules (H₂O, HCl, NH₃) are eliminated.

#### Addition Polymerisation
Monomers must contain a C=C double bond (e.g. ethene CH₂=CH₂, chloroethene CH₂=CHCl, phenylethene C₆H₅CH=CH₂). The π-bond breaks and monomers link head-to-tail:

nCH₂=CH₂ → –(CH₂–CH₂)ₙ–

Initiation uses radicals (benzoyl peroxide), ions or Ziegler–Natta catalysts. No atoms leave, so the polymer has the same empirical formula as the monomer.

#### Condensation Polymerisation
Monomers carry two reactive functional groups (–OH, –COOH, –NH₂). Each bond formed releases a small molecule:
- **Proteins:** nH₂N–CHR–COOH → –(NH–CHR–CO)ₙ– + (n–1)H₂O (peptide/amide link)
- **Nylon:** diamine + dicarboxylic acid → polyamide + water
- **Starch/cellulose:** nC₆H₁₂O₆ → (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ + (n–1)H₂O

#### Natural Polymers Summary

| Natural Polymer | Monomer | Linkage | Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starch | α-glucose | α-1,4-glycosidic | Condensation |
| Cellulose | β-glucose | β-1,4-glycosidic | Condensation |
| Proteins | α-amino acids | Peptide (amide) | Condensation |
| Natural rubber | Isoprene (2-methyl-1,3-butadiene) | C=C addition | Addition |

#### Rubber and Vulcanisation
Raw rubber (cis-1,4-polyisoprene) is soft and sticky. **Vulcanisation** heats it with sulphur (~3–5%); sulphur bridges form short cross-links between chains, increasing elasticity, tensile strength and temperature range.

#### Typical WAEC Patterns
- Give a polymerisation equation and ask whether it is addition or condensation (look for H₂O/HCl by-product).
- Identify the monomer from a given repeat unit (divide by n, restore C=C).
- Distinguish proteins (amino acids) from carbohydrates (glucose).
- State one use of vulcanised rubber (tyre treads, hoses).

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Thermoplastics vs Thermosets
**Thermoplastics** (polythene, PVC, nylon) have linear or branched chains held by weak intermolecular forces; they soften on heating and can be remoulded, which aids recycling. **Thermosets** (Bakelite, melamine, hardened epoxy) develop permanent 3-D covalent cross-links during curing; further heating decomposes them rather than melting. **Elastomers** (vulcanised rubber) have sparse cross-links that allow stretching and recovery.

#### Properties from Structure
Chain length increases tensile strength and melting point up to a limit. Branching lowers density and crystallinity (LDPE vs HDPE). Cross-linking raises rigidity and heat resistance. Strong intermolecular forces (H-bonding in nylon, Kevlar) raise melting points dramatically — nylon-6,6 melts at ~265 °C because of hydrogen bonding between –CO–NH– groups.

#### Worked Example 1
A polymer has molecular mass 168 000 g mol⁻¹ and its monomer (CH₂=CHCl, M = 62.5 g mol⁻¹) is the repeat unit. Calculate the degree of polymerisation.
n = 168 000 ÷ 62.5 = **2688 repeat units**.

#### Worked Example 2
Identify the monomer of –(CH₂–CH(C₆H₅))ₙ– (polystyrene). Open the repeat unit, restore the C=C: **C₆H₅–CH=CH₂** (phenylethene/styrene). The reaction is addition polymerisation because no by-product is released.

#### Common Mistakes
- Writing H₂O as a product of addition polymerisation (only condensation loses small molecules).
- Saying proteins are made from glucose — they are made from α-amino acids; glucose forms carbohydrates.
- Drawing the repeat unit of nylon with single (–CO–NH–) instead of showing both reactants (diacid + diamine).
- Confusing starch (α-glucose, digestible) with cellulose (β-glucose, indigestible to humans due to β-1,4 linkages).

#### Practice Prompts
1. A condensation polymer releases HCl on formation; one monomer is ethane-1,2-diol. Name the other monomer and state the class of the polymer.
2. Explain why vulcanised rubber is more elastic and heat-resistant than raw natural rubber, using the idea of cross-linking.
