# E. Efficiency, Equity and the Role of Government — HKDSE Economics Notes

This topic covers Topic E of the Compulsory Part of HKDSE Economics. It addresses market failure (externalities, public goods, merit goods, demerit goods, information failure); the role of government (Pigouvian taxes and subsidies, tradable permits, regulation, provision of public goods); and equity versus efficiency in policy choice.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Market failure**: the market fails to allocate resources efficiently. Causes: externalities, public goods, merit goods, demerit goods, information failure, market power.
- **Externality**: a side effect on a third party not involved in the transaction. Positive (e.g. vaccination, education) or negative (e.g. pollution, smoking).
- **Negative externality**: private marginal cost (PMC) < social marginal cost (SMC). The market over-produces.
- **Positive externality**: private marginal benefit (PMB) < social marginal benefit (SMB). The market under-produces.
- **Public good**: non-rivalrous and non-excludable (e.g. national defence, street lighting, flood control). The market under-provides; the government must provide.
- **Common-pool resource**: rivalrous but non-excludable (e.g. fish stocks, common grazing land). Subject to the tragedy of the commons.
- **Merit good**: a good the government considers beneficial. Often with positive externalities (e.g. education, healthcare). The market under-produces.
- **Demerit good**: a good the government considers harmful. Often with negative externalities (e.g. cigarettes, alcohol). The market over-produces.
- **Information failure**: asymmetric information or lack of information leads to suboptimal decisions (e.g. used car market, health insurance).
- **Pigouvian tax**: a tax equal to the marginal external cost. Internalises the externality.
- **Pigouvian subsidy**: a subsidy equal to the marginal external benefit. Encourages positive-externality production or consumption.
- **Tradable permits**: government issues a fixed number of permits; firms can trade. Used for pollution control (cap-and-trade).
- **Regulation**: government rules that mandate or restrict behaviour (e.g. emission standards, minimum wage).
- **Equity**: fairness in the distribution of resources or welfare.
- **Trade-off between equity and efficiency**: redistributive taxation can reduce incentives to work, but improve equity.

#### Examiner traps

- Treating all externalities as negative — some are positive.
- Confusing merit goods with public goods — merit goods are excludable (e.g. education can be gated by fees); public goods are not.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Negative externalities

Sources: pollution (air, water, soil); congestion; smoking (passive smoking); alcohol consumption.

**Pigouvian tax**: a tax equal to the marginal external cost. Shifts the private marginal cost curve up to equal the social marginal cost. The new equilibrium is at the socially optimal output.

Examples: carbon tax, gasoline tax, tobacco tax, alcohol tax.

#### Positive externalities

Sources: vaccination, education, R&D, beekeeping.

**Pigouvian subsidy**: a subsidy equal to the marginal external benefit. Shifts the private marginal benefit curve up to equal the social marginal benefit. The new equilibrium is at the socially optimal output.

Examples: subsidies for vaccinations, R&D tax credits, subsidies for environmental improvements.

#### Public goods

**Two characteristics**:

- **Non-rivalrous**: one person's consumption does not reduce another person's consumption.
- **Non-excludable**: it is impossible or very costly to prevent non-payers from consuming.

Examples: national defence, street lighting, lighthouses, flood control, basic research.

The market fails to provide public goods (free-rider problem). The government provides them, funded by taxation.

#### Common-pool resources

Rivalrous but non-excludable. Subject to overuse (tragedy of the commons).

Examples: fish stocks, groundwater, common grazing land, the atmosphere (carbon sink).

Solutions: government regulation, quotas, tradable permits, community management.

#### Merit and demerit goods

**Merit goods**: the government considers beneficial, often with positive externalities. The market under-produces because consumers undervalue them.

Examples: education, healthcare, preventive healthcare, vaccinations.

Government action: subsidies, public provision, regulation, information campaigns.

**Demerit goods**: the government considers harmful, often with negative externalities. The market over-produces because consumers overvalue them.

Examples: cigarettes, alcohol, gambling.

Government action: taxes (Pigouvian taxes), regulation, bans, public information.

#### Information failure

Asymmetric information: one party has more information than the other.

- **Adverse selection**: before the transaction, the better-informed party exploits the less-informed party (e.g. used car market — sellers know the quality).
- **Moral hazard**: after the transaction, the better-informed party changes behaviour (e.g. insurance — insured people may take more risks).
- **Principal-agent problem**: an agent (e.g. doctor, manager) acts on behalf of a principal (e.g. patient, shareholder) but may have different incentives.

Solutions: regulation, disclosure requirements, third-party verification, market mechanisms (signalling, screening).

#### Equity vs efficiency

**Equity**: fairness in distribution.

**Efficiency**: maximum total surplus.

**Policies to promote equity**:

- **Progressive taxation**: higher-income individuals pay a higher proportion of income in tax.
- **Transfer payments**: welfare, unemployment benefits, pensions.
- **Public provision of merit goods**: free or subsidised education, healthcare.

**Trade-off**: redistributive policies can reduce incentives to work, invest, or take risks, lowering total output and efficiency. The optimal balance is debated.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Cross-topic links

- **C. Market and Price**: welfare analysis.
- **D. Competition and Market Structure**: market power as a cause of market failure.
- **J. International Trade and Finance**: international externalities (carbon emissions, pollution).

#### Exam technique

- **For externality questions**: identify the type (positive/negative); state the divergence between private and social marginal cost/benefit; propose the appropriate Pigouvian policy.
- **For public good questions**: test for non-rivalry and non-excludability; explain why the market fails.
- **For equity-efficiency questions**: identify the trade-off; propose a balanced policy.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "All externalities can be solved by taxes." Some externalities (e.g. climate change) are hard to tax; regulation or tradable permits may be more effective.
- "Public goods are provided free by the government." They are funded by taxation; the provision is not "free" in an economic sense.
- "Equity means equal outcomes." Equity means fairness; it may be achieved by equal opportunities rather than equal outcomes.
