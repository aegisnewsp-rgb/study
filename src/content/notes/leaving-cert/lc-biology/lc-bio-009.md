# The Origins of Life: Evolution — Leaving Certificate (Established) Biology Notes

Unit 1.5 closes Strand 1 with the theory that holds the whole discipline together. The specification's own framing is worth reading carefully: evolution is presented as the **best explanation** for how life on Earth has and continues to evolve, and as the mechanism for explaining common origins and predicting future directions for specialised organisms. That is a scientific claim about explanatory power, and Higher-level questions test it as one — the paper does not ask "is evolution true", it asks "what evidence supports this explanation, how strong is each line, and what does the evidence not settle".

Three ideas carry the unit: **variation**, **selection**, and **isolation**. Variation is the raw material and comes from mutation, recombination and sexual reproduction. Natural selection is the filter that acts on it. Isolation is what turns a selected population into a new species. Everything the specification lists as evidence — embryology, fossils, phylogeny, comparative anatomy, antibiotic resistance, speciation — is a different line of support for the same account.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Variation within a population** arises from genetic variation (mutation and recombination during meiosis) and from environmental variation. Only **heritable** variation can be acted on by natural selection. Environmental variation acquired during life is not passed on — the classic example is the blacksmith's muscular arm, which develops through use in one lifetime.
- **Mutation** is a change in a gene (gene mutation) or in a whole chromosome (chromosome mutation). Mutation is the only source of *new* alleles; recombination only reshuffles existing ones. Mutations are random with respect to need — they are not produced because an organism requires them.
- **Natural selection** in four conditions: (1) there is **variation**; (2) some of that variation is **heritable**; (3) individuals with certain variants **survive and reproduce better**; (4) this happens over **many generations**. Remove any one condition and selection does not occur. Natural selection increases the frequency of alleles that improve reproductive success in the current environment — it does not aim at anything.
- **Direction of selection.** *Directional* selection favours one extreme (peppered moth; antibiotic resistance). *Stabilising* selection favours intermediate values and acts against both extremes (birth weight in humans). *Disruptive/diversifying* selection favours both extremes against the intermediate (finches with different beak sizes on separate islands).
- **Selection acts on phenotype, not genotype.** Only the expressed, observable characteristic affects survival; the underlying allele's frequency changes as a consequence. This distinction explains why recessive alleles can persist in a population hidden in heterozygotes.
- **Natural vs artificial selection.** Natural selection has no goal and no human agency; artificial selection is directed by humans for a chosen outcome. Both use the same raw material — variation — and both change allele frequencies, which is why selection programmes can produce dramatic change such as the many breeds of domestic dog from a single ancestral species.
- **Isolation and speciation.** Allopatric isolation (geographical barrier) and sympatric isolation (same area, different niche or behaviour). Isolation reduces gene flow; populations diverge genetically; when they can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring they are separate species. **Allopatric speciation** in the peppered moth and in the Galápagos finches (named in the specification) is the standard account; the apple maggot fly is the standard sympatric example.
- **Evidence the specification names:** embryology (similar early development across vertebrates), the fossil record (shows change over time in the same lineage), phylogeny (branching relationships inferred from shared derived characters), comparative anatomy (homologous and analogous structures), antibiotic resistance, and speciation itself.
- **The concept of a theory.** A scientific theory is a well-substantiated explanation of a broad set of observations, repeatedly tested and supported, not a guess. Evolution is one of the most extensively tested theories in science.

#### Examiner traps

- **Writing "the organism needs to adapt and the population changes".** Adaptation by need is Lamarckian and wrong. Mutations arise randomly; selection is non-random.
- **Calling an acquired characteristic inherited.** Big muscles from exercise are not heritable. Only pre-existing heritable variation is acted on.
- **Stopping at "favourable traits survive".** Add *heritable*, and add that survival alone is not enough — it is **reproductive success**, measured by contribution to the next generation.
- **Confusing homologous and analogous structures.** Homologous structures share a common ancestor (human arm, whale flipper, bat wing) and may have different functions; analogous structures have similar functions but different origins (bird and insect wing).
- **Treating a fossil as proof on its own.** A fossil is evidence; the argument is the *pattern* across many fossils in a dated sequence.
- **Saying selection makes a species "perfect".** Selection only improves fit to the current environment; conditions change, and a formerly advantageous allele can become disadvantageous.

- [ ] Can you state the four conditions for natural selection without looking?
- [ ] Can you distinguish directional, stabilising and disruptive selection from a described example?
- [ ] Can you explain a named case of antibiotic resistance using variation + selection?

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### Explaining natural selection properly — the four-step paragraph

This is the single most-used answer structure in the topic. Learn it as a paragraph skeleton and adapt it.

> *In a population of [organism], [environmental factor] [changed or is]. Individuals already show variation in [trait], and this variation is caused by [random mutation / recombination], not by the organism needing the trait. Those individuals whose [trait] makes them [less / more] likely to [survive and reproduce] in the new conditions leave more offspring. The allele for [trait] is therefore carried to more of the next generation, and its frequency rises. Over many generations the population changes, and the change is described as evolution by natural selection.*

*Worked example — antibiotic resistance.* In a hospital population of a bacterium, a small number carry a mutation conferring resistance to a particular antibiotic. When the antibiotic is given, susceptible bacteria die while the resistant ones survive and divide. The next generation is overwhelmingly resistant. Repeating the treatment selects more strongly each time. Nobody induced the mutation — the antibiotic selected it. That is the whole point of the example, and it is why the specification lists it as evidence.

#### Reading evidence critically

The specification asks you to consider evidence and discuss how the theory is important, which means each line of evidence has a **strength and a limitation**. Being able to name both is worth more than naming three and explaining none.

| Evidence | What it shows | What it does not settle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fossil record | Change over time within lineages; transitional forms | Fossilisation is rare and biased; soft tissue almost never preserved |
| Embryology | Similar early development across related groups | Convergence can produce similar embryos independently |
| Comparative anatomy | Homologous structures imply common ancestry | Analogous structures look similar but arose independently |
| Phylogeny | Branching relationships from shared derived characters | Depends on which characters are chosen as informative |
| Antibiotic resistance | A selection event observed in real time | Shows the mechanism, not the full history of the group |
| DNA and molecular sequencing | Genetic distance between organisms | Cannot recover organisms that never left fossils |

#### Artificial selection and the domestication argument

Artificial selection is the strongest **experimental** support for the idea that selection changes a species, because humans have imposed it deliberately, recorded the parents chosen each generation, and can reproduce the result. The specification's concern is the direction of the process: under artificial selection the outcome is whatever the breeder wants, which demonstrates that selection can drive dramatic change but tells us nothing about what happens without a human choosing. Under natural selection the environment is the selecting agent and there is no goal.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Worked Higher-level questions with answers

**Q1.** A population of bacteria is exposed to antibiotic A for 10 generations and then to antibiotic B. All the bacteria survive antibiotic A, but resistance to B is lower in the resistant strain than in the original. Explain.
*A.* The population had a resistance allele present at low frequency before any antibiotic was used — a pre-existing mutation, not one caused by A. Antibiotic A selected strongly for it, and every bacterium in the population now carries it, so it is fixed. That does not mean A conferred resistance to B. Resistance to B depends on a different gene, and the population is still polymorphic at that locus. Because the population has passed through a selective bottleneck imposed by A, it has lost genetic diversity, so alleles at other loci are present at lower frequency than before — the classic founder/bottleneck effect. Resistance to one drug may also carry a fitness cost, so a bacterium resistant to A may actually be *less* fit without A.

**Q2.** Evaluate the statement "the fossil record shows that humans evolved from monkeys".
*A.* The record shows that humans and modern monkeys share a common ancestor, which is a different claim. Fossil evidence is consistent with humans and modern monkeys having diverged from a common ancestral population over tens of millions of years; it does not show that any living monkey changed into a human, because the lineages are branches of a tree, not a ladder. Direct ancestry in the strict sense is also not demonstrable from fossils, because a fossil population is a sample of a generation rather than the whole population. The claim as stated is therefore a popularisation, not a supported inference.

**Q3.** A population curve shows a species at carrying capacity, then a new predator arrives and the population crashes to a low level. Explain in terms of selection.
*A.* The original population was already selected for traits that avoid the previous limiting factors. The new predator imposes a new selection pressure, and individuals carrying any pre-existing heritable variation giving better camouflage, different behaviour or different burrowing success survive better and reproduce more. The population's allele frequencies shift towards those variants over generations. If the crash has taken numbers so low that variation is greatly reduced, the population may lack the variation needed to respond to the next change — the bottleneck effect, and the reason small populations are at high risk of extinction.

**Q4.** Why is the theory of evolution more than "just a theory"?
*A.* In science, a theory is an explanation built from many lines of evidence that predicts new observations and has survived repeated attempts to falsify it. Evolution predicts that related groups share DNA to a degree proportional to how recently they diverged, that transitional fossils should appear in dated strata, and that resistance should evolve wherever a selection pressure is applied. Each prediction has been tested and holds. What a theory is *not* is a guess or an untested idea — that is a hypothesis. The specification makes this point explicitly and it is a reliable short question.

#### How this unit pays elsewhere

- **Unit 1.2** supplies the mechanisms: mutations arise during DNA replication, and proteins built from the mutated gene give the phenotype on which selection acts.
- **Unit 1.4** supplies the variation: recombination and independent assortment produce a new combination of alleles in every generation.
- **Unit 2.4** supplies a case study the specification names in its own right — antibiotic resistance as evidence for evolution by natural selection.
- **Unit 3.3** is the mirror image: humans now *direct* the production of variation, cutting DNA with restriction enzymes and inserting genes. Artificial selection by design rather than by breeding.
- **The Biology in Practice Investigation** is 40% of the grade. Investigating antibiotic resistance in common bacteria, or the effect of an environmental factor on a population, produces exactly the primary-data-with-justified-conclusions evidence the assessment descriptors reward.

#### Mistakes students actually make

1. **Writing "organisms adapt during their lifetime and pass it on."** This is Lamarck's inheritance of acquired characteristics and it is the single most penalised error in the topic. Selection acts on variation that already exists.
2. **Conflating variation and selection.** Variation is random with respect to need; selection is non-random. Keep both halves of that sentence.
3. **Saying "the fittest survive".** Fitness is reproductive success relative to others in the same population and environment, not strength. Many ailing individuals out-reproduce an athlete.
4. **Naming homology and analogy as synonyms.** One is ancestry, one is function.
5. **Offering evidence with no evaluation.** A "discuss evidence" question wants a strength and a limitation per line, not a list.
6. **Writing "the theory of evolution".** The specification expects you to say what a theory is. Dropping the word costs an easy mark.
7. **Ignoring human influence in local ecosystems.** Unit 3.1 asks you to evaluate primary data on the effects of human activity on species diversity — a place where overhunting, overfishing and habitat destruction are selection pressures acting now.
