# Average and Mixtures

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

The **arithmetic mean** of $n$ observations equals their sum divided by $n$: $\bar{x} = \dfrac{\sum x_i}{n}$, in the same unit as the data. A **weighted average** multiplies each value by its frequency or quantity weight before summing. **Mixtures** combine two ingredients of different concentrations or prices; the **rule of alligation** gives the mixing ratio as (Dear − Mean) : (Mean − Cheap).

- **Quick alligation ratio:** Cheap : Dear = (Dear Price − Mean Price) : (Mean Price − Cheap Price)
- **Average shift shortcut:** New average after adding one value $v$ = $\dfrac{n\bar{x} + v}{n + 1}$
- **Mixture concentration:** % = $\dfrac{\text{Solute}}{\text{Solution}} \times 100$

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Definitions and core formulas

The **arithmetic mean** is the balance point of a data set. For values $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ with sum $S$, the mean is $\bar{x} = \dfrac{S}{n}$. A **weighted average** arises when each value contributes a different weight $w_i$ (frequency, quantity, mass), giving $\bar{x}_w = \dfrac{\sum w_i x_i}{\sum w_i}$.

In **mixture problems**, two solutions of different concentrations are combined. The **rule of alligation** finds the ratio in which they must be mixed to obtain a target concentration $M$ lying between the two source concentrations $C_1$ (cheap/lower) and $C_2$ (dear/higher):

$$\frac{Q_1}{Q_2} = \frac{C_2 - M}{M - C_1}$$

#### Change in average

If the average of $n$ items is $\bar{x}$ and a new item of value $v$ is added, the new average becomes $\bar{x}' = \dfrac{n\bar{x} + v}{n+1}$. Removing an item gives $\bar{x}' = \dfrac{n\bar{x} - v}{n-1}$. This single-step shift is the most frequently tested average variant on HAT-UG.

#### Worked formula relationships

| Concept | Formula | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple mean | $S / n$ | Equal-weight data set |
| Weighted mean | $\sum w_i x_i / \sum w_i$ | Different quantities per item |
| Alligation ratio | $(C_2 - M) : (M - C_1)$ | Two-mixture blending |
| New average (add) | $(n\bar{x} + v)/(n+1)$ | One value joined |
| New average (remove) | $(n\bar{x} - v)/(n-1)$ | One value dropped |

#### Standard problem patterns

- Average of a class after a new student joins with marks $v$.
- Ratio of two qualities (e.g. milk and water) to produce a target mixture percentage.
- Successive replacement: withdrawing $x$ litres from a vessel and refilling with water $k$ times reduces solute concentration by the factor $\left(1 - \dfrac{x}{V}\right)^k$.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge cases and the alligation mechanism

Alligation assumes the two ingredients are mixed in **comparable total volumes**. If one vessel holds 40 L of 20% acid and another holds 60 L of 35% acid, the cheap-to-dear quantity ratio from the formula $(35 - M):(M - 20)$ gives the **mixing proportion**, not the absolute volumes. The actual quantities taken are proportional to this ratio.

A frequent trap appears when a vessel of capacity $V$ contains a fraction $f$ of solute, and a volume $x$ is repeatedly replaced by water. After $k$ replacements, the remaining solute fraction is $f\left(1 - \dfrac{x}{V}\right)^k$. Students often compute only the first step and forget the exponent $k$.

#### Connection to ratio and proportion

Average and Mixture problems reduce to **ratio reasoning** at heart. HAT-UG Quantitative Reasoning pairs these questions with ratio–proportion items; recognising the underlying proportion saves calculation time.

#### Common mistakes

- Treating a weighted average like a simple mean when one ingredient is present in larger quantity.
- Forgetting to update $n$ by $\pm 1$ in average-shift problems.
- Inverting the alligation ratio direction, producing the dear-to-cheap ratio instead.
- Adding concentration percentages instead of converting to actual solute mass or volume first.

#### Practice prompts

1. The average of 8 numbers is 42. A 9th number 66 is added; another number 30 is then removed. Find the final average.
2. In what ratio must tea costing Rs. 280/kg be mixed with tea costing Rs. 320/kg so that the mixture sells at Rs. 300/kg?
