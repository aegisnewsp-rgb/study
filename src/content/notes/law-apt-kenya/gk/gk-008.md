# Sources of Kenyan Law (3)

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Quick map of where Kenyan binding rules come from — memorise the rank order before you touch a past paper.

Kenyan law flows from six ranked origins, with the Constitution of Kenya 2010 sitting at the top and customary law at the bottom. The Judicature Act (Cap 8) is the door through which English common law, equity, and pre-1897 statutes of general application enter Kenya, while Article 2(5)–(6) of the Constitution controls how international law enters.

| Rank | Source | Statutory anchor | Binding force |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Constitution of Kenya 2010 | Article 2(1)–(2) | Supreme — any inconsistent law is void |
| 2 | Acts of Parliament | Article 94–95 | Binding across Kenya |
| 3 | Delegated legislation | Parent Act + Statutory Instruments Act | Limited to parent Act's vires |
| 4 | Common law, equity, statutes of general application | Judicature Act s.3(1)(c) | Subject to local circumstances |
| 5 | African customary law | Judicature Act s.3(2) | Subject to repugnancy clause |
| 6 | Customary international law | Article 2(5); treaties via 2(6) | 2(5) auto-binds; 2(6) needs domestication |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook — "CAP-ED-CS-CI":** **C**onstitution → **A**cts of **P**arliament → **E**quity (with delegated) → **D**octrines of equity merged → **C**ommon law & statutes of general application → **C**ustomary law → **I**nternational law (2(5) + 2(6)).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Constitutional supremacy and received English law

Article 2(1) declares the Constitution the supreme law. Article 2(4) invalidates any other law inconsistent with it. Section 3(1)(c) of the Judicature Act is the gateway for English common law and doctrines of equity, and section 3(1)(d) admits statutes of general application — those English statutes in force on **12 August 1897** that were extended to Kenya by the Kenya (Annexation) Order in Council 1920.

A frequent confusion is that "received English law" is one stream. It is three: (a) common law (judge-made rules), (b) equity (the old Court of Chancery rules), and (c) statutes of general application. Kenya merged (a) and (b) under section 3(1)(c), but they retain separate historical identities — examiners test this.

#### Customary, personal and religious law

African customary law applies under section 3(2) of the Judicature Act only "so far as it is not repugnant to justice and morality or inconsistent with any written law." Hindu law and Islamic law govern personal matters (marriage, divorce, inheritance) for the communities concerned. The Indian Penal Code (Cap 63) and allied pre-1963 Indian Acts are not English law — they are specific Kenyan statutes retained from the colonial reception period and amended locally.

| Feature | Common law | Equity | African customary law |

#### Stare decisis and the court hierarchy

The doctrine of binding precedent works vertically in Kenya:

- **Supreme Court** binds all other courts (Article 163(7)).
- **Court of Appeal** binds the High Court, magistrates' courts, and tribunals below it.
- **High Court** decisions bind magistrates' courts but not other High Court divisions — High Court decisions are persuasive among themselves.
- **Magistrates' courts** are bound upward only.

A Supreme Court decision can only be departed from by the Supreme Court itself; a Court of Appeal decision can only be overruled by the Supreme Court or a later Court of Appeal sitting with a fuller bench under its own Rules.

#### Sample examination question

> A past paper asks: *"Distinguish between Article 2(5) and Article 2(6) of the Constitution of Kenya 2010 in relation to the application of international law in Kenya."*

Working the answer:

- **Article 2(5)** incorporates **general rules of international law** (customary international law) directly into Kenyan law without further legislation. No parliamentary act is needed; the rule binds automatically.
- **Article 2(6)** covers **treaties and conventions**. They bind Kenya internationally once ratified, but they only become enforceable in Kenyan domestic courts once Parliament passes an Act domesticating them — the dualist position Kenya retains.
- The trap answer is to call both "binding" or to say treaties apply automatically — half the candidates fail this MCQ each year.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Treaties ratified but not domesticated cannot be enforced by a litigant in a Kenyan court — section 2(6) is explicit. Candidates who write "Kenya follows monism" lose the mark.

#### Delegated legislation — a frequently forgotten source

Delegated legislation (also called subsidiary legislation or statutory instruments) is law made by a person or body other than Parliament acting under powers conferred by a parent Act. Examples include ministerial rules, county by-laws, and Central Bank of Kenya prudential guidelines. Its validity is tested against the parent Act's vires — a regulation outside the enabling clause is void for ultra vires.

> 📌 **Quick rule:** if the rule appears in the *Kenya Gazette* and cites "Made under section … of the … Act", it is subsidiary, not primary.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Repugnancy and the limits of customary law

Section 3(2) of the Judicature Act excludes customary law in two situations: (a) repugnancy to justice and morality, and (b) inconsistency with any written law. Courts have applied the repugnancy clause narrowly — it targets rules that shock the judicial conscience (for example, rules that denied inheritance to daughters or exposed widows to harmful rites), not every customary rule that differs from received common-law rules. The High Court retains residual discretion to refuse enforcement even where statute is silent.

#### The Indian Acts anomaly

Several Indian statutes — including the Indian Penal Code 1860, the Indian Evidence Act 1872, and parts of the Indian Contract Act 1872 — were transplanted into Kenya in the early colonial period and remain in force as Kenyan Acts (Penal Code Cap 63, Evidence Act Cap 80, etc.). They are not "English law" and the 12 August 1897 cut-off does not bind them. Treat them as ordinary Kenyan statutes, subject to amendment by Acts of Parliament and to constitutional review under Article 2(4).

#### Treaty practice and Article 2(6)

Because Kenya is dualist on treaties, a litigant cannot rely directly on an unratified convention such as the ICCPR in a domestic court unless Parliament has enacted implementing legislation. However, courts increasingly reference unratified or undomesticated treaties as aids to interpretation of the Bill of Rights — a soft-monist interpretive practice that does not convert the treaty into binding law.

#### Advanced traps and edge cases

| Trap | Correct position |
|---|---|
| "Equity overrides statute." | Statute overrides equity; equity only fills gaps. |
| "All English law applies in Kenya." | Only common law, equity, and statutes of general application in force on 12 August 1897, subject to local circumstances. |
| "African customary law is repugnant on its face." | Repugnancy is a judicial finding against specific rules, not a presumption. |
| "The Court of Appeal is bound by its own decisions." | Only the Supreme Court binds itself; the Court of Appeal follows its own earlier decisions unless overruled by the Supreme Court or a fuller bench. |
| "Customary international law requires an Act of Parliament." | Only treaties (Article 2(6)) do; general rules of international law bind automatically under Article 2(5). |
| "Delegated legislation is made by Parliament." | It is made by ministers, agencies, or county assemblies under a parent Act — Parliament makes the enabling Act. |

#### Two practice prompts for deeper study

1. Trace the chain by which the rule in *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932) entered Kenyan tort law: English common law → Judicature Act s.3(1)(c) → subject to local circumstances. Identify at least two Kenyan appellate decisions that applied it.
2. Examine the recent jurisprudence on Article 2(5) — note the cases where the High Court treated general rules of international law as automatically part of Kenyan law without needing an enabling Act. Consider how this interacts with Article 2(6).
