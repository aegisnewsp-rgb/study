# Order of Course Choices

Your course list is the single highest-leverage document in a CAO application. It is also the one most candidates fill in in a single sitting, in the wrong order, and never revisit. The CAO allocates one offer per round — your highest-preference course that you are entitled to — so the order is not a preference register. It is an instruction.

## The rule that makes order matter

In each allocation round, the CAO looks at the candidate's course list from the top and offers the **first** course on the list for which the candidate satisfies the entry requirements and has sufficient points. Everything below that line is not considered in that round.

Three things follow, and each one is commonly misunderstood.

**Being higher on the list is not a penalty.** A candidate who lists a course first and misses it is not worse off than one who never listed it. The higher preference is attempted first, and failing costs nothing — the candidate simply moves down their own list.

**A course you did not list will never be offered.** There is no wildcard. If a course is not on your list at the moment the round is processed, it cannot be allocated to you, whatever your points.

**Rearranging mid-cycle changes what you are offered, not what you are entitled to.** The CAO permits changes to the course list between rounds. A reorder is a genuine strategic move and is treated as one.

## "Genuine order of preference"

The CAO asks candidates to list courses in genuine order of preference, and the Handbook attaches real consequences to how that instruction is met.

Where a course is restricted (see cao-010), the ordering rules bind more tightly — a restricted-entry course generally cannot be placed below another course at the same institution in the way an unrestricted course can. Ignoring a restriction in your list can invalidate the placement of the course beneath it.

Beyond the formal rules, treat the instruction as a constraint on your own thinking. A list ordered by prestige rather than by what you would genuinely study is a list you will be tempted to reorder under pressure, mid-cycle, when the decision should be calm and pre-committed.

## How to build the list

**Start from the course, not from the ranking.** For each course on your shortlist, write down the course code, the entry requirements, the restricted status, and the points history. If you cannot fill in those four things, the course is not on your list yet — it is on a wish list.

**Work down until the list is genuinely long.** A list should extend below the range where you expect to be offered, because the allocation rounds exist precisely to handle the case where your first preference does not open up. A list that stops at your target course leaves you with nothing in Round 2 if it does not.

**Include a mix of ambition and certainty.** The error is not reaching for a high course; the error is a list that is *only* high courses, or *only* safe ones. A list with an ambitious top, a realistic middle and a genuinely acceptable floor behaves properly through the rounds.

**Put the restricted courses where the rules require.** Check each restricted course's Handbook entry for how it may be ordered relative to other courses at the same institution, and place it accordingly.

## What the rounds actually do to your list

Round 1 allocates one offer. It is drawn from your list, so Round 1 is a single-shot statement of where you sit. Most candidates are surprised by how much Round 1 reveals and how little they prepared.

After Round 1 results, you can change your list for Round 2. At that stage you can also indicate whether you would accept an offer below your Round 1 preference. This is the mechanism that produces the counter-intuitive advice some candidates get: declining a Round 1 offer is sometimes rational when a better offer remains likely, and accepting one can end the process. That trade is decided on expected values, not on nerves — see cao-009.

> Confirm the current list limit, the change-of-list deadlines and the restricted-entry ordering rules in the CAO Handbook on cao.ie before you finalise. These are set per admission cycle.

## Self-check before you close the page

1. You listed a course in preference position 1 and missed it. What does that cost you? *(Nothing in that round. The CAO moves down your own list and offers the next course you are entitled to. Ranking higher carries no penalty for missing.)*
2. A course you could easily get into is not on your list. Will CAO offer it? *(No. Only courses on the list at the time of the round are considered; there is no discretionary offer outside the list.)*
3. Why does a restricted-entry course carry extra ordering constraints? *(Because the Handbook's restricted-entry rules limit where it may sit relative to other courses at the same institution, and a badly placed restricted course can compromise the entries around it.)*
4. You have Round 1 and Round 2 to plan. When should your list be final? *(Round 1 order should be decided and committed in advance. Round 2 exists for adjustment using information Round 1 gives you — and the decision to accept or decline at that point should already have been thought through, not improvised.)*
5. Your list contains only courses whose entry requirements you have not actually checked. What is the risk? *(A course can be ineligible regardless of points — subject requirements, restricted status, minimums. You would spend a whole round on a course you could never have been offered, and lose the chance to list a real alternative beneath it.)*
