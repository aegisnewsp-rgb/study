# Theory of Production

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Theory of Production** studies how firms convert inputs (labour L, capital K) into output and decide on the most efficient input mix. The production function Q = f(L, K) links total output to factor quantities.

- **Total Product (TP):** total output from all units of the variable input.
- **Average Product (AP):** AP = TP ÷ L.
- **Marginal Product (MP):** MP = ΔTP ÷ ΔL (extra output from one more unit of L).

**Three stages** govern the short run: Stage I (rising AP, rising MP), Stage II (MP falling but positive, AP at its maximum — the rational stage), and Stage III (MP negative, TP falls). Only Stage II is economically efficient.

**Exam pointer:** MP cuts AP at AP's **maximum**. For a Cobb-Douglas function Q = A·L^α·K^β, constant returns to scale require **α + β = 1**.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Production Function Basics

The short-run production function holds at least one input (commonly capital) fixed, while the long-run function allows every input to vary. Total Product (TP) rises as the variable factor increases, but **Marginal Product (MP)** is the decisive variable: it first rises (increasing returns), then falls (diminishing returns), and finally turns negative.

The **Law of Variable Proportions** states that as successive units of a variable factor are combined with a fixed factor, MP eventually declines. This is a **short-run** phenomenon driven by the fixed-input bottleneck.

#### Relationship Between TP, AP, and MP

| Measure | Formula | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| TP | Sum of output from all units | Rises, peaks, then falls |
| AP | TP ÷ L | Rises, reaches max, declines |
| MP | ΔTP ÷ ΔL | Rises, then falls, turns negative |

When MP > AP, AP is rising; when MP < AP, AP is falling. **MP equals AP at AP's maximum**, after which AP declines even though MP remains positive for some range.

#### Returns to Scale (Long Run)

In the long run, all factors vary together. Doubling every input can produce more than, exactly, or less than double the output — giving Increasing, Constant, or Decreasing Returns to Scale.

For the Cobb-Douglas function Q = A·L^α·K^β, scaling both inputs by λ multiplies output by λ^(α+β). Hence **α + β = 1 → CRS**, **α + β > 1 → IRS**, **α + β < 1 → DRS**.

#### Isoquants, Isocost, and Producer's Equilibrium

An **isoquant** shows all (L, K) combinations yielding the same output; its slope is **−MRTS = −MP_L ÷ MP_K**. The **isocost** line C = wL + rK shows affordable combinations with slope **−w/r**.

Producer's equilibrium requires the isoquant to be **tangent** to the isocost: **MRTS = w/r**, achieved at the lowest cost for a given output.

#### CMA Foundation Exam Pattern

In Paper 2, expect 1–2 MCQs or a short numerical (2–3 marks). Typical questions ask you to identify the production stage from a schedule, compute MP/AP, or interpret Cobb-Douglas elasticities.

> **Trap:** Stage III begins only when **MP < 0**, not when MP = 0.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Short Run vs Long Run — A Common Confusion

Students frequently blur the **Law of Variable Proportions** (short run, one factor fixed, diminishing MP of the variable factor) with **Returns to Scale** (long run, all factors scale together). The former is about *how extra doses of one input perform against a fixed input*; the latter is about *what happens when the entire production recipe is scaled up or down*.

#### Economies and Diseconomies of Scale

Long-run cost curves trace out enveloping short-run curves. **IRS** typically arises from technical indivisibilities (e.g., a single large machine doing the work of many small ones), managerial specialisation, bulk purchase discounts, and marketing spread. **DRS** emerges from coordination failures, communication overhead, and bureaucratic inertia as the firm grows beyond optimal size. **Internal economies** are firm-specific; **external economies** come from industry-wide factors such as skilled labour pools and supplier networks.

#### Worked Numeric — Cobb-Douglas Returns

Suppose Q = 4·L^0.6·K^0.4. Here A = 4, α = 0.6, β = 0.4, so α + β = 1.00 → **constant returns to scale**: doubling both inputs doubles output. If instead Q = 4·L^0.7·K^0.5, then α + β = 1.2 → **IRS**, and output rises by 2^1.2 ≈ 2.297×, more than double.

#### Two Practice Prompts

1. Given the schedule L = 1,2,3,4,5 producing TP = 20, 50, 75, 90, 95, compute MP and AP for each level. Identify the unit where AP is maximised and confirm MP = AP there.
2. For Q = 2L^0.4·K^0.6, state the type of returns to scale and the output elasticity of capital. If w = ₹40, r = ₹20, write the isocost equation and the producer-equilibrium condition.

> **Mnemonic for stage boundaries:** "Stage II = MP positive but falling" — that's where every profit-maximising firm chooses to operate.
