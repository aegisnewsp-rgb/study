# Introduction to Law and the Tanzanian Legal System

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> Core concepts, sources of law, court hierarchy, and reception dates for rapid Tanzania Law School admission revision.

Law is a formal system of rules enacted or recognized by a sovereign authority to govern human conduct, maintain public order, resolve disputes, and allocate rights and duties within society.

**Foundational Tanzanian Legal Anchors:**
1. **The Reception Date:**
   - English common law, doctrines of equity, and statutes of general application were formally received in Tanganyika on **22nd July 1920** under the Tanganyika Order in Council, 1920.
   - Preserved under Section 2(3) of the **Judicature and Application of Laws Act (JALA), Cap 358 [R.E. 2019]**.
   - These received laws apply only so far as the circumstances of Tanzania and its inhabitants permit, and subject to qualifications for local conditions.

2. **The Grundnorm / Supreme Law:**
   - **Constitution of the United Republic of Tanzania, 1977 (Cap 2):** Any law inconsistent with the Constitution is void to the extent of the inconsistency (Article 64(5)).
   - Enshrines the Bill of Rights in Part III of Chapter One (Articles 12 to 29), introduced via Act No. 15 of 1984.

3. **Hierarchy of Mainland Tanzania Courts:**
   - **Court of Appeal of Tanzania:** Highest appellate court for civil and criminal appeals from the High Court (Article 117 of Constitution; Appellate Jurisdiction Act, Cap 141).
   - **High Court of Tanzania:** Original jurisdiction in unlimited civil and criminal matters, constitutional interpretation, and specialized divisions: Commercial Division, Land Division, Labour Division, and the High Court Corruption and Economic Crimes Division.
   - **Resident Magistrate's Courts & District Courts:** Subordinate courts established under Magistrates' Courts Act, Cap 11.
   - **Primary Courts:** Base court tier at ward level; presided over by Primary Court Magistrates sitting with two lay assessors. Advocates cannot represent parties in Primary Courts (Section 33, MCA).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

> Classification of legal branches, sources of law under Cap 358, judicial precedent, and customary law integration.

#### Classification of Law in Mainland Tanzania
Legal rules are divided into distinct categories based on subject matter, procedural rules, and enforcement mechanisms:
1. **Public Law vs. Private Law:**
   - *Public Law:* Regulates relationships between the state and private citizens, or between organs of state. Includes Constitutional Law, Administrative Law, Criminal Law, and Revenue Law.
   - *Private (Civil) Law:* Regulates relationships between individual legal persons (natural and corporate). Includes the Law of Contract (Law of Contract Act, Cap 345), Law of Torts, Land Law (Land Act, Cap 113), Family Law (Law of Marriage Act, Cap 29), and Company Law (Companies Act, Cap 212).
2. **Substantive Law vs. Procedural Law:**
   - *Substantive Law:* Defines legal rights, duties, liabilities, and offences (e.g., Penal Code, Cap 16; Law of Contract Act, Cap 345).
   - *Procedural (Adjective) Law:* Prescribes the machinery, rules, and steps by which substantive rights are enforced in court (e.g., Civil Procedure Code, Cap 33; Criminal Procedure Act, Cap 20; Law of Limitation Act, Cap 89).

### Table 1: Civil Law vs. Criminal Law in Tanzanian Jurisprudence

| Dimension of Distinction | Civil Proceedings in Mainland Tanzania | Criminal Proceedings in Mainland Tanzania |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Origin and Objective** | Resolution of private wrongs; compensation, restitution, or specific performance | Punishment of public offences against the Republic; deterrence, reformation, retribution |
| **Initiator / Parties** | Aggrieved private citizen or corporation (Plaintiff vs. Defendant) | The Republic through Director of Public Prosecutions (DPP vs. Accused) |
| **Standard of Proof** | **Preponderance of probabilities** / Balance of probabilities | **Beyond reasonable doubt** (*Woolmington v DPP* standard) |
| **Burden of Proof** | Rests on plaintiff asserting affirmative claim (Section 110, Evidence Act, Cap 6) | Rests throughout on prosecution; accused enjoys presumption of innocence (Art. 13(6)(b)) |
| **Pleadings & Governing Codes** | Plaint, Written Statement of Defence (Civil Procedure Code, Cap 33) | Charge Sheet or Indictment (Criminal Procedure Act, Cap 20) |
| **Terminal Outcome** | Judgment awarding damages, injunctions, declarations, or dismissal | Conviction resulting in imprisonment, fine, community service, or acquittal |
| **Settlement by Parties** | Parties may compromise, settle out of court, or withdraw freely | Composition restricted; withdrawal requires DPP consent under Section 91 CPA (*nolle prosequi*) |

#### Sources of Law Under the Judicature and Application of Laws Act (Cap 358)
Section 2 of JALA outlines the primary and secondary sources of law applied by courts in Mainland Tanzania:
1. **The Constitution:** The supreme legal standard.
2. **Statutes / Acts of Parliament:** Primary legislation enacted by the Parliament of Tanzania and assented to by the President (Article 97).
3. **Subsidiary / Delegated Legislation:** Regulations, orders, rules, by-laws, and notices issued by Ministers, local government authorities, or statutory boards under delegated parliamentary powers. Published in the official Gazette (*Gazeti la Serikali*) as Government Notices (GN).
4. **Received English Law (Reception Date: 22 July 1920):**
   - *Common Law:* Judge-made law developed by English courts.
   - *Doctrines of Equity:* Principles developed by the English Court of Chancery to soften common law rigidities (injunctions, specific performance, trusts).
   - *Statutes of General Application:* English parliamentary acts in force on 22 July 1920, applied only if local legislation is silent.
5. **Customary Law:**
   - Recognized under Section 11 of JALA.
   - Governs civil matters between members of a traditional community regarding customary land tenure, bridewealth, marriage, and intestate succession (Customary Law Declaration Order, 1963, GN 279/1963 and GN 436/1963).
   - *Limitation:* Customary law is inapplicable if it conflicts with human rights under the Constitution or statutory enactments.
6. **Islamic Law:**
   - Applies in personal matters of marriage, divorce, maintenance, and inheritance where the parties are Muslims and intended Islamic law to govern their relationship (Section 9, Judicature and Application of Laws Act; Law of Marriage Act, Cap 29).
7. **Judicial Precedents (*Stare Decisis*):**
   - Decisions of the Court of Appeal bind all subordinate courts in Tanzania. Decisions of the High Court bind Resident Magistrate's, District, and Primary courts.

> Jurisprudential theories of law, constitutionalism, human rights enforcement, and statutory interpretation rules.

#### Jurisprudential Perspectives on the Nature of Law
Tanzanian legal education tests candidates on foundational legal philosophies:
- **Positivist School (John Austin, H.L.A. Hart):**
  - Austin defined law as *"the command of a sovereign, backed by the threat of sanctions, and habitually obeyed."*
  - H.L.A. Hart (*The Concept of Law*) rejected Austin's command model, defining law as the union of **primary rules** of obligation (criminal and civil duties) and **secondary rules** of recognition, change, and adjudication.
- **Natural Law School (St. Thomas Aquinas, Lon Fuller, John Finnis):**
  - Holds that law derives its validity from moral principles rooted in nature, reason, or divine order. An unjust law lacking moral foundation is not law at all (*lex injusta non est lex*).
- **Historical School (Friedrich Carl von Savigny):**
  - Posited that law is not made by sovereign decrees but develops organically from the national character and collective consciousness of the people (*Volksgeist*). Highly influential in analyzing the persistence of Tanzanian customary law alongside statutory codes.
- **Pure Theory of Law (Hans Kelsen):**
  - Conceived law as a hierarchy of norms (*Stufenbau*) deriving validity from a single foundational master norm, the **Grundnorm**. In Tanzania, the 1977 Constitution represents the Grundnorm.

| Norm Tier | Legal Category / Instrument | Enacting Body / Source | Validity Standard / Override Rule |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |

| **Tier 5 (Customary / Religious)**| Customary Law & Islamic Personal Law | Traditional practices / Quranic jurisprudence | Applies only in personal status; must not offend written law or constitutional rights |

## Worked Tanzania Law School Exam Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Clash Between Customary Law and the Constitution

**Scenario:** Juma, a resident of Mwanza, passes away intestate leaving behind agricultural land and a residential house registered in his name. Juma was an adherent of Sukuma customary law. Relying on Rule 20 of the Customary Law Declaration Order, 1963 (GN 279/1963), the clan council appoints Juma's eldest brother as the sole administrator and heir, excluding Juma's two adult daughters, Asha and Rehema, on the grounds that under Sukuma patrilineal customs, female children cannot inherit ancestral real property.
Asha and Rehema wish to challenge this customary disinheritance in the High Court of Tanzania.

**Questions:**
1. State the legal basis upon which Asha and Rehema can challenge Rule 20 of GN 279/1963.
2. Cite the constitutional provisions and leading Tanzanian precedents that support their petition.
3. What order is the High Court empowered to grant?

**Detailed Legal Solution:**

1. **Legal Basis of Challenge:**
   - While Section 11 of the Judicature and Application of Laws Act (JALA), Cap 358 recognizes customary law, it explicitly subjects customary rules to written laws and natural justice.
   - More fundamentally, Article 64(5) of the 1977 Constitution of the United Republic of Tanzania establishes that any law (including subsidiary legislation like GN 279/1963 and customary rules) that conflicts with the Constitution is null and void to the extent of the inconsistency.

2. **Constitutional Provisions & Case Law:**
   - **Article 12(1) & (2):** All human beings are born free and are equal before the law, entitled to recognition and respect for their dignity.
   - **Article 13(1) & (2):** All persons are equal before the law without discrimination based on gender, sex, origin, or status.
   - **Article 24(1):** Every person is entitled to own property and to have protection of that property in accordance with law.
   - **Precedents:**
     - In ***Bernado Ephrahim v. Holaria Pastory & Gervazi Kaizilege* (1990) TLR 106 (HC)**, Mwalusanya J. held that the Haya customary law barring women from selling clan land was discriminatory against women and unconstitutional, violating Article 13(4) of the Constitution and international conventions ratified by Tanzania (CEDAW).
     - In ***Attorney General v. Ndunguru & Another*** and related jurisprudence, the High Court reaffirmed that customary rules discriminating against female offspring regarding inheritance violate the equality guarantees of the Constitution.

3. **Appropriate Court Orders:**
   - Under the Basic Rights and Duties Enforcement Act (Cap 3), the High Court has jurisdiction to declare Rule 20 of GN 279/1963 unconstitutional and void to the extent that it discriminates against female heirs on the basis of sex.
   - The court will issue an order directing that the estate be distributed equally among all surviving children irrespective of gender.

### Scenario 2: Jurisdiction and Legal Representation in Primary Courts

**Scenario:** Baraka files a civil suit in the Primary Court of Kariakoo against Omari, claiming breach of a building contract valued at TZS 45,000,000. Baraka instructs Mr. Mwamba, an Advocate of the High Court of Tanzania, to appear on his behalf in the Primary Court. The Primary Court Magistrate rejects Mr. Mwamba's appearance and orders the trial to proceed with Baraka appearing in person.

**Questions:**
1. Did the Primary Court Magistrate act lawfully in barring Advocate Mwamba from appearing?
2. Does the Primary Court have subject-matter and pecuniary jurisdiction to try this contractual claim?

**Detailed Legal Solution:**

1. **Legality of Barring the Advocate:**
   - Yes, the Primary Court Magistrate acted strictly in accordance with the law.
   - Under **Section 33(1) of the Magistrates' Courts Act, Cap 11 [R.E. 2019]**, advocates and legal practitioners are barred from appearing or representing any party in proceedings before a Primary Court.
   - Representation in Primary Courts is restricted to the parties themselves, or relatives/members of the household authorized in writing. This rule was designed to maintain simple, inexpensive, and accessible proceedings at the community level.

2. **Pecuniary Jurisdiction Evaluation:**
   - Under Section 18 of the Magistrates' Courts Act as amended by the Written Laws (Miscellaneous Amendments) Acts, the pecuniary jurisdiction of Primary Courts for recovery of civil debts arising out of contract is capped at **TZS 50,000,000** for claims governed by statutory law.
   - Since Baraka's claim is TZS 45,000,000, it falls within the pecuniary ceiling of TZS 50,000,000. Therefore, the Primary Court possesses competent pecuniary jurisdiction to adjudicate the dispute.

## Critical Tanzania Law School Traps & Heuristic Checkpoints

- **Trap 1: The Reception Date Year**: Never cite 1961 or 1964 as the reception date of English law. The reception date for Mainland Tanzania (Tanganyika) is **22nd July 1920** (Tanganyika Order in Council, 1920). For Zanzibar, received English law was introduced via the Zanzibar Order in Council, 1897 (reception date: **7th July 1897**).
- **Trap 2: Advocates in Primary Courts**: Do not assume advocates have an unrestricted constitutional right to appear in every court. Section 33 of the Magistrates' Courts Act explicitly excludes advocates from Primary Courts, and the Court of Appeal has upheld the validity of this exclusion.
- **Trap 3: Customary Law Codification**: Remember that customary law in Tanzania is partly codified through the Customary Law Declaration Orders of 1963 (GN 279 and GN 436). However, this codification applies primarily to patrilineal Bantu communities, not to matrilineal societies or pastoralist groups like the Maasai unless formally extended.
- **Trap 4: Dualist Treaty System**: Tanzania follows a dualist approach to international law. International treaties (such as the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights or the ICCPR) ratified by Tanzania do not automatically become enforceable in domestic courts unless Parliament passes specific enabling legislation domesticating the treaty.
