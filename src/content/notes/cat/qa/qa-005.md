# Ratio

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **ratio** compares two same-kind quantities as a:b, where **a** is the *antecedent* and **b** the *consequent*. Both terms must carry the same unit; the ratio itself is dimensionless. The core operational fact: when a sum **S** is split between two partners in ratio **m:n**, each share equals **S · m/(m+n)**.

#### Must-know operations:

- **Proportion:** a:b = c:d ⇔ ad = bc.
- **Compounded ratio** of a:b and c:d is **ac:bd**.
- **Duplicate ratio** = a²:b²; **Triplicate ratio** = a³:b³.
- **Mean proportional** x between a and b: x = √(ab), with a:x = x:b.
- **Alligation rule:** (Cheaper qty)/(Dearer qty) = (DCP − MP)/(MP − CCP).

**Exam pointers for CAT QA (4% weight):** Expect 1–2 application-based MCQs, usually wrapped inside partnership or mixture contexts. Convert every ratio to lowest integers before substituting into formulas. Watch for *time-weighted capital* in profit-sharing questions.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Definition and Notation

A ratio expresses "how many times" one quantity contains another, written **a:b** or equivalently **a/b**. The first term (a) is the antecedent, the second (b) is the consequent. Ratios are dimensionless — they describe a relationship, not an absolute amount. So 6 litres : 9 litres simplifies to 2:3, but 6 kg : 9 litres is **not a valid ratio** because the units differ.

#### Proportion and Cross-Multiplication

Four quantities a, b, c, d are in **proportion** when a:b :: c:d, meaning a/b = c/d. Cross-multiplying gives **ad = bc**, the test you should use on the calculator-free CAT paper. The fourth proportional to a, b, c is x = bc/a.

#### Dividing a Quantity in a Ratio

If a sum **S** is divided in ratio m:n, the first share is **mS/(m+n)** and the second is **nS/(m+n)**. Example: ₹500 split 2:3 → ₹200 and ₹300. For three-way splits, the share of the term carrying coefficient kᵢ is kᵢS/(k₁ + k₂ + k₃).

#### Compounded, Duplicate, Triplicate Ratios

The compounded ratio of a:b and c:d is **ac:bd**. Duplicate and triplicate ratios raise each term to the 2nd or 3rd power. Crucially, compounding two successive ratios is **not** the same as adding them — 2:3 compounded with 3:4 yields 6:12 = 1:2, *not* 5:7.

| Operation | Formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Compound (a:b, c:d) | ac : bd | 2:3 and 3:4 → 6:12 = 1:2 |
| Duplicate of a:b | a² : b² | 2:3 → 4:9 |
| Triplicate of a:b | a³ : b³ | 2:3 → 8:27 |
| Mean proportional | √(ab) | Between 4 and 9 → 6 |

#### Inverse Ratio and Alligation

The **inverse ratio** of a:b is b:a. In **alligation**, when two ingredients of cost DCP and CCP are mixed to obtain mean price MP, the quantity ratio (cheaper : dearer) is **(DCP − MP) : (MP − CCP)**. This is the workhorse formula for mixture questions.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Continued Proportion

Three quantities a, x, b are in **continued proportion** when a:x = x:b. Here x is the mean proportional, equal to √(ab). A useful check: in continued proportion the middle term squared equals the product of the extremes — a quick sanity test before committing an answer.

#### Partnership Profit-Sharing

Profit splits by **time-weighted capital**, not raw capital. If A invests ₹x for n months and B invests ₹y for m months, the profit ratio is **xn : ym**. Forgetting the time multiplier is the single most common CAT trap in partnership problems.

#### Successive Ratio Changes

When a quantity's ratio changes stepwise (say 2:3 then 3:4), multiply the corresponding terms to get the compounded ratio: 2:3 and 3:4 give 2:4 = 1:2. This is also how successive percentage changes are modeled — a 20% rise then 25% rise equals 1.20 × 1.25 = 1.50, a 50% net increase.

#### Common Mistakes

- Comparing quantities with **different units** without normalization (always reduce to the same unit first).
- Adding ratios directly instead of compounding them — leads to answers off by a large margin.
- Applying alligation when the given ratio is of **cost**, not **quantity**.
- Computing mean proportional on non-integer ratios without first simplifying to lowest terms.

#### Worked Micro-Example

Two alloys cost ₹200/kg and ₹500/kg. Mixed in ratio 3:2, what is the mean price?
Applying alligation: (500 − MP)/(MP − 200) = 3/2 → 2(500 − MP) = 3(MP − 200) → 1000 − 2MP = 3MP − 600 → **MP = ₹320/kg**.

#### Practice Prompts

1. A, B, C invest ₹40,000, ₹60,000, ₹80,000 for 6, 4, 3 months respectively. Find the profit-sharing ratio.
2. If x:y = 3:4 and y:z = 5:7, compute x:z and the mean proportional between x and z.
