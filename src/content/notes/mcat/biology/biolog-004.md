# Bioenergetics — MCAT Pakistan

Bioenergetics is the study of how cells capture, store, transfer, and use energy: photosynthesis in chloroplasts, cellular respiration in mitochondria, ATP synthesis by chemiosmosis, and the limiting factors that tune each pathway to the environment. Under the PMDC 2026 Biology allotment of 81 MCQs (≈45 % of the 180-MCQ MDCAT paper, PMDC Public Notice, 2026), Bioenergetics yields roughly 7–9 MCQs per paper — a high-yield unit because it is mechanistically rich and numerically testable (stoichiometry, yields, redox). PMDC confirms the 2026 syllabus is identical to the 2025 Uniform Curriculum (PMDC MDCAT Curriculum 2025), so the learning outcomes and chapter list below are unchanged for this cycle.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

**What to lock in within the first hour.**
- **ATP is the universal currency.** Hydrolysis of its terminal phosphoanhydride bond releases ≈30.5 kJ/mol under cellular conditions; ATP → ADP + Pi powers active transport, biosynthesis, and mechanical work.
- **Photosynthesis overview.** 6 CO₂ + 12 H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ + 6 H₂O. Light reactions (thylakoid) split water and make ATP + NADPH; Calvin cycle (stroma) fixes CO₂ into sugar using that ATP + NADPH.
- **Cellular respiration overview.** C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + ≈30–32 ATP. Glycolysis (cytoplasm, 2 ATP net), pyruvate oxidation (mitochondrial matrix, 0 ATP), Krebs cycle (matrix, 2 ATP/GTP per glucose), oxidative phosphorylation (inner mitochondrial membrane, ≈26–28 ATP).
- **Chloroplast anatomy.** Outer membrane, inner membrane, thylakoid (stacked into grana), stroma. The light reactions happen on the thylakoid membrane; the Calvin cycle happens in the stroma.
- **Mitochondrion anatomy.** Outer membrane, inner membrane (cristae), intermembrane space, matrix. The electron transport chain sits on the inner membrane; the Krebs cycle runs in the matrix.
- **Redox shorthand.** NADPH and NADH carry electrons; NADP⁺ and NAD⁺ are their oxidised forms. NADPH powers the Calvin cycle; NADH powers the electron transport chain.
- **Limiting factors of photosynthesis.** Light intensity, CO₂ concentration, and temperature each have a saturating region; beyond saturation, the other factor becomes limiting.

**One-line recap before walking in.** Light reactions make ATP + NADPH; Calvin cycle uses them to fix CO₂. Glycolysis makes pyruvate + NADH + 2 ATP; mitochondria extract the rest.

### 🟡 Standard — Exam Prep (3d-3w)

#### Light-dependent reactions (thylakoid)
Two photosystems work in series. **Photosystem II** absorbs 680 nm light, excites P680, splits water (2 H₂O → 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻ + O₂) and feeds electrons into the chain. Plastoquinone carries them to the cytochrome b₆f complex, which pumps H⁺ into the thylakoid lumen — building the proton gradient that drives ATP synthase (chemiosmosis). **Photosystem I** absorbs 700 nm light, re-energises the electrons, and uses them to reduce NADP⁺ to NADPH via ferredoxin.

The **Z-scheme** is the energy diagram: P680 → P680* (excited) → PQ → cyt b₆f → PC → P700 → P700* → Fd → NADP⁺ reductase. MCAT questions test the order of carriers and the source of O₂ (water, not CO₂). Photophosphorylation yields ≈3 ATP per NADPH produced, balancing the Calvin cycle's demand.

#### Calvin cycle (light-independent, in the stroma)
The cycle has three phases: **carbon fixation** (CO₂ + ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate → two 3-phosphoglycerate, catalysed by RuBisCO), **reduction** (3-PGA → 1,3-BPG → G3P, using ATP and NADPH), and **regeneration** of RuBP (using more ATP). Fixing one CO₂ requires 3 ATP + 2 NADPH. Six CO₂ fixations (one glucose) require 18 ATP + 12 NADPH. C₃ plants fix CO₂ directly into 3-PGA; C₄ and CAM plants first fix into 4-carbon oxaloacetate to avoid photorespiration.

#### Photorespiration and limiting factors
**RuBisCO** has dual affinity: it can fix CO₂ (carboxylation) or O₂ (oxygenation). Oxygenation produces 2-phosphoglycolate, which the cell salvages at a high ATP cost — this is photorespiration, and it wastes fixed carbon. C₄ plants (maize, sugarcane) concentrate CO₂ around RuBisCO via bundle-sheath cells; CAM plants (cacti, succulents) open stomata only at night to fix CO₂ into malate, then decarboxylate during the day.

A **limiting-factor graph** plots photosynthesis rate against light intensity at several CO₂ levels. The initial slope is light-limited (linear), then plateaus where CO₂ becomes limiting. The CO₂-response curve plateaus where light or temperature becomes limiting. Temperature optimum sits around 25–30 °C for C₃ plants (above which Rubisco's oxygenase activity rises sharply).

#### Glycolysis (cytoplasm)
Ten steps, all enzymatically catalysed, in two phases. **Energy-investment phase** (steps 1–5): 2 ATP consumed to phosphorylate glucose and cleave it into two triose phosphates. **Energy-payoff phase** (steps 6–10): each triose produces 1 NADH + 2 ATP, so per glucose: net **2 ATP + 2 NADH**. The end product is pyruvate. Under anaerobic conditions, pyruvate is reduced to lactate (animals) or ethanol + CO₂ (yeast), regenerating NAD⁺ so glycolysis can continue.

#### Pyruvate oxidation and Krebs cycle
**Pyruvate** enters the mitochondrial matrix via a symporter with H⁺; the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex releases CO₂ and reduces NAD⁺ to NADH, producing acetyl-CoA. Per glucose: 2 NADH (no ATP yet).

The **Krebs (citric acid) cycle** runs once per acetyl-CoA. Per turn: 3 NADH + 1 FADH₂ + 1 GTP (= 1 ATP) + 2 CO₂. Per glucose (two turns): 6 NADH + 2 FADH₂ + 2 GTP + 4 CO₂. The cycle is amphibolic: intermediates feed biosynthesis (amino acids, porphyrins, lipids) as well as oxidation.

#### Oxidative phosphorylation and ATP yield
**Electron transport chain** (inner mitochondrial membrane): NADH donates electrons to complex I, passes to coenzyme Q, complex III, cytochrome c, complex IV, and finally reduces O₂ to H₂O. Complexes I, III, and IV pump H⁺ from the matrix to the intermembrane space, building the proton-motive force. **ATP synthase** (complex V) lets H⁺ flow back into the matrix; the rotation phosphorylates ADP + Pi to ATP (chemiosmosis). FADH₂ enters at complex II (after succinate dehydrogenase in the Krebs cycle), pumping fewer protons → ≈1.5 ATP vs ≈2.5 ATP per NADH.

**Total ATP per glucose** (modern textbook figures): ≈30–32 ATP. Older sources state 36–38 because they count the 2 NADH from glycolysis as 3 ATP each (mitochondrial shuttle). For PMDC, the consensus range ≈30–32 is safe; do not write a single-number total without acknowledging the shuttle caveat.

**Worked example.** A toxin blocks complex IV of the electron transport chain. Predict the consequence. — **Electrons stop; NADH accumulates; the proton gradient cannot be maintained; ATP synthase stalls; the cell switches to anaerobic glycolysis and lactate builds up.** Cyanide, azide, and CO all act here. The same outcome follows from uncouplers (2,4-dinitrophenol): they collapse the gradient without stopping electron flow.

#### Anaerobic respiration and fermentation
Without O₂, the ETC stalls, NADH accumulates, and there is no acceptor for pyruvate's electrons. **Lactic acid fermentation** (muscle cells under heavy exercise, *Lactobacillus*) reduces pyruvate to lactate using NADH, regenerating NAD⁺. **Alcoholic fermentation** (yeast, some plant cells) decarboxylates pyruvate to acetaldehyde (releasing CO₂) and reduces acetaldehyde to ethanol. Both yield only the 2 ATP from glycolysis.

#### Connections between photosynthesis and respiration
Photosynthesis and respiration are chemical mirror images. The O₂ released by photosynthesis comes from water (proved by ¹⁸O labelling experiments). The CO₂ released by respiration comes from the oxidation of glucose carbons. ATP, NAD(P)H, and the proton gradient are the shared energy carriers across both. A question linking "where does the O in glucose ultimately come from?" should be answered "from CO₂ in the Calvin cycle", not "from water".

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (1mo+)

#### Energetics in detail
The free energy change for ATP hydrolysis in cells (ΔG ≈ −50 to −60 kJ/mol, with about −30.5 kJ "useful" under standard cellular conditions) is large because the products (ADP and Pi) are resonance-stabilised and Pi is ionised at physiological pH. Photosynthesis stores ≈1,600–1,800 kJ per mole of glucose; respiration releases roughly the same amount. The thermodynamic efficiency of oxidative phosphorylation is ≈40 % (the rest is lost as heat).

#### Regulation and metabolic control
- **Glycolysis.** PFK-1 is the rate-limiting enzyme, allosterically inhibited by ATP and citrate (energy charge is high) and activated by AMP and fructose-2,6-bisphosphate (energy charge is low).
- **Krebs cycle.** Isocitrate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase are activated by Ca²⁺ and ADP, inhibited by ATP and NADH — matching the cycle's rate to the cell's energy demand.
- **Electron transport chain.** Respiration is feedback-controlled by the proton gradient: a high gradient slows electron flow; ATP demand collapses the gradient and accelerates it.
- **Photosynthesis.** Light-harvesting antennae adjust to high light (non-photochemical quenching) by dissipating excess energy as heat via the xanthophyll cycle, preventing photo-oxidative damage.

#### Modern extensions
- **Cyclic electron flow** in PSI alone generates extra ATP without NADPH, balancing the Calvin cycle's ATP:NADPH demand when needed.
- **Photophosphorylation vs oxidative phosphorylation** share the chemiosmotic principle — Peter Mitchell's 1961 hypothesis — but use different electron donors (water vs NADH) and different terminal acceptors (NADP⁺ vs O₂).
- **Mitochondrial disease** often presents with neurological and muscular symptoms (high-energy-demand tissues), e.g., MELAS, LHON. Knowing that the mitochondria carry their own DNA (mtDNA, maternally inherited) explains the inheritance pattern in some MCQ stems.

#### Connections to other units
Pair Bioenergetics with **Enzymes** (all pathway steps are enzyme-catalysed; allosteric regulation is enzyme-level), with **Cell Structure** (chloroplasts and mitochondria are double-membraned organelles with their own DNA), and with **Coordination and Control** (thyroxine upregulates basal metabolic rate; adrenaline triggers glycogenolysis in liver and muscle).

## Common traps

1. **Saying O₂ comes from CO₂ in photosynthesis.** It does not. The O₂ released by the light reactions comes from the splitting of water, proved by Ruben and Kamen's 1941 isotope-labelling experiments. A diagram showing O₂ leaving the chloroplast labelled "from CO₂" is wrong.
2. **Counting 36–38 ATP per glucose as fact.** Modern measurements give ≈30–32 ATP because the 2 cytosolic NADH from glycolysis must be shuttled into the mitochondria (malate-aspartate or glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle), each with a different cost. State the range.
3. **Calling the Calvin cycle "dark reactions" or implying it needs darkness.** It runs in light or dark provided ATP + NADPH are present. The textbook term is *light-independent* or *carbon-fixation* reactions.
4. **Misreading the limiting-factor graph.** When light intensity is very low, raising CO₂ does nothing — light is the limiting factor. When light is saturating, CO₂ becomes limiting. A question asking "what limits photosynthesis at low light?" has the same answer regardless of CO₂ level.
5. **Conflating photophosphorylation with substrate-level phosphorylation.** Photophosphorylation makes ATP using a proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane; substrate-level phosphorylation makes ATP directly by transferring a phosphate from a high-energy intermediate to ADP (steps 7 and 10 of glycolysis; succinyl-CoA synthetase in the Krebs cycle). They produce the same product by different routes.
