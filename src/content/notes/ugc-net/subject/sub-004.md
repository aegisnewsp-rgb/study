# Contemporary Issues

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your UGC NET Paper-1 attempt.

**Contemporary Issues** in UGC NET is an interdisciplinary Paper-1 unit that tests current socio-political, economic, environmental, and technological concerns through the lens of the Indian constitutional, legal, and developmental framework. It carries roughly 4% weightage in Paper-1 (Teaching & Research Aptitude, 50 MCQs) and surfaces as cross-cutting content in Paper-2 of Political Science, Sociology, Public Administration, Education, and Social Work. High-recurrence themes: Sustainable Development, Gender Justice, Communalism vs. Secularism, Right to Information, Environmental Ethics, and Digital Divide.

- **Brundtland definition (1987):** Sustainable development = meeting present needs without compromising future generations' ability to meet theirs.
- **42nd Amendment, 1976:** inserted *socialist*, *secular*, and *integrity* into the Preamble.
- **RTI Act:** 2005 (enforced 12 Oct 2005); **RTE Act:** 2009 enforced April 2010 (86th Amendment → Article 21A).
- **IT Act 66A:** struck down in *Shreya Singhal v. UoI* (2015) as violative of Article 19(1)(a).
- **Right to Privacy:** declared a Fundamental Right under Article 21 by a 9-judge bench in *K.S. Puttaswamy v. UoI* (2017).

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"S-E-R-C-I-G-D"** — Sustainability, Education/RTE, RTI, Constitutionalism (secularism + 42nd Amend.), IT/Privacy, Gender (POSH), Demographic dividend. These seven buckets cover ~70% of Paper-1 hits.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to a couple of months before the exam.

#### Core Conceptual Map

Contemporary Issues is not a single discipline — examiners expect you to apply **multiple analytical lenses** (political theory, sociology, environmental ethics, gender studies, public policy) to the same issue. A question on climate change, for instance, may demand knowledge of India's NDC targets (45% emissions intensity reduction by 2030 vs. 2005 baseline; 50% non-fossil installed capacity) alongside the *MC Mehta* jurisprudence on the Polluter Pays Principle and Article 48A (DPSP).

#### Concept Comparison Matrix — Easily Confused Terms

| Concept | Core Meaning | Frequent Trap | Exam-Tested Distinction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sex vs. Gender | Biological vs. socially constructed | Treating them as synonyms | UGC NET explicitly tests the sex/gender binary in gender-justice MCQs |
| Indian Secularism vs. Western Secularism | Equal respect for all religions (positive) | Separation of church and state (negative) | 42nd Amendment codified Indian variant; no doctrine of state non-intervention |
| Liberalisation vs. Globalisation vs. Privatisation | De-licensing industry; WTO integration; disinvestment of PSUs | Used interchangeably | New Industrial Policy 1991 has all three but they are distinct processes |
| RTI vs. RTE | Right to Information (2005) vs. Right to Education (2009) | Year swap distractors | RTI = 2005; RTE = 2009 enforced April 2010 via Article 21A |
| Article 21 vs. 21A | Right to Life (existing) vs. Right to Education (6–14 yrs) | Assuming higher education is a Fundamental Right | Higher education is NOT a Fundamental Right; only 6–14 age group is guaranteed |
| Anthropocentric vs. Ecocentric vs. Deep Ecology | Human-centred / Nature-centred / Intrinsic value of all life | Conflating ecocentrism with deep ecology | Deep ecology (Naess) rejects instrumental valuation entirely |
| Liberal vs. Radical Feminism | Equality within patriarchy vs. Restructure patriarchy itself | Both demand "equality" | Radical feminism targets the system; liberal feminism targets legal reform |
| Fundamental Rights vs. DPSP | Justiciable vs. Non-justiciable | Treating DPSP as enforceable rights | Article 37 explicitly states DPSP are fundamental in governance but not justiciable |

#### Mechanisms and Standard Problem Types

- **Application questions:** "Which Article/Act addresses X?" — recall provisions like Article 14 (equality), 15(3) (special provisions for women/children), 19(2) (reasonable restrictions on speech), 21 (life/liberty), 25(2) (religious freedom subject to public order/morality).
- **Case-law questions:** Identify the judgment (*Puttaswamy*, *Vishaka*, *MC Mehta*, *Shreya Singhal*, *Navtej Singh Johar* 2018) and its ratio.
- **Statute identification:** RTI 2005, RTE 2009, POSH 2013, Consumer Protection 2019, IT Act 2000, Biological Diversity Act 2002, Companies Act 2013 §135 (CSR 2% of net profits).

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** Which of the following statements about Fundamental Rights under the Indian Constitution is **incorrect**?
(a) Right to Privacy is recognised as a Fundamental Right under Article 21.
(b) Right to Education for children aged 6–14 is a Fundamental Right under Article 21A.
(c) The Right to Higher Education is also a Fundamental Right under Article 21.
(d) Freedom of speech under Article 19(1)(a) is subject to reasonable restrictions under Article 19(2).

**Solution:** Evaluate each option against constitutional text and jurisprudence. (a) Correct — a unanimous 9-judge bench in *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017) declared privacy an intrinsic part of Article 21. (b) Correct — the 86th Amendment (2002) inserted Article 21A, operationalised by the RTE Act 2009 (enforced 1 April 2010), guaranteeing free and compulsory education for ages 6–14. (c) **Incorrect** — higher education is governed by Articles 41 (DPSP) and 45 (now subsumed under NEP 2020 framework), but is NOT a Fundamental Right. The 93rd Amendment (2005) added Article 15(5) enabling reservations in private aided higher education, but this is not equivalent to a fundamental right guarantee. (d) Correct — Article 19(2) lists eight grounds of reasonable restriction (security of State, public order, decency, morality, sovereignty, integrity, foreign relations, contempt, defamation, incitement to offence).

#### Answer: (c).

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students frequently select option (b) as the wrong answer because they assume *higher* education is also covered by 21A. The 21A guarantee is strictly for ages 6–14; confusing it with the broader right to life (Article 21) is a classic Paper-1 distractor.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline targeting high scores.

#### Edge Cases, Exceptions and Layered Traps

- **Qualified vs. Absolute Rights:** No Fundamental Right in the Indian Constitution is absolute. Articles 19(2)–(6), 25(2), 26(2) and 30(1) proviso all embed "reasonable restrictions." The State can impose limitations on speech, assembly, profession and religion in the name of public order, morality, or State security. Even Article 21 yields to Article 21 procedure established by law.
- **Intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989):** Gender discrimination in India often co-occurs with caste, class, disability, and rural-urban location. UGC NET rewards layered analysis — a Dalit woman in a drought-affected region experiences compounded marginalisation that single-axis feminist frameworks fail to capture.
- **CSR ≠ Philanthropy:** Section 135 of the Companies Act 2013 mandates 2% of *average net profits of the preceding three financial years* be spent on CSR activities listed in Schedule VII. Failure is not a penalty-trigger but requires disclosure under Section 135(6) and explanation in the Board's report.
- **Net Neutrality and the OTT regime:** TRAI's 2015 *Prohibition of Discriminatory Tariffs for Data Services Regulations* enforces no slow-lanes; OTT apps are now classified under the Telecommunications Act 2023.
- **Climate Justice vs. Climate Mitigation:** Mitigation = reducing emissions; Justice = differential historical responsibility (CBDR principle) demanding developed nations bear greater burden. India invokes CBDR-RC at COP summits to defend its developmental space.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. **Conceptual integration:** Critically evaluate how *K.S. Puttaswamy (2017)* and the *Navtej Singh Johar (2018)* judgments together expand the scope of Article 21 for marginalised communities. Discuss whether the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 adequately operationalises the *Puttaswamy* proportionality test (legality, legitimate aim, necessity, balancing).
2. **Policy analysis:** Examine the National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC, 2008) — eight missions including Solar, Energy Efficiency, Sustainable Habitat, Water, Himalayan Ecosystem, Green India, Sustainable Agriculture, Strategic Knowledge. Identify two missions whose targets have been substantially missed and propose mid-course corrections grounded in the Sendai Framework (2015–2030) priorities.

#### Common Mistakes That Cost Marks

| Mistake Pattern | Correct Position | Frequency in Paper-1 |
|---|---|---|
| Citing struck-down §66A IT Act as current law | Struck down in *Shreya Singhal* (2015); replaced by IT Rules 2021/2023 | Very high |
| Treating DPSP as justiciable | Article 37 — fundamental in governance, NOT enforceable by courts | High |
| SDG goals memorised by number only | Understand interlinkages and NITI Aayog SDG India Index methodology | High |
| 42nd Amendment confused with 44th | 42nd (1976) inserted *socialist, secular, integrity*; 44th (1978) attempted to remove them under Janata govt but failed | Medium |
| Confusing biological sex with socially constructed gender | Sex = biological; Gender = social construct; UGC NET distinguishes | Medium |

> 💡 **High-Yield Revision Hook:** For every case-law question, anchor your recall to **"P-V-S-N"** — *Puttaswamy* (Privacy, 2017), *Vishaka* (Sexual Harassment at workplace → POSH 2013, 1997), *Shreya Singhal* (Section 66A struck down, 2015), *Navtej Singh Johar* (Section 377 partial strike-down, 2018). Memorise year + issue + Article violated.
