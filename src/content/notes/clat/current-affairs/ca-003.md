# Legal News

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Legal News in CLAT covers recent judicial, legislative, and constitutional developments from roughly the 12 months preceding the exam. The Consortium of NLUs tests comprehension of *what happened*, *which court or legislature acted*, and *which Article, Section, or doctrine is implicated*. Candidates must connect current events to static GK, not memorise dates.

- **Core legal-news categories:** Supreme Court/High Court judgments, new Central Acts/Ordinances, Law Commission reports, Constitution Bench rulings, PIL outcomes, and international legal developments affecting India.
- **Mandatory linking habit:** Every news item must map to at least one constitutional or statutory anchor — Article 21, Article 32, Article 226, Section BNS, Section BNSS, Section IPC (legacy).
- **Top three 2023–2024 triggers:** BNS/BNSS/BSA replacing IPC/CrPC/IEA from 1 July 2024; Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023; Electoral Bonds verdict (February 2024).
- **Always identify:** the issuing court, the bench strength, the binding ratio, and the operative Article/Section.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"ABC-D"** — **A**rticle + **B**ench + **C**ourt + **D**octrine. Before answering, mentally fill all four boxes; an option missing the Article or Doctrine is usually the distractor.

| Term | One-line meaning |
|---|---|
| Ratio Decidendi | The binding legal reason in a judgment |
| Obiter Dicta | Persuasive but non-binding observations |
| Ordinance | Law made by the President under Article 123 when Parliament is not in session |
| PIL | Public Interest Litigation — Article 32/226 locus standi relaxed |
| Constitution Bench | Bench of 5 or more SC judges for substantial constitutional questions |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### What CLAT Actually Tests in Legal News

The Current Affairs section carries 5% weightage, but legal events dominate the comprehension stems. A 2024 CLAT passage on the new criminal laws required candidates to identify that BNS Section 63 replaced IPC Section 354, and that sedition under IPC Section 124-A was dropped and re-enacted as BNS Section 152 with a narrower definition. Without mapping to the new section, candidates lost marks.

CLAT frames questions in three modes: factual recall (which court/Act), linkage (Article to case), and application (does this judgment apply pan-India or only between the parties).

#### Concept Comparison Matrix — Easily Confused

| Concept A | Concept B | Key Distinction |
|---|---|---|
| BNS | IPC | BNS replaces IPC from 1 Jul 2024; section numbers are renumbered |
| BNSS | CrPC | BNSS introduces electronic FIR, video conferencing, mercy petition timeline |
| BSA | IEA | BSA allows electronic and digital records as primary evidence |
| Article 32 | Article 226 | 32 → SC only; 226 → HC + SC; both are writ jurisdictions |
| Collegium | NJAC | Collegium is the *current* system; NJAC was struck down in 2015 (99th Amendment) |
| Ordinance | Bill | Ordinance lapses in 6 weeks of reassembly unless ratified |
| Ratio | Obiter | Ratio binds lower courts; obiter only persuades |
| Constitution Bench | Division Bench | Constitution Bench has 5+ SC judges for substantial questions of law |

#### Must-Know Legal Vocabulary

1. **Writ Petition** — formal request to SC/HC under Article 32/226 for enforcement of fundamental rights.
2. **Habeas Corpus** — "produce the body" writ, used against illegal detention.
3. **PIL** — litigation in public interest with relaxed standing rules.
4. **Constitutional Bench** — SC bench of five or more judges hearing substantial questions of constitutional law.
5. **Basic Structure Doctrine** — Parliament cannot amend the Constitution so as to destroy its basic structure (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973).
6. **Living Tree Doctrine** — Constitution interpretation that grows with time (used in *Puttaswamy* and *Navtej Johar*).

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** In a CLAT comprehension passage, the Supreme Court in February 2024 struck down the Electoral Bonds Scheme. Which of the following Articles were held to be violated by the scheme?

(a) Article 14 and Article 19(1)(a)
(b) Article 21 and Article 19(1)(a)
(c) Article 32 and Article 226
(d) Article 14 and Article 21

#### Solution:

1. Identify the case: *Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India* (February 2024), decided by a 5-Judge Constitution Bench led by CJI Chandrachud.
2. The Court held that anonymous political funding violated the **Right to Information** of voters under **Article 19(1)(a)** and the **Right to Equality** under **Article 14** (arbitrariness + transparency).
3. Option (a) correctly maps to the binding ratio: *Article 14 + 19(1)(a)*.
4. Option (c) is procedurally wrong — Articles 32 and 226 are writ jurisdictions, not the substantive provisions violated.
5. The bench relied on the *Puttaswamy* (2017) Right to Privacy line, but privacy was not the operative ground here — it was informational transparency.

#### Answer: (a) Article 14 and Article 19(1)(a)

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students often pick (b) because the judgment cited *Puttaswamy*. But the Court used privacy reasoning only to extend informational autonomy into political funding — the *binding ratio* rested on Articles 14 and 19(1)(a), not Article 21. Confusing obiter with ratio is the classic trap.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Doctrinal Edge Cases and Boundary Conditions

1. **Ordinance lifecycle (Article 123):** Ordinance lapses at the end of **six weeks** from the date Parliament next meets. Re-promulgation is permitted only when Parliament is genuinely unable to act, per the *DC Wadhwa* (1987) ruling. A Bill pending in Parliament is **not law** until both Houses pass it and the President assents — CLAT options frequently test this.
2. **Constitution Bench vs. Larger Bench conflict:** When a 3-Judge bench disagrees with a 5-Judge precedent, the matter is placed before a 5-Judge bench; only a 5-Judge bench can overrule another 5-Judge bench. CLAT 2023 tested whether a 2-Judge bench could revisit *Sabarimala* (2018, 5-Judge) — answer: no.
3. **BNS Section 63 vs. IPC Section 354:** Same offence (assault or criminal force on a woman with intent to outrage modesty), new section number. CLAT 2024 expected candidates to map old IPC sections to new BNS equivalents — penalising those who recalled "354" without recognising "63 BNS" as the current provision.
4. **Sedition under BNS Section 152:** The narrow re-enactment (acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India) replaced IPC Section 124-A but added safeguards — words, signs, or representations must threaten, not merely criticise. Mere speech remains protected under Article 19(1)(a).
5. **ICJ and Indian implications:** Election-related disputes and extradition matters (Nirav Modi, 2023) tested whether India is bound by ICJ advisory opinions — India follows a *consent-based* approach; ICJ rulings bind only parties to the dispute.

#### Common Traps in Legal News Passages

| Trap | How the Examiner Sets It |
|---|---|
| Conflating Bill with Act | Mentions a Bill debated in Parliament as if enacted — distractors assume assent |
| Wrong bench strength | Lists a 2-Judge ruling when the binding precedent was 5-Judge |
| Stale reference | Quotes an IPC section when BNS has replaced it post-July 2024 |
| Confusing PIL maintainability | Treats PIL outcome as binding precedent (PILs often settle via directions, not ratio) |
| Confusing NJAC with Collegium | Presents NJAC as the *current* appointment system |
| Wrong treaty attribution | Treats a UNHRC observation as binding on Indian courts |
| Mixing up Article 32 and 226 | 32 is SC-only writ jurisdiction; 226 is HC writ jurisdiction |

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

- **Prompt 1:** A CLAT stem cites *Navtej Singh Johar* (2018). Identify (a) the bench strength, (b) the Articles read into the ratio, and (c) which earlier judgment was overruled (*Suresh Kumar Koushal*, 2013). Failure mode: students often cite Naz Foundation (HC, 2009) as the overruled authority — that was the HC ruling that did not reach SC.
- **Prompt 2:** A passage states that the Mediation Act, 2023, came into force on a specific date. Identify the date (15 September 2023 via notification), the body enforcing it (Mediation Council of India), and the constitutional basis (Entry 11, List III — Concurrent List). Failure mode: candidates answer "private contract law" instead of locating the List III entry.

#### Exam Strategy

Allocate roughly 1.5 hours weekly to maintain a fortnightly log of SC/HC orders, presidential assents, and Law Commission reports. Map each entry to its Article/Section. CLAT Current Affairs questions reward linkage precision — the candidate who answers "BNS Section 63, not IPC 354" scores over the one who writes "Section 354" alone.
