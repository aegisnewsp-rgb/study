# Comprehension and Analysis of Written Information

Section 1 of HPAT-Ireland does not test whether you have read a lot. It tests whether you can hold a short passage in your head, find the one clause that answers the question, and drop the rest. The skill is deliberately unglamorous and it is where a large share of Section 1 candidates lose time, because they read everything at equal weight instead of reading for the question.

## The single rule: question first, passage second

Candidates who read the passage first are working the wrong way round. You cannot know what to retain until you know what will be asked. The efficient order is:

1. Read the question stem and identify the **noun phrase** being asked about — the specific thing you must find.
2. Note the **qualifier** — the condition attached to it. *Only*, *except*, *according to the second paragraph*, *before*, *in contrast to*.
3. Read the passage once, scanning for the noun phrase.
4. Stop as soon as you have the answer. Reading past it only costs time.

The noun phrase and the qualifier together are the search key. Written down, they beat rereading the passage every time.

## What the passage is built from

HPAT passages are constructed rather than sampled — they are written to carry a specific load. Recognising the construction tells you where the answer sits.

**Every fact has one home.** In a well-built passage, the detail you need is stated once, in one place, and the rest of the text exists to make that one detail findable under time pressure. If you find two apparently relevant sentences, you have either found a distractor or misread the qualifier.

**Distractors are near-misses.** They are the same subject, the same entities, the same timeframe, with one word changed — a negation swapped, a comparison reversed, a modifier moved, or a detail belonging to a different part of the passage. They do not announce themselves. They are defeated by matching the qualifier, not by reading harder.

**The last line is often load-bearing.** Academic-style passages frequently put a conclusion, a qualification or a reversal at the end. Candidates who stop early lose exactly that line.

**Tables and diagrams appear alongside prose.** Where a question refers to a figure or a table, the answer is frequently in the table even when the prose around it is the more readable text. Check both; do not assume the passage paragraph carries it.

## Inference, and where it stops

HPAT separates three levels, and the distinction is worth marking in your own answers:

| Level | What it means | Example form |
|---|---|---|
| Stated | Directly in the text | "According to the second paragraph, …" |
| Supported inference | Follows necessarily from what is stated | "Which statement must also be true?" |
| Weak inference | Plausible but not necessary | "Which statement might be true?" |

"Where must the author disagree?" is a supported inference: the passage commits to an alternative. "What might the author be sceptical of?" is weak: several answers may be consistent. When the stem says *must*, *necessarily* or *best*, you are in the strict register and must rule out everything that is merely consistent. When it says *might*, *suggest* or *could*, consistency is enough.

Candidates lose marks by answering the strict question at weak-inference strength, and by answering the weak question as though it were strict. Read the modal verb as a specification.

## Method under time

- **Two passes maximum.** A first locating pass and a confirming glance. A third read is a sign you did not note the noun phrase.
- **Mark as you go.** A single character against an option — correct, wrong, or *answer here* — stops you re-reading all four options later. "Answer here" is the valuable mark: it tells you where to return if time runs short.
- **Guess rather than leave.** There is no penalty for a wrong answer on a multiple-choice paper. An unanswered item is strictly worse than a coin toss. Sweep for blanks at a checkpoint rather than at the end.
- **Do not let a hard item eat the next one.** Section 1 items are not ordered by difficulty in a way that rewards persistence. Coming back later with the mark already made is worth more than five minutes sunk.

## What this does not involve

Nothing here is recalled. HPAT draws on no syllabus, no subject knowledge and no prior reading. The entire content of this skill is: find the question, find the clause, discard the rest. Candidates who approach it as a knowledge test perform far below candidates who approach it as a search procedure.

## Self-check

1. You have not yet read the passage. What is the first thing you do? *(Read the question stem and write down the noun phrase plus any qualifier.)*
2. You find two sentences that appear to answer the question. What do you do? *(Check the qualifier. One of them will fail it — a negation, a condition or a referent mismatch. If both pass, you have probably misidentified what is being asked.)*
3. A stem says "which statement must be true?" Your chosen answer is consistent with the passage but not forced by it. Is it safe? *(No. "Must" requires a supported inference — something that follows necessarily. Consistency is the weaker test and other options may also be consistent.)*
4. The passage ends with "however". Why does that word matter? *(It signals a reversal, qualification or limitation, which is exactly the kind of line a question stem is built from. It is often the only line in the final third worth reading closely.)*
5. You have left four items blank and have time for two. What do you do? *(Guess on all four before attempting the two. On a multiple-choice paper an unanswered item scores nothing while a guess has some chance; leaving blanks first and running out of time is the worse ordering.)*
