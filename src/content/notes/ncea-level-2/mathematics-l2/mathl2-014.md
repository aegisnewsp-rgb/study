# Apply systems of equations in solving problems (91269) — NCEA Level 2 Mathematics Notes

Achievement Standard 91269 is the smallest internal standard (2 credits) in the NCEA Level 2 Mathematics algebra strand. It tests systems of simultaneous linear equations in two or three variables, solved algebraically and graphically, applied to problems with constraints. The standard is the foundation for the Level 3 standard 91587 (systems of three equations in three variables).

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **2 credits**, internally assessed by the school, NZQA moderated.
- Topics: systems of two linear equations in two variables, systems of three linear equations in three variables (Level 2 introduces the idea; Level 3 standard 91587 deepens), graphical solution (intersection of lines), algebraic solution (substitution, elimination).
- **Merit** requires relational thinking — choosing the right method for the system.
- **Excellence** requires extended abstract thinking — justifying method choice, generalising to higher dimensions.

#### Examiner traps

- Sign errors when multiplying equations to eliminate a variable.
- Stopping after finding one variable (need to substitute back for the others).
- Confusing inconsistent (no solution) and dependent (infinitely many solutions) systems.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Systems of two equations in two variables

General form:

a₁x + b₁y = c₁
a₂x + b₂y = c₂

Three cases:

- **Unique solution** — the two lines intersect at one point. The lines have different gradients.
- **No solution** — the two lines are parallel and distinct. Same gradient, different intercept.
- **Infinitely many solutions** — the two lines are the same. Same gradient and intercept.

#### Algebraic solution methods

**Substitution:**

1. Solve one equation for one variable.
2. Substitute into the other equation.
3. Solve for the remaining variable.
4. Substitute back.

**Elimination:**

1. Multiply one or both equations so the coefficients of one variable match.
2. Add or subtract the equations to eliminate that variable.
3. Solve for the remaining variable.
4. Substitute back.

#### Systems of three equations in three variables

General form:

a₁x + b₁y + c₁z = d₁
a₂x + b₂y + c₂z = d₂
a₃x + b₃y + c₃z = d₃

Solve by reducing to a 2x2 system:

1. Use two of the three equations to eliminate one variable (say, z). Get one equation in x and y.
2. Use a different pair of equations to eliminate z again. Get a second equation in x and y.
3. Solve the 2x2 system for x and y.
4. Substitute back into one of the original equations to find z.

At Level 2, students are introduced to the 3x3 case; the full mechanics are covered in Level 3 standard 91587.

#### Graphical interpretation

For two equations in two variables:

- Each equation represents a line.
- The solution is the intersection point of the two lines.
- If the lines are parallel, no intersection; if they are the same line, infinite intersections.

For three equations in three variables:

- Each equation represents a plane.
- The solution is the intersection point of the three planes (unique if the planes are not parallel and not coincident).

#### Worked example

Solve:

2x + 3y = 12
4x − y = 5

Using elimination:

Multiply the first equation by 1 and the second by 3:

2x + 3y = 12
12x − 3y = 15

Add: 14x = 27, so x = 27/14 ≈ 1.93.

Substitute into the second equation: 4(27/14) − y = 5 → 108/14 − y = 5 → y = 108/14 − 70/14 = 38/14 = 19/7 ≈ 2.71.

Check with the first equation: 2(27/14) + 3(19/7) = 54/14 + 57/7 = 54/14 + 114/14 = 168/14 = 12. ✓

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Method selection heuristic

For a 2x2 system:

- **One variable isolated already?** Substitution.
- **Coefficients match after a simple multiplication?** Elimination.
- **Both methods work?** Either; elimination is usually less error-prone.

For a 3x3 system (introduced at Level 2, deepened at Level 3):

- **Reduction to 2x2:** use elimination systematically.

#### Geometric interpretation

Each linear equation in n variables represents a hyperplane in n-dimensional space. The system has:

- A unique solution if the hyperplanes intersect at exactly one point.
- No solution if the hyperplanes are inconsistent (parallel and distinct in some sense).
- Infinitely many solutions if the equations are dependent (the hyperplanes share a common subspace).

This geometric view generalises to any number of variables and is the foundation for linear algebra.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Subtracting always eliminates." Only if the coefficients match after multiplication.
- "No solution means I made an error." Not necessarily — some systems are genuinely inconsistent (e.g., x + y = 1, x + y = 2).
- "Infinitely many solutions is the same as no solution." No — infinitely many means there is a whole family of solutions; no solution means there are none.
- "Three equations always have a unique solution." No — they might have no solution, one solution, or infinitely many solutions depending on the geometry.
