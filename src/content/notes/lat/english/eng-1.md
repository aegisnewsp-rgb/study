# Vocabulary and Word Usage

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Vocabulary and Word Usage** in LAT English measures precision: choosing the *right* word, not just *a* word. Five question families dominate the section — **synonyms**, **antonyms**, **analogies (A:B :: C:D)**, **one-word substitutions**, and **usage-in-sentence** (confusables, idioms, phrasal verbs).

Memorise these non-negotiable pairs before the exam: **affect** (verb, to influence) vs **effect** (noun, result); **principal** (main / head of school) vs **principle** (rule); **stationary** (not moving) vs **stationery** (paper goods); **complement** (to complete) vs **compliment** (praise). Each appears almost every LAT cycle.

For analogies, name the relationship first — *cause-effect, part-whole, function, degree, synonym, antonym* — then match it. A student who reads the stem as "X is to Y as Z is to ___" loses marks; a student who reads it as "X *functions as* Y, so Z *functions as* ___" scores.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Lexical Categories Tested

LAT examiners do not test whether you know a word exists; they test whether you can deploy it correctly in a **legal or formal register**. Four distinctions carry most of the marks:

- **Denotation vs connotation.** Denotation is the dictionary meaning; connotation is the emotive charge. *"Slim"* and *"skinny"* both denote leanness, but only *slim* is a compliment — a letter of recommendation that calls a candidate *skinny* has misfired on connotation.
- **Polysemy vs homonymy.** *Bank* (river bank / financial bank) is **polysemous** — one root, related senses. *Bank* (river edge) vs *bank* (financial institution) is treated as homonymous because the senses share no semantic link. LAT analogy stems often exploit this overlap.
- **Idioms and phrasal verbs.** Phrases like *to bear with*, *to abide by*, *to set aside* (a judgment) carry **non-compositional** meaning. You cannot decode *"The court set aside the order"* from the literal sense of *set* + *aside*; the legal phrase is fixed.
- **Confusables.** Pairs whose spellings resemble each other but whose functions diverge — *discreet/discrete, elicit/illicit, eminent/imminent, judicial/judicious*.

#### Analogy Question Mechanics

The stem **A : B :: C : D** asks you to abstract the **logical relation** between A and B, then find a D that stands in the same relation to C. Standard relation types in LAT:

| Relation | Example A:B | Likely C:D |
|---|---|---|
| Synonym | candid : frank | honest : truthful |
| Antonym | arbitrary : reasoned | capricious : deliberate |
| Function | gavel : judge | stethoscope : doctor |
| Part-Whole | chapter : book | verse : poem |
| Degree | warm : hot | cool : cold |

Trap: two word-pairs may *sound* related (both contain the word *law*, both involve courts) but rest on different underlying relations. Always name the relation in plain English before scanning the options.

#### One-Word Substitution Strategy

LAT frequently asks: *"A person who believes that human actions are predetermined"* → **determinist**; *"A government by the few"* → **oligarchy**; *"Words that have opposite meanings"* → **antonyms**. Build these in clusters (governance, personality, law, crime) and revise weekly.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Etymology as a Decoder

Roughly 60% of English legal and academic vocabulary derives from **Latin** or **Greek** roots. Recognising roots lets you decode unfamiliar stems under exam pressure:

- *jur-/jus-* (Latin *ius*, law) → **jurist, jurisdiction, jurisprudence, jury, perjury, injurious**.
- *leg-* (Latin *lex*, law) → **legislate, legal, legitimate, legacy, legate**.
- *dict-* (Latin *dicere*, to say) → **dictate, dictum, contradict, predict, verdict, indict**.
- *crim-* (Latin *crimen*, accusation) → **crime, criminal, incriminate, recriminate**.
- *equ-* (Latin *aequus*, equal) → **equity, equitable, equanimity, adequate**.

**Trap to avoid:** a shared root does not guarantee semantic kinship. *Jury* (a body of jurors) and *injury* (harm) both descend from *ius*, but etymological cousins ≠ meaning-cousins. Use roots as a *first* guess, never a *final* one.

#### Connotation in Legal Register

LAT often frames usage questions inside legal or formal sentences, where **register** (the level of formality) is the deciding factor. *"The judge was **scrupulous** in her rulings"* works; *"The judge was **finicky** in her rulings"* does not, because *finicky* carries a trivialising connotation inappropriate to judicial conduct. When two options both fit the literal slot, pick the one whose connotation matches the surrounding tone.

#### Unpaired "Antonyms"

Not every word has a true opposite. *Unique, eternal, omnipotent, infinite* have no genuine antonym — substitute *nearly unique* rather than fabricate *ununique*. LAT traps candidates with options like *ununique* or *non-eternal*; recognise these as malformed and eliminate them.

#### Common Pitfalls

1. Picking a synonym that fits the bare dictionary gloss but ignores **legal collocation** (*sue* ≠ *prosecute* ≠ *litigate*).
2. Treating idioms as compositional (*to table a motion* in American English means to postpone, in British English means to bring up — LAT almost always uses the British sense).
3. Memorising word lists without seeing each word in a **contextual sentence**.

#### Practice Prompts

**Prompt A (Analogy).** *Arbitrator : Dispute :: Mediator : ?*
Identify the relation: an arbitrator *adjudicates* a dispute, so a mediator *facilitates resolution of* a conflict. Best answer: **Conflict / Negotiation / Settlement**.

**Prompt B (Confusables).** Choose: *"The witness was asked to ___ between the two suspects."* — **differentiate**, not *differ*. *Differ* is intransitive (*we differ on this point*); *differentiate* takes the *between* construction.
