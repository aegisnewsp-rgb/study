# Atoms

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Bohr's atom: electron orbits a nucleus in fixed stationary orbits where angular momentum is quantised, and photons are emitted or absorbed only when the electron jumps between orbits. The Rydberg formula gives every line of hydrogen.

| Quantity | Formula | SI units |
|---|---|---|
| Angular momentum quantisation | mvr = nh/(2π) | kg·m²/s |
| nth orbit radius | rₙ = n²a₀/Z | m |
| Energy of nth orbit | Eₙ = −13.6 Z²/n² | eV |
| Spectral wavelength | 1/λ = R_H (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) | m⁻¹ |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook (Memory Hook):** **"Lyman Balmer Paschen Brackett Pfund"** = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 — the lower level n₁ of each hydrogen series. Lyman = UV (1), Balmer = visible (2), Paschen/Brackett/Pfund = IR (3, 4, 5).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

> The working understanding — derivations, problem types, and the traps CUET setters exploit.

#### Three models that built the modern atom

**Thomson's plum pudding (1904)** proposed a uniform positive sphere with embedded electrons. It failed Geiger-Marsden's α-scattering experiment: most α-particles passed straight through, but a tiny fraction bounced back by angles greater than 90°. Rutherford's nuclear model (1911) followed: nearly all mass and positive charge sits inside a nucleus roughly 10⁻¹⁵ m across, with electrons orbiting a region about 10⁵ times larger.

**Impact parameter b** relates to scattering angle θ:
b = (Ze²)/(4πε₀ · KE) · cot(θ/2)
where KE is the incident α kinetic energy. For a head-on collision θ = 180°, cot(θ/2) = 0, and the distance of closest approach R₀ = Ze²/(4πε₀·KE).

**Bohr's postulates (1913)** — three rules that fused Rutherford's nucleus with Planck's quantum:

1. Electron moves in circular stationary orbits without radiating.
2. Angular momentum is quantised: mvr = nh/(2π).
3. Photon energy hν = E_i − E_f governs every transition.

#### Quantities that fall out of Bohr's postulates

Setting Coulomb force equal to centripetal force, then applying quantisation, gives:

| Derived quantity | Result | Variable meaning |
|---|---|---|
| rₙ | n²a₀/Z | rₙ in m; a₀ = 5.29 × 10⁻¹¹ m; n integer; Z atomic number |
| vₙ | (2.18 × 10⁶) Z/n | vₙ in m/s |
| Eₙ | −13.6 Z²/n² | Eₙ in eV |
| Orbital period Tₙ | n³/(Z² · 6.6 × 10¹⁵) | Tₙ in s |

The Bohr radius a₀ = 0.529 Å is the hydrogen ground-state radius, the unit of length for atomic physics. Doubling Z (He⁺, Li²⁺) halves every radius at the same n.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Feature | Rutherford model | Bohr model | Quantum mechanical model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Orbit description | Electron orbits classical nucleus | Stationary circular orbits, mvr = nh/(2π) | Orbital = probability cloud \|ψ\|² |
| Predicts spectra | No | Yes (hydrogen only) | Yes (hydrogen + multi-electron, approximate) |
| Treats electron as | Point charge | Particle with quantised orbits | Wave-particle duality |
| Respects Heisenberg | No | No (Δx · Δp = 0 implied) | Yes (Δx · Δp ≥ ℏ/2) |
| Fails for | All spectra | Multi-electron atoms, intensities | Relativistic effects in heavy atoms |

#### Worked exam-type problem

The wavelength of the second line of the Balmer series of hydrogen equals which of these — compute and pick: (a) 486 nm (b) 410 nm (c) 656 nm (d) 389 nm?

Balmer means n₁ = 2. "Second line" means the next transition after 3 → 2, which is 4 → 2.

1/λ = R_H (1/2² − 1/4²) = 1.097 × 10⁷ × (1/4 − 1/16) = 1.097 × 10⁷ × (3/16)
1/λ = 2.057 × 10⁶ m⁻¹
λ = 4.86 × 10⁻⁷ m = 486 nm

Answer: (a) 486 nm.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** "First line" of Balmer is the lowest-energy transition ending at n₁ = 2, i.e. 3 → 2 (656 nm, the red Hα line). "Second line" is 4 → 2. Many students pick 410 nm (Hδ, which is actually the 4th line). Numbering starts at the longest wavelength, not the highest-energy end of the series.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

> Edge cases, mathematical limits, and where Bohr breaks.

#### Where Bohr breaks down

- **Multi-electron atoms:** Bohr gives no recipe for shielding. Helium's first ionisation energy is 24.6 eV, not 4 × 13.6 = 54.4 eV — electron-electron repulsion drops it. Quantum mechanics with effective nuclear charge Z_eff fixes this.
- **Orbital angular momentum direction:** Bohr allows one circular orbit at each n. QM gives ℓ = 0, 1, …, n−1, each with (2ℓ+1) magnetic sublevels. A "2p" orbital has three orientations.
- **Zeeman splitting:** In a magnetic field, spectral lines split into components Bohr cannot explain. Quantum numbers mₗ and electron spin mₛ do.
- **Hyperfine structure:** Even QM misses the 21 cm hydrogen line without including nuclear spin.

#### de Broglie standing-wave interpretation

Bohr's quantisation rule becomes geometric: an integer number of wavelengths must fit around the orbit.

2πrₙ = nλ, with λ = h/(mv) → mvr = nh/(2π).

This predicts only circular orbits. An elliptical orbit with the same major axis has a longer circumference, so n wavelengths would not fit unless the wavelength changes. QM resolves this through quantised angular momentum in direction as well as magnitude.

#### Heisenberg limits on Bohr orbits

For a Bohr electron: Δx ≈ rₙ ≈ 5 × 10⁻¹¹ m, so Δp ≥ ℏ/(2Δx) ≈ 10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s.

But the electron's momentum itself is about 2 × 10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s. Bohr's model assigns Δx · Δp ≈ ℏ/2, sitting exactly at the quantum floor. Any sharper definition of position destroys the orbit.

| Limit / boundary | Behaviour | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| n → ∞ | Eₙ → 0, rₙ → ∞ | Ionisation; electron becomes free |
| n = 1, Z → large | rₙ → 0 | Electron falls into nucleus for very heavy hydrogen-like ions |
| Δt for transition | ≈ 10⁻⁸ s | Energy linewidth ΔE ≥ ℏ/(2Δt) gives natural line width |
| Photon momentum | p = h/λ | Recoil of the atom shifts emission frequency slightly (Mössbauer effect) |

#### Advanced practice prompts

1. A hydrogen-like ion emits a photon of wavelength 4.86 nm in a 4 → 2 transition. Identify the ion. (Use 1/λ = R_H · Z² (1/4 − 1/16). Solve for Z.)
2. Estimate the minimum uncertainty in the speed of an electron confined to a Bohr orbit of radius 0.53 Å using Δx · Δp ≥ ℏ/2. Compare with v₁ ≈ 2.2 × 10⁶ m/s and state whether Bohr's model is consistent.
