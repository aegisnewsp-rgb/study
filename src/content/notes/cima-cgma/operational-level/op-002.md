# P1 Management Accounting

P1 is one of nine Objective Tests in the CGMA Professional Qualification, alongside P1's siblings E1 and F1 at Operational Level and the six modules above it. Each Objective Test is 90 minutes, computer-based and computer-marked, available on demand throughout the year, and AICPA & CIMA states that the items "test all component learning outcomes across the entire subject" — a short multiple-choice question and a drag-and-drop item, drawing on every part of the syllabus rather than on the chapter you have just read.

That has one practical consequence: breadth pays. A candidate who has covered every part of P1 thinly will outscore one who has mastered two parts, because the exam samples across all of it.

## Cost behaviour, and why it is the first thing

A cost is fixed in total over a relevant range and variable per unit. Within that range, total fixed cost is constant and total variable cost moves with activity, so total cost rises at a decreasing rate while unit cost falls. That single graph is the foundation everything else sits on, and questions about it appear in several forms.

The classic trap is cost that is variable but not in proportion to activity — a stepped cost such as a machine changeover, or a supervisor paid per shift. It behaves as fixed within a shift and variable across shifts. If a question gives you a cost that jumps at a threshold, that is what it is, and treating it as smoothly variable produces the wrong break-even.

## Marginal and absorption costing

Marginal costing includes variable costs in inventory and treats fixed production costs as a period cost. Absorption costing includes fixed production overhead in inventory.

The entire difference is fixed production overhead: under absorption it is carried forward in inventory and released as cost of sales later, so profit in any single period moves with production volume. Produce more than you sell and profit rises; produce less and profit falls — for reasons that have nothing to do with trading.

This is the most heavily examined idea in P1 and the one where a well-chosen explanation earns marks. Learn it as a mechanism rather than as a set of rules.

## Break-even

Break-even is where contribution equals fixed costs: units = fixed cost divided by contribution per unit, where contribution per unit is selling price less variable cost per unit.

Margin of safety is actual or budgeted volume less break-even volume, expressed as a percentage of actual volume. Its meaning is how far sales can fall before the business loses money, which is a risk measure rather than a profit measure.

Learn the units and write them down. Mixing currency with currency is the most common arithmetic error in this topic and it is entirely avoidable.

## Budgeting, variances and standard costing

A variance has a cause, and the mark is in identifying it correctly. Standard costing decomposes cost and revenue differences into price and volume effects for materials and labour, plus efficiency and capacity effects for variable overheads.

The four-finger analysis is worth memorising: a favourable materials price variance with an unfavourable usage variance usually means the cheaper material was bought and more was needed, and that is a purchasing decision with a production consequence. Finding that link is what earns the higher marks, rather than correctly computing four numbers.

## Common mistakes

Saying absorption costing gives "a better" profit figure. It defers fixed production overhead; it does not create profit, and it makes profit depend on production volume.

Treating a stepped cost as smoothly variable in the break-even calculation.

Omitting the unit from a break-even answer. "Fixed cost divided by contribution" is only meaningful as a number of units.

Analysing variances without linking them. Four correct numbers and no explanation scores materially less than the same four with the purchasing-versus-usage story.

Confusing a revenue variance caused by price with one caused by volume, and answering with whichever the question did not ask for.

Forgetting that variable overhead efficiency and capacity variances only arise where an actual hour rate exists, which is a standard examiner trap in a question that omits it.
