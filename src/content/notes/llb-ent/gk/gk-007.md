# Notable South African Legal Cases

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Six cases cover the majority of marks on this 3% Gk slice — master them before chasing edge material.

The 1996 Constitution sits above every other source of law, and **Pharmaceutical Manufacturers (2000)** is the case that operationalises that supremacy. Whenever a question asks which judgment "binds the judiciary to the Bill of Rights when developing the common law," the answer is **Pharmaceutical Manufacturers**, not Certification (1996).

- **S v Makwanyane (1995)** — death penalty struck down; dignity, life and freedom clauses engaged.
- **Pharmaceutical Manufacturers (2000)** — horizontal application of the Bill of Rights through common law; courts cannot review Parliament's rules.
- **Grootboom (2001)** — section 26 housing; reasonableness review replaces minimum-core.
- **Treatment Action Campaign (2002)** — section 27 health; nevirapine for mother-to-child transmission.
- **Minister of Home Affairs v Fourie (2006)** — same-sex marriage; Civil Union Act followed.
- **Bhe (2005)** — customary primogeniture abolished; sections 9 and 25.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Architecture of the case law

South Africa's mixed legal system blends Roman-Dutch common law, English common law, legislation, customary law, and the 1996 Constitution. The Constitutional Court (ConCourt) is apex on constitutional matters; the Supreme Court of Appeal (SCA) remains apex on pure common-law points. Every landmark judgment below flows from the Constitution's supremacy and is reasoned in the register of **transformative constitutionalism** — using rights to redress the apartheid past.

#### Doctrine versus holding matrix

Candidates often confuse the legal doctrine with the specific outcome. The table below isolates both.

| Case | Core doctrine | What it did NOT do |
|---|---|---|
| Pharmaceutical Manufacturers (2000) | Horizontal application via s 8(3); separation of powers respects Parliament's rules | Did not give courts free reign to review internal procedure of the National Assembly |
| Makwanyane (1995) | Rights to dignity and life are interdependent; death penalty dehumanises | Was decided under the Interim Constitution, not the 1996 text |
| Grootboom (2001) | Reasonableness review for socio-economic rights | Did not impose a minimum-core obligation on the state |
| TAC (2002) | Section 27 obliges reasonable measures; court can compel a specific programme | Did not grant an unqualified right to all antiretrovirals |
| Fourie (2006) | Common-law definition of marriage read in with s 9 to include same-sex couples | Did not abolish the separate Civil Union Act framework |
| Bhe (2005) | Customary rule of male primogeniture violates equality and property dignity | Did not dissolve customary succession wholesale |
| Khumalo v Holomisa (2002) | Ubuntu extends to media law; dignity-centred defamation | Did not abolish the defences of truth and reasonableness |
| Mazibuko (2010) | Reasonableness, not minimum core, governs water rights | Did not set a fixed litre-per-day floor |

#### Worked exam item (multiple choice)

**Stem.** A candidate is asked: "Which judgment established that the Bill of Rights binds the judiciary when developing the common law, and that courts may not review the rules of Parliament?"

Options commonly offered:
1. Certification of the Constitution (1996)
2. **Pharmaceutical Manufacturers of SA: In re Ex parte President of the RSA (2000)** ← correct
3. S v Makwanyane (1995)
4. Government v Grootboom (2001)

**Resolution.** Option 1 concerns the Constitutional Court's own jurisdiction and the supremacy of the final Constitution — it does not bind the judiciary horizontally. Option 3 abolishes capital punishment under sections 10–12. Option 4 introduces reasonableness for section 26 housing. Only option 2 expressly states that section 8 binds the judiciary when developing the common law, while simultaneously protecting Parliament's internal rules from judicial review.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Candidates gravitate to **Certification (1996)** whenever they see "supremacy" in the stem. That case confirmed the Constitution's supremacy and the Court's jurisdiction — it did not establish the horizontal-application principle. Match the principle in the stem to the principle in the holding, not to the word "supremacy."

#### Frequently tested pairings

- Section 26 + reasonableness → **Grootboom (2001)**.
- Section 27 + reasonable measures + specific programme → **TAC (2002)**.
- Section 8(3) + common-law development → **Pharmaceutical Manufacturers (2000)**.
- Section 9 + customary inheritance → **Bhe (2005)**.
- Section 9 + same-sex marriage → **Fourie (2006)**.
- Sections 10, 11, 12 + death penalty → **Makwanyane (1995)**.

> 📌 **Section Watch:** Sections 8, 9, 10, 25, 26, 27 and 36 are the recurring statutory anchors. If the stem cites a section outside this set, check whether the question is testing the limitation clause (s 36) instead of a landmark case.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Why reasonableness, not minimum core, dominates post-2000 jurisprudence

The Constitutional Court split early on whether socio-economic rights impose a **minimum core obligation** — a baseline below which no state programme may fall. **Grootboom (2001)** rejected an enforceable minimum core for housing, and **Mazibuko (2010)** closed the door on a fixed litres-per-day floor for water. The doctrinal ceiling is now **reasonableness**: the court asks whether the state's programme is comprehensive, balanced, flexible and capable of meeting short-, medium- and long-term need. Practitioners litigating section 26 or 27 claims frame relief around the *policy* chosen, not around a numerical floor.

#### Ubuntu as method, not rule

**Bhe (2005)** and **Khumalo v Holomisa (2002)** place ubuntu at the centre of common-law development — yet ubuntu operates as a *value* informing interpretation, not a freestanding cause of action. Treating it as a rule produces circular arguments. The same logic runs through the SCA's incremental use of ubuntu in delict and contract cases.

#### Edge-case traps worth their own bullet

1. **Makwanyane's founding text.** It was decided under the Interim Constitution of 1993, not the final 1996 Constitution. Markers asking "under which Constitution" expect "Interim."
2. **TAC's scope.** The order compelled provision of nevirapine *to prevent mother-to-child transmission* in public hospitals — not universal antiretroviral access.
3. **Pharmaceutical Manufacturers' second half.** Candidates remember the horizontal-application line but forget the separation-of-powers holding: the Constitutional Court refused to sit in judgment over the National Assembly's internal rules.
4. **Fourie's remedy.** The Court gave Parliament twelve months to cure the defect; the Civil Union Act 17 of 2006 followed. The judgment did not itself create a marriage regime.
5. **Bhe vs Mdunge.** Bhe (2005) struck down primogeniture in intestate succession; *Mdunge v Minister of Home Affairs* concerns proprietary consequences of customary marriages — different doctrines, different sections.
6. **Trinity Asset Management (2018).** The SCA re-affirmed that the South African Revenue Service's tax-collection powers sit within separation of powers; courts decline to second-guess fiscal discretion. Useful when stems link "Rule of Law" to revenue.

#### Connections to the wider syllabus

- **Criminal law / sentencing.** Makwanyane feeds into the constitutional matrix applied to sentencing under the Criminal Law Amendment Act.
- **Family law.** Fourie and Bhe sit alongside the Recognition of Customary Marriages Act 120 of 1998 in any custom-and-constitution essay.
- **Administrative law.** Grootboom's reasonableness test is the same template applied to administrative action under PAJA.
- **Property law.** Bhe intersects with section 25 property-clause reasoning and intestate succession reform.

#### Two advanced practice prompts

1. **Compare** the doctrinal outcomes in Grootboom and Mazibuko. Why did the Court decline to entrench a minimum core for either housing or water, and what does that tell you about how section 27's "right to have access to… health care, food and water" is litigated today?
2. **Apply** s 8(3) of the Constitution to a private-sector dismissal based on HIV status. Walk from the horizontal-application principle in Pharmaceutical Manufacturers to a possible common-law development claim, naming the value of ubuntu as part of the reasoning.
