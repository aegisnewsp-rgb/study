# Legal Aptitude: Constitutional Law

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Last-day summary of constitutional provisions that LAT examiners actually test.

The 1973 Constitution of Pakistan is the binding supreme law. Any law inconsistent with its Fundamental Rights (Articles 8–28) is void under **Article 8**. Amendments follow **Article 239**: a Bill passes each House separately, then needs a **two-thirds majority of total membership** of the National Assembly — not merely two-thirds of members present and voting.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** "**FARA**" — **F**undamental rights void a law (**A**rticle 8), **R**ights are subject to reasonable restrictions (**A**rticles 9–13), **A**mendments need two-thirds of total membership (Article 239).

| Provision | Article | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Law void if inconsistent with Fundamental Rights | 8 | Strikes down conflicting statute |
| Inherent rights of life, liberty, property | 4 | Residual protection |
| Amendment by two-thirds of total membership | 239 | Rigid amendment procedure |
| Federal Shariat Court jurisdiction | 203-D | Shariah review of laws |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Salient Features of the 1973 Constitution

The Constitution is **written, rigid, federal, parliamentary, and Islamic**. Federalism divides power between the Federation and four Provinces under Articles 142–159. Parliament consists of the **President, National Assembly, and Senate**. Islamic character is enforced through the Federal Shariat Court under Article 203-D and the Principles of Policy in Articles 29–40.

#### Fundamental Rights — Articles 8 to 28

Article 8 voids any law, custom, or administrative action inconsistent with Fundamental Rights. Articles 9–28 enumerate specific rights: security of person (9), due process and arrest safeguards (10), equality and non-discrimination (25, 27), freedom of speech and assembly (16, 17), and freedom of religion (20–22). These rights are **not absolute** — they admit reasonable restrictions imposed by law in the public interest.

#### Comparison Matrix — Commonly Confused Provisions

| Concept A | Concept B | Distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Article 4 (inherent rights) | Article 8 (Fundamental Rights void) | Article 4 protects pre-constitutional common-law rights; Article 8 strikes down post-1973 conflicting law |
| Federal List Part I | Federal List Part II (Concurrent List) | Part I is **exclusive** federal competence; Part II permits provincial legislation not repugnant to federal law |
| Basic Structure Doctrine | Article 239 amendment power | Pakistan's Supreme Court has not adopted basic structure review as binding doctrine; amendments remain reviewable only on procedural grounds |
| Federal Shariat Court | Supreme Court of Pakistan | FSC is a specialized Article 203-D body for Shariah review; SC is the apex court with appellate jurisdiction |
| Principles of Policy (Art. 29–40) | Fundamental Rights (Art. 8–28) | Principles are **non-justiciable** directives to the State; Fundamental Rights are enforceable |

#### Amendment Procedure — Article 239

A constitutional amendment Bill is passed by **two-thirds of the total membership** of each House separately. Certain amendments affecting the federal-provincial bargain additionally require ratification by **not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting** in each Provincial Assembly.

#### Principle Application — A Sample LAT Passage

The Federal Government enacts the "Pakistan Media Coordination Act, 2024" granting the Ministry of Information power to pre-censor online news. A journalist challenges pre-publication restraint as violating freedom of speech. Which constitutional provision directly governs?

#### Resolution:

1. Identify the right invoked — freedom of speech and expression under Article 19.
2. Check whether the restriction is a "reasonable restriction imposed by law" in the interest of public order, morality, or national security — pre-publication **prior restraint** carries a heavy presumption against validity.
3. Apply Article 8: if the statute is inconsistent with Article 19 and fails the reasonableness test, it is void to the extent of inconsistency.
4. Conclusion: the pre-censorship provision is likely struck down; subsequent punitive regulation would be the constitutionally permissible route.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students often mark Article 25 (equality) when the passage concerns speech. Match the **specific right** to the article — speech is **19**, not 25. Another trap: assuming prior restraint is a "reasonable restriction" merely because it cites national security. Courts apply strict scrutiny to prior restraint.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Edge Cases and Judicial Review Boundaries

Three fault lines dominate LAT passages on constitutional law.

**1. The Basic Structure Question.** India's Supreme Court adopted the basic structure doctrine in *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973), holding Parliament cannot amend the Constitution's basic structure. Pakistan's Supreme Court has, in *Zafar Ali Shah v. General Pervez Musharraf* (PLD 2005 SC 530) and earlier rulings, rejected the binding adoption of this doctrine on the ground that the 1973 Constitution contains its own exhaustive amendment procedure under Article 239. LAT candidates should answer that amendments are reviewable only on **procedural compliance**, not substantive basic-structure grounds.

**2. Federal Shariat Court Reach.** Article 203-D empowers the FSC to examine whether any law is repugnant to the Injunctions of Islam as laid down in the Holy Quran and Sunnah. Appeals lie to the Shariat Appellate Bench of the Supreme Court. Confusing the FSC's **appellate or original jurisdiction** with the SC's constitutional jurisdiction is a frequent error — the FSC cannot strike down a law on grounds other than repugnancy to Islam.

**3. Provincial Ratification Trap.** Article 239(4) requires Provincial Assembly ratification when an amendment affects:
- the **federal-legislative** distribution under Articles 142–159,
- the **number of seats** in a Provincial Assembly, or
- the **procedure for constitutional amendment** itself.

A LAT MCQ may present a one-line amendment and expect you to identify whether provincial ratification is needed. The test is the **substantive effect**, not the amendment's title.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. Parliament passes an amendment deleting Article 19 (freedom of speech) by a two-thirds vote but without Provincial Assembly ratification. Identify the procedural defect and explain the judicial outcome under Article 239.
2. The Punjab Provincial Assembly enacts a law on cryptocurrency regulation. The Federal Government argues this falls within Federal List Part I (banking). The Province argues it falls within the residual provincial competence. Apply the Federal List Part I vs Part II (Concurrent) distinction and decide which level prevails.

> 📌 **Formula Check:** There is no numeric formula in Constitutional Law. The operative numbers are constitutional thresholds — **two-thirds of total membership** of each House (Art. 239(2)), and **two-thirds of members present and voting** in each Provincial Assembly for ratifying amendments affecting federal-provincial balance (Art. 239(4)(c)). These are distinct thresholds — do not interchange them.

#### Common Traps and Exceptions

| Trap | Correct Position |
|---|---|
| Fundamental Rights are absolute | Subject to reasonable restrictions by law (Art. 9–28 internal clauses) |
| Article 8 applies to pre-1973 laws | Article 8 voids only post-constitutional law; pre-1973 saving under Art. 4 |
| FSC can hear all constitutional matters | FSC jurisdiction limited to Shariah repugnancy review |
| Two-thirds means two-thirds of those present | Article 239 specifies **total membership**, not members present and voting |
| Basic structure doctrine binds Pakistan | Not adopted as binding doctrine by the Supreme Court of Pakistan |
