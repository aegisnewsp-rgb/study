# Cause and Effect Relationships

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **cause** is the event or condition that makes something happen; the **effect** is what happens as a result. In NCEE Verbal Reasoning, you must read a short statement and decide which option is the correct **consequence** of the action described, or which action produced the stated outcome. The single most-tested idea is **direction**: cause always comes *before* effect in time, never after. Two events occurring together is **correlation**, not causation — a frequent trap. When a chain is given (A → B → C), the effect of A is C, not B. Look for **transitive causation**: if A causes B and B causes C, then A causes C. Always pick the most **direct** link — examiners reward the proximate cause, not the most dramatic one.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Definitions
- **Cause**: the antecedent event that produces a change. Symbolically, *A → B* means A is the cause of B.
- **Effect (Consequence)**: the consequent event that follows from the cause.
- **Direct cause**: produces the effect without any intermediate step (fire → burns).
- **Indirect (remote) cause**: produces the effect through one or more intermediate links (lightning → power surge → computer damage).
- **Proximate cause**: the *closest* link in the chain — usually the best answer.
- **Necessary cause**: must be present for the effect to occur (oxygen for combustion).
- **Sufficient cause**: guarantees the effect on its own (a spark in a gas-filled room).

#### Reasoning Rules
1. **Temporal order**: cause precedes effect. Anything that happens *after* the effect cannot be its cause.
2. **Transitivity**: if A causes B and B causes C, then A causes C. But A is the *remote* cause; B is the *proximate* cause.
3. **Correlation ≠ causation**: shared timing or location does not prove a causal link. Two children falling ill after lunch does not mean lunch caused it — a virus may be the real cause.
4. **Negative cause**: the *absence* of a factor can be the cause (absence of rain → drought).

#### Typical NCEE Question Patterns
- **Completion type**: "Because it rained heavily, the match was…" — pick the effect (postponed / cancelled).
- **Odd-one-out**: four scenarios where three share a true cause-effect link and one is coincidental.
- **Best-cause selection**: given the effect, choose the *most direct* cause from four options.

#### Quick Comparison Table
| Pattern | What to identify | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| A because B | B is the cause | Reversed wording |
| A, therefore B | A is the cause | Hidden middle step |
| A led to B | A → B directly | Remote vs proximate |
| A and B occurred | None — correlation | Trap: pick a causal option |

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Mechanism Traps
- **Multiple causes → one effect**: a single outcome (poor harvest) may stem from several causes (drought, pests, poor seed). NCEE options often include only one valid cause; reject partial but insufficient options.
- **One cause → multiple effects**: a storm may cause flooding, power cuts, and school closure. Only one of these is usually offered as the correct answer — pick the *most immediate* effect, not a downstream consequence.
- **Reversed-cause trap**: an option that restates the cause as if it were the effect (e.g., cause = "heavy rain", wrong option = "cloudy skies" — that is a *cause of the cause*, not the effect).
- **Motive vs cause**: "He stole because he was hungry" — hunger is the cause; desire for money would be a motive. Examiners treat them differently; a cause must logically *produce* the outcome.

#### Worked Example
**Statement:** "Bola left her umbrella at home, so she got drenched on the way to school."
**Question:** Which of the following is the effect?
- (A) Bola left home (cause, not effect)
- (B) It rained (cause of the cause)
- (C) She got drenched ✓ — direct effect
- (D) Her uniform was wet — downstream effect of being drenched

The **best** answer is (C): the most direct, immediately produced consequence.

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics
This skill underpins **passage inference** (drawing conclusions from text), **analogy** (A:B as C:?), and **logical sequence** (arranging events in causal order). Mastering it lifts scores across all Verbal Reasoning sub-sections, not just standalone cause-effect items.

#### Practice Prompts
1. *"Because the power failed, the freezer thawed and the food spoiled."* Identify the **proximate cause** of the food spoiling.
2. *"Two students scored 100% in the exam; both studied the night before."* Explain why this does **not** prove that night-time study causes top marks.
