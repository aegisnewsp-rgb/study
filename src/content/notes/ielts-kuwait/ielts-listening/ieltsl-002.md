# Part 2 — Social Monologue — IELTS Listening Notes

Part 2 of the IELTS Listening test is a single-speaker monologue set in an everyday social context — for example, a recorded welcome speech about a community festival, an audio guide walking visitors through a museum, a radio programme introducing a new sports complex, or a presenter explaining arrangements for an open day. The recording is approximately 3-4 minutes long and is followed by 10 questions testing plan/map/diagram labelling, summary completion, sentence completion and multiple choice. Part 2 is one notch harder than Part 1 because the speaker talks more and you wait longer for the answer.

> Verify the live Listening test format, computer-delivered vs paper-based delivery, and the 10-minute transfer-time policy at your chosen test centre on https://ielts.org/ before booking.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

Part 2 is one speaker, not a conversation. There is no second voice to rescue you if you miss something. The single biggest mistake is treating Part 2 like Part 1 and expecting the speaker to repeat key numbers and names — the speaker usually says each fact once and moves on.

- **Speaker count**: one. Always.
- **Duration**: 3-4 minutes including the question-reading time that comes before the recording.
- **Question count**: 10 questions. Same as Part 1 and Part 3.
- **Question types tested in Part 2**: plan/map/diagram labelling, summary completion, sentence completion, multiple choice (you may also see matching, but it is rarer in Part 2 than in Part 1 or Part 3).
- **Tone of speaker**: presentational and informational, slightly more formal than the conversational Part 1 exchange.
- **Listening arc**: speaker (a) introduces the topic and place, (b) describes 3-5 features in a logical order, (c) closes with a call to action or summary. Questions follow the same arc.
- **Transfer time**: 10 minutes at the end of the Listening test to copy answers onto the answer sheet (paper-based) or review them (computer-delivered).

#### Common Part 2 contexts you should expect to hear

These six contexts recur in published IELTS books (Cambridge English IELTS 15, 16, 17 and the British Council takeielts.britishcouncil.org page). Prepare each as a separate vocabulary block.

- **A guided tour or audio guide**: museum, gallery, heritage site, university campus.
- **A community event**: festival, open day, charity fundraiser, school fair.
- **A facility description**: new library, sports centre, swimming pool, park.
- **A welcome speech**: conference opening, orientation talk, volunteer briefing.
- **A radio programme**: features about a local business, an interview-style intro, a community noticeboard segment.
- **An arrangements talk**: instructions for a trip, a workshop schedule, a recipe walkthrough.

#### Three traps that catch even prepared students

1. **The "false opening" trap**. The speaker spends the first 30-60 seconds describing the place or the host organisation (e.g. "Welcome to the Riverside Heritage Museum, which was opened in 1987 and is run by a small charitable trust"). The first question does not test this intro. If you focus on it you miss the first numbered answer that arrives at 0:45-1:00.
2. **The "promotional filler" trap**. The speaker inserts 5-10 seconds of promotional filler between facts (e.g. "and we are very proud to offer free parking to all members"). This filler does not answer anything. Students who listen for the next answer too eagerly panic when the next fact seems delayed.
3. **The "shift of focus" trap**. Halfway through the monologue the speaker pivots from the venue to the programme, or from the history to the facilities. The pivot usually lands at a section break (e.g. "now let me tell you about..."). When you hear a section break, expect a new question group to follow immediately.

#### Lite strategy — five moves that win Part 2

1. **Read every question and the surrounding context before the recording starts.** You have ~30 seconds. Use them.
2. **Predict the lexical set.** "Map of a museum" predicts rooms (gallery, atrium, lecture theatre, café). "Festival schedule" predicts dates, times, venues.
3. **Listen for signposting language.** "First of all", "the second thing", "another thing to mention", "finally". Each signals a new numbered answer is coming.
4. **Track spatial order on maps.** Part 2 maps are walked in a fixed order: entrance, then clockwise or counter-clockwise. The speaker never randomly jumps between locations.
5. **Write what you hear, not what you predict.** Spelling counts. Plurals count. Word-limit instructions ("NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS") count.

### 🟡 Standard — Full Prep (1d–1wk)

#### Section 1 — The structure of a Part 2 monologue

A Part 2 monologue is built in three movements. Recognising them helps you predict when the next question is coming.

| Movement | What the speaker does | How long | Where Part 2 questions sit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening (intro) | Welcomes the listener. Names the place, event or programme. Sometimes gives a history snippet. | 30-60 seconds | Usually NO questions here (trap #1) |
| Body | Walks through 3-5 features in a logical order (spatial, chronological). Each feature is signposted ("first", "another thing"). | 2-3 minutes | Most Part 2 questions test this section. |
| Closing | Summarises the key facts. Often includes a call to action ("we hope to see you there") or a date/time ("the exhibition closes on 28 October"). | 30-60 seconds | Sometimes 1-2 questions sit here. |

#### Section 2 — The four question types that dominate Part 2

These four question families account for ~85% of Part 2 question stems. Drill one at a time.

1. **Plan / map / diagram labelling** — you are given a floor plan, a map of a campus, or a diagram (e.g. a building cross-section). You label 4-6 features with letters from the recording. The speaker walks the route. Listen for spatial signposting: "on your left", "as you enter", "straight ahead", "next to", "opposite", "at the end of the corridor".
2. **Summary completion** — a short paragraph (50-80 words) with 4-7 gaps. You fill the gaps with EXACT words from the recording. Word-limit triggers ("no more than two words", "one word only") are enforced strictly. Anything beyond the word limit is marked wrong even if the meaning is right.
4. **VoiceTY completion** — a sentence stem with a gap at the end. You complete it with EXACT words from the recording. Often a noun phrase, a number, or a date. Watch for singular vs plural agreement ("$5 entry fee" not "$5 entry fees").
3. **Multiple choice** — three options (A/B/C) or five options (A-E). The speaker may give two distractors that sound right but are not. Listen for "but", "however", "although", "actually" — these signal the correct answer is replacing the distractor.

> **Word-limit warning (CRITICAL).** "No more than two words" means: the answer can be one word or two words, never three. It cannot be a hyphenated compound if the hyphen is not in the recording ("well-known" might count as one word, "well known" might count as two — match the recording's pronunciation).

#### Section 3 — A typical Part 2 script walkthrough

Below is the kind of monologue you will hear on test day, condensed from Cambridge English IELTS 17 Listening Test 1 Part 2.

> **Speaker**: Good morning, everyone, and welcome to the Oakwood Community Sports Centre. My name is Daniel, and I will be giving you a short tour of our new facility before we open the doors to the public at 11:00. The building you are standing in was originally the Oakwood Library, which closed in 2014 after more than seventy years of service to the local area. *(question gap: usually no question here)*
>
> As you walk through the main entrance, on your **right-hand side** is our welcome desk, where our staff will be happy to answer any questions you may have. Just past the welcome desk, you will find our **café area**, which serves hot and cold drinks and a selection of sandwiches throughout the day.
>
> Moving further into the building, you come to the **main sports hall**. This hall can be divided into three separate courts by movable partitions, and it is used for badminton, basketball and indoor football. On the **left-hand side** of the hall, we have a small gallery where spectators can watch matches.
>
> Upstairs, you will find our **fitness suite**, which is equipped with twelve treadmills, six rowing machines and a full range of free weights. We also have two **exercise studios** on this floor, where we run yoga, pilates and dance classes throughout the week.

The map questions would test: the welcome desk (right of entrance), the café (past the welcome desk), the main sports hall (further in), the spectator gallery (left of the hall), the fitness suite (upstairs), the exercise studios (also upstairs).

#### Section 4 — Five-part Part 2 vocabulary block

| Theme | Words and phrases to recognise on first hearing |
|---|---|
| Spatial | entrance, foyer, reception, corridor, atrium, hallway, lobby, staircase, lift, escalator, on your left/right, opposite, next to, adjacent to, at the end of, in the corner, on the first/second floor |
| Venue types | sports centre, leisure centre, community hall, conference centre, exhibition hall, heritage centre, gallery, museum, library, theatre, auditorium |
| Time markers | opening hours, business hours, weekday, weekend, public holiday, school term, half-term, season, summer/winter schedule, peak times, by appointment only |
| Programme features | workshop, guided tour, talk, demonstration, open day, trial session, drop-in session, advance booking, sign-up sheet, registration desk |
| People and roles | volunteer, steward, receptionist, instructor, guide, tour leader, host, organiser, curator, manager, member of staff |

#### Section 5 — Pre-listening checklist (do this in the 30 seconds before Part 2 starts)

- [ ] Read question 1 AND its prediction. Note the lexical set you expect.
- [ ] For map questions, identify the entry point and the direction of travel.
- [ ] For summary completion, count the gaps and note the word limit.
- [ ] For multiple choice, underline the differences between the three options.
- [ ] For matching questions, scan the list of items and skim the audio cues you expect.
- [ ] Mark any predicted question that may need a number, date or proper noun.

### 🟠 Exam Day Strategy (one week before the test)

#### Common Part 2 mistakes (do not make these)

1. **Writing more words than the word limit allows**. "No more than two words" means two words maximum. Writing three is marked wrong, even if all three words appear in the recording.
2. **Spelling the answer correctly but in the wrong section of the answer sheet**. Section A of the answer sheet is for Part 1. Section B is for Part 2. Section C is for Part 3. Section D is for Part 4. Fill the correct section.
3. **Panicking at the false opening**. The first 30-60 seconds of a Part 2 monologue is usually a welcome or intro. If question 1 does not arrive in the first minute, that is normal. Do not panic.
4. **Missing plural 's' endings**. The speaker says "tickets". You write "ticket". Marked wrong. Listen for the final /s/ or /z/ sound.
5. **Following the speaker instead of the route on a map question**. The speaker may describe the route in a different order than the questions test it. Trust the map, not the speaker.

#### High-Yield Part 2 patterns (band 7.0+)

1. **Spatial signposting**: "as you walk through the door", "straight ahead", "on your left", "opposite the reception desk", "at the far end of the corridor", "just past the staircase".
2. **Sequential signposting**: "the first thing to mention is", "another thing to remember", "one more feature worth highlighting", "and finally".
3. **Time markers**: "we are open from 9 am to 6 pm, seven days a week", "the café closes at 5 pm on Sundays", "the exhibition runs until 28 October".
4. **Quantifier patterns**: "we have three studios", "we offer six different membership tiers", "we have over 200 free parking spaces", "the main hall can hold up to 500 people".
5. **Promotional fillers (IGNORE)**: "we are very proud to", "we are delighted to", "we would like to thank", "we hope you enjoy". These are filler, not answers.
6. **Distractor reversals**: "the café is on the left ... actually, no, on second thought, we moved it to the right". The correct answer is the SECOND statement, not the first.
