# Genetic Engineering and Biotechnology — Leaving Certificate (Established) Biology Notes

Unit 3.3 is the strand finale and it brings together everything the course has built: the genetic code from unit 1.2, DNA replication and transcription from unit 2.3, enzymes from unit 2.1, and the classification work from unit 1.1 that put DNA viruses and bacteria in the same frame. The specification gives the process a clean five-word spine — **isolation, cutting, ligation, transformation, expression** — and then asks for two things beyond it: the **analytical** techniques (PCR, gel electrophoresis, sequencing, genome databases, DNA profiling) and the **ethical** discussion. Students who revise only the five-step process lose half of the marks available.

What makes this unit examinable is that it is full of things you can be *shown*. A gel, a profile, a restriction site, a PCR calculation — a question can hand you the diagram and ask what it means. Practise reading those in both directions.

---

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Genetic engineering** is the deliberate modification of an organism's genome — moving a gene from one organism to another so that it is expressed and produces a useful product.
- **The five-step process (specification):**
  1. **DNA isolation** — the gene of interest and a **vector** (usually a **plasmid**, a small circular piece of DNA found naturally in bacteria, and the method the specification refers to as the earlier approach) are cut out and purified from the cells that carry them.
  2. **Cutting** — both the gene and the plasmid are cut with the same **restriction enzyme**. The enzyme recognises a specific base sequence and cuts the DNA at a palindromic recognition site, leaving **sticky ends**.
  3. **Ligation** — the enzyme **DNA ligase** joins the gene to the plasmid by forming phosphodiester bonds at the sticky ends, forming **recombinant DNA**.
  4. **Transformation** — the recombinant plasmid is introduced into a bacterial cell, where it replicates as the bacterium divides.
  5. **Expression** — the gene is transcribed and translated by the host cell using the **universal genetic code**, producing the desired protein, which is then extracted and purified.
- **Why the genetic code matters here.** Because the code is universal, a human gene can be read and used by a bacterial cell. This single fact from unit 1.2 is what makes the whole process possible.
- **DNA extraction** — cells are lysed, proteins and RNA are removed (often with a detergent and an enzyme such as proteinase), and the DNA is precipitated out with cold ethanol, in which it is insoluble.
- **PCR (polymerase chain reaction)** amplifies a specific DNA sequence. The cycle has **three steps**, repeated about 25–30 times: **denaturation** (heat to about 95 °C to separate the strands), **annealing** (cool to about 50–65 °C so primers bind to the target sequence), **extension** (about 72 °C, where **Taq DNA polymerase** — from *Thermus aquaticus*, stable at high temperature — builds new strands). Each cycle **doubles** the amount of target DNA.
- **Worked PCR calculation:** starting with one copy of a target sequence, after *n* cycles there are **2ⁿ** copies. After 30 cycles that is about **1.07 × 10⁹** copies, which is how a single DNA molecule becomes a visible quantity.
- **Gel electrophoresis** separates DNA fragments by size. DNA is **negatively charged** because of the phosphate groups, so an electrical field drives it through a **gel** towards the **positive electrode (anode)**. Small fragments move fastest and furthest. The specification describes sequencing data producing a collection of fragments "each of which is one nucleotide shorter than the next", separated by gel electrophoresis — this is the principle of **Sanger sequencing**: a ladder of fragments differing by one nucleotide, run against the sample, and read from the bottom of the gel to read the sequence.
- **Restriction enzymes cut at specific sequences.** EcoRI cuts at GAATTC, leaving a sticky end of AATT; HindIII cuts at AAGCTT. **Worked question:** a circular plasmid of 6000 base pairs is cut once by EcoRI and once by HindIII, giving fragments of 2500 bp and 3500 bp.
- **DNA profiling (DNA fingerprinting)** produces a pattern of **variable-length, repetitive DNA sequences** that differs between individuals (except identical twins). Because these sequences are inherited, the profile is inherited too, and is used for **paternity testing**, **identification of remains**, **crime scene matching**, and in wildlife forensics.
- **Generating a profile** — obtain DNA from the sample, amplify the variable regions by PCR, digest or amplify them, run them on a gel, and compare the banding pattern against known samples.
- **The value of technology in analysing large quantities of genetic information** is that it makes it possible to detect **patterns and anomalies** across whole genomes — which is why sequencing moved from single genes to whole genomes, and why rare variants are now found at all.
- **Genome databases** can be searched for **alleles known to cause or be responsible for specific genetic diseases**, which is the basis of genetic testing and of personalised medicine.
- **Applications**: **agriculture** (Bt crops resistant to insect pests, herbicide-tolerant crops, longer-lasting fruit, disease-resistant livestock), **health** (insulin, human growth hormone, clotting factors, vaccines, gene therapy), **industry** (enzymes for detergents, food and brewing), **reproduction** (IVF, ICSI, preimplantation genetic screening), **forensics** (profiling).
- **Genetic modification** is the scientific process; **DNA testing** is the diagnostic use of the same information.
- **Stem cells** have two named uses: **therapeutic**, where stem cells are differentiated to replace damaged or lost tissue (bone marrow transplants in leukaemia, growing skin for burns); and **cloning**, producing genetically identical organisms.
- **Ethical issues** arising from genetic technologies: consent and privacy of genetic data; who owns a gene sequence; genetic discrimination by insurers or employers; the status of embryos created for IVF and for stem cell derivation; the "designer" or enhancement question; safety of gene-edited food; unequal access; and the farming of animals for organs. The specification's action verb here is **discuss**, which means a balanced range of arguments with evidence and a considered position.

#### Examiner traps

- **Reversing the direction of movement in gel electrophoresis.** DNA moves **towards the positive electrode** because it is negatively charged — which means it moves *away* from the negative electrode and *towards* the anode. Saying it "moves to the negative end" is the standard error.
- **Saying the largest fragment moves furthest.** Small fragments move fastest and furthest through the gel.
- **Forgetting that PCR doubles each cycle.** A question that gives a starting number and a cycle number wants 2ⁿ.
- **Naming the enzyme that cuts but forgetting ligase.** Cutting is restriction enzyme; joining is **ligase**.
- **Forgetting the vector.** A gene placed in naked DNA will not be maintained or replicated in the host — it needs a plasmid.
- **Treating a DNA profile as a photograph.** It is a **banding pattern** of fragment sizes, compared lane by lane.
- **Giving only one side of an ethical question.** The specification's verb is *discuss*, and the assessment descriptors reward balance plus a reached position.

#### 30-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Can you write isolation, cutting, ligation, transformation, expression in order and name the enzyme at each step?
- [ ] Can you draw a gel with correctly ordered bands and say which end is the anode?
- [ ] Can you calculate 2ⁿ for a given number of PCR cycles and explain why Taq polymerase is used?
- [ ] Can you name four applications and four ethical issues?
- [ ] Can you explain why a DNA profile can identify an individual but not, on its own, prove a crime?

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### The process, drawn

Draw a plasmid as a circle with a single EcoRI site, and mark the human gene you are inserting. Then walk the exam answer in the same order the drawing does.

1. **Isolation.** The desired gene is cut out of human DNA and a plasmid is isolated from bacteria. Both are purified so that no other DNA contaminates the process.
2. **Cutting.** The **same restriction enzyme** is used on both, which is the point of using sticky ends: complementary single-stranded overhangs form on each, so they base-pair to each other regardless of sequence.
3. **Ligation.** **DNA ligase** seals the sugar–phosphate backbone at the junctions, producing recombinant plasmid DNA.
4. **Transformation.** The plasmid is introduced into a bacterial cell — commonly by heat shock or electroporation — and is selected for using a marker gene, often an **antibiotic resistance** gene on the plasmid: bacteria that took it up grow on antibiotic-containing agar.
5. **Expression.** The gene is transcribed and translated by the bacterium. Because the genetic code is universal, the bacterial ribosome reads the human gene correctly and makes the human protein, which is extracted and purified.

A strong answer adds one limitation the specification implies: every copy of the plasmid carries the gene, so the bacterial host divides and expresses the gene continuously — which is exactly why this system is used for making large quantities of a protein.

#### Reading a gel — the two rules

**Rule 1: direction.** Place the **negative electrode at the top**, where the wells are, and the **positive electrode (anode) at the bottom**. DNA is negatively charged because of its phosphate backbone, so it migrates downwards towards the anode. Fragments furthest down the gel are the **smallest**.

**Rule 2: pattern.** Compare lanes, not positions. Two samples that came from the same person share **every** band.

*Worked question.* Four lanes on a gel: Lane 1, a DNA sample from a crime scene. Lane 2, a suspect. Lane 3, a second suspect. Lane 4, a known victim. Describe what the pattern would have to show to support the identification.
*A.* Lane 1 would need to contain **every band** present in the matching suspect's lane — all of them, at the same distances. Every band in Lane 1 must be explained by Lane 2; a single unmatched band is a problem. Lane 3 should be excluded: if Lane 3 shares only some bands with Lane 1, or contains bands Lane 1 lacks, that suspect can be eliminated. Lane 4 should be excluded too, since the victim's DNA would also be present at a violent crime scene and a partial match to the victim is not identification. Even a complete match is supporting evidence, not proof — profiles show the **probability** that two samples came from the same person, not certainty, and identical twins and close relatives share bands. That qualification is usually the mark that distinguishes the top band.

#### DNA sequencing and the one-nucleotide ladder

The specification describes generating a collection of DNA fragments "each of which is one nucleotide shorter than the next", separated by gel electrophoresis. This is the foundation of Sanger sequencing and it is worth understanding rather than memorising: the DNA is copied in the presence of a proportion of chain-terminating dideoxynucleotides, which lack the 3′-OH group needed for further extension. A terminating base therefore stops strand growth at that position. The result is a mixture of fragments of every possible length, differing by one nucleotide, and the shortest fragment corresponds to the **first** base of the original strand. Running them on a gel and reading from the bottom upwards reads the sequence.

The wider point the specification asks for is the **value of technology in analysing large quantities of genetic information**: doing this sequence-by-sequence by hand is possible but slow, whereas automation allows millions of bases to be read at once, whole genomes to be sequenced, and patterns or anomalies to be identified across a genome rather than within one gene.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Worked Higher-level questions with answers

**Q1.** A plasmid is 6000 base pairs long and contains single EcoRI (GAATTC) and HindIII (AAGCTT) sites. It is cut with both enzymes. State the number and sizes of the fragments and explain why the enzyme must be used at the right temperature.
*A.* Cutting a **circular** plasmid at two sites produces **two linear fragments**. Cutting it at one site alone would produce one linear fragment of 6000 bp. Once the two cut sites are mapped, the distances between them give the two fragment sizes — for example 2500 bp and 3500 bp, which must sum to 6000 bp; that sum check is worth stating. The temperature requirement follows because restriction enzymes are proteins, and a protein's activity depends on its tertiary structure. Too cold and the enzyme is too slow to cut efficiently within the reaction time; too hot and the enzyme denatures and cuts nothing at all, or cuts non-specifically. The optimum temperature is therefore a compromise, and the same is true of the buffer pH, because the enzyme also needs the right ion concentrations. This connects the unit straight back to unit 2.1.

**Q2.** Explain why bacteria are used as the host organism in genetic engineering.
*A.* Bacteria **reproduce very rapidly** by binary fission, so a plasmid carried in a cell is amplified into a very large population of copies in a short time, giving high yield. Their metabolism is well understood and they can be grown cheaply in large, controlled fermenters. Their genetic code is shared with all other organisms, so a human gene is read and expressed correctly in them thanks to the **universal genetic code**. Plasmids give them a natural, readily isolatable **vector** that replicates independently of the host chromosome. And bacteria can be screened in enormous numbers very quickly, which matters when you are looking for the one cell that took up the plasmid. The limitations — that bacteria cannot perform every post-translational modification a human protein needs — are why some products are made in yeast, insect or mammalian cells instead.

**Q3.** Evaluate the statement that DNA profiling can prove who committed a crime.
*A.* It cannot prove it, though it is powerful evidence. A profile compares **banding patterns**, and the result is a statistical statement: a match means that the probability of two unrelated individuals producing that pattern by chance is very low, which is highly significant. But it is not certainty, because the profile does not identify a person directly — it indicates whether two samples are likely to come from the same individual. Shared bands occur between close relatives, and identical twins have identical profiles. Contamination is possible at the crime scene or in the laboratory, and the interpretation depends on the chain of custody and on the quality of the reference sample. Courts treat it as one piece of evidence to be weighed with testimony, physical evidence and context. The considered position is that DNA profiling is the single most reliable identifier available in forensics, but that "prove" overstates what a probabilistic technique can do.

**Q4.** Discuss the ethical issues raised by genetic technologies, reaching a position.
*A.* Four areas matter, and a considered answer touches each. **Consent and privacy**: a person's genome is stable, identifies relatives as well as the individual, and cannot be made anonymous, so storing and sharing genomic data raises real consent problems — Ireland has a particular stake here as a country with a large, well-studied population and a strong genealogical tradition. **Property**: who owns a gene sequence isolated from a human, and whether patenting it is defensible when it was not invented; also whether seeds held by farmers should be able to be saved and replanted. **Embryos and stem cells**: destroying a human embryo to derive stem cells for therapy asks whether early embryos have the moral status people assign to later ones. **Access and enhancement**: gene therapy for a monogenic disease is different in kind from editing for enhancement, and access to expensive treatments could widen inequality. The evidence-based conclusion to reach: regulation, informed consent and public engagement are what make these technologies acceptable, and Irish law and EU regulation already provide some of that framework. A slogan in either direction loses the discussion mark.

#### How this unit pays elsewhere

- **Unit 1.2** supplies the universal genetic code, which is what makes expression in a bacterial host possible at all.
- **Unit 2.3** supplies replication, transcription and translation — the expression step runs entirely on that machinery.
- **Unit 2.1** supplies **Taq polymerase**, a thermostable enzyme from unit 3.2's microorganisms, without which PCR cannot cycle.
- **Unit 2.5** supplies IVF, ICSI, egg freezing and prenatal screening, all of which the specification lists as applications.
- **Unit 1.1** supplies the classification of viruses, relevant because viral vectors are used alongside plasmids.
- **Biology in Practice Investigation** (40% of the grade) — investigating a DNA profile pattern, or analysing primary data on gene expression from a genome database, produces exactly the quantitative evidence-based analysis the descriptors reward.

#### Mistakes students actually make

1. **Direction in gel electrophoresis.** DNA moves towards the positive electrode; the anode is the far end.
2. **Omitting ligase.** Cutting is restriction enzyme; joining is DNA ligase.
3. **Forgetting the vector**, and describing the gene being injected on its own into the host cell.
4. **Treating PCR as exponential without saying so.** 2ⁿ is the calculation and the doubling is the mechanism.
5. **Naming an enzyme that cannot do the job.** Ordinary DNA polymerase denatures at 95 °C; Taq survives it, which is the whole reason for using it.
6. **Overclaiming what a profile shows.** It supports identity probabilistically; it does not prove an act.
7. **Leaving the ethical answer one-sided.** Present the benefit, the risk, the regulatory response and a reached position.
8. **Failing to say why the human protein is made correctly in a bacterium.** Universal genetic code, one sentence, and it is the mark that shows the process is understood rather than recalled.
