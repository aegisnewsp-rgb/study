# Motion in One and Two Dimensions

NUMS allocates 40 of the 150 Paper I multiple-choice questions to Physics and states that 70% of Physics questions are recall-level. That is good news and bad news: the bad news is you cannot buy your way past a formula you have not learned, and the good news is you do not need to be good at problem-solving to score well on most of them.

Physics questions are also unforgiving about units. Almost every avoidable error in this topic is a unit or a sign error, not a conceptual one.

## One dimension first

Displacement is a vector: it has direction and it can be zero even when distance is large. Velocity is the time rate of change of displacement; speed is its magnitude. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, so constant velocity means zero acceleration regardless of how fast you are travelling.

The kinematic equations for constant acceleration — $v = u + at$, $s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$, $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$ — are three equations for four unknowns. Solve by counting: whichever of $u$, $v$, $a$, $s$, $t$ is missing tells you which equation to use. Using the wrong one because you did not count is the single most common procedural error in the topic.

Sign convention is not optional. Take upward as positive and gravity is $a = -9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$. Take downward as positive and gravity is $+9.8$. Either works, but mixing them inside one calculation produces an answer that is often physically plausible and completely wrong.

## Reading the graphs

A position-time graph tells you velocity from its slope and acceleration from its curvature. A horizontal segment means the object has stopped. A straight downward slope under an upward-positive convention means it has passed its highest point and is falling back.

A velocity-time graph tells you acceleration from the slope and displacement from the area under the curve. A velocity-time graph that crosses the axis means the direction of motion changed at that point, which almost always marks the highest or lowest point of a projectile.

Both traps come from the same habit: reading a graph by its shape instead of by what the axes measure. The shape of a curve is meaningless on its own. A line that rises on a position-time graph is acceleration; the same line on a velocity-time graph is constant acceleration.

## Two dimensions

Horizontal and vertical motion are independent, which is the entire content of the projectile-motion idea. Horizontal velocity is constant because there is no horizontal force; vertical velocity changes at $g$ because gravity acts on it. The trajectory is a parabola because a constant horizontal velocity combined with accelerating vertical motion produces one.

Two facts fall out of that and both are heavily tested. At the highest point, vertical velocity is zero but horizontal velocity is unchanged — the object is not stationary, it is momentarily not moving vertically. And in the absence of air resistance, time to rise equals time to fall, so the range is doubled by the initial vertical speed while the range depends on the square of that speed.

## Common mistakes

Using $g = 9.8$ as a mass-dependent force. Gravity is an acceleration, $g$, not a force. The force is $mg$, where $m$ is the mass in kilograms, and only then does Newton\'s second law apply.

Forgetting that velocity can be zero while speed is not, or that displacement can be zero while distance is large. Read the question\'s word.

Applying a vertical-constant-acceleration equation to a vertical axis on a graph whose vertical axis is something else — a stock chart and a reaction-rate graph both plot against time, and only one of them lets you use $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$.

Mixing conventions mid-calculation, usually by remembering $-9.8$ halfway through. Write the convention at the top of the page.

Measuring displacement as the area under a velocity-time graph and forgetting the area under a position-time graph means nothing. Match the graph to the question.
