# Measurements and Units

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **physical quantity** is anything measurable, expressed as **Q = n × u**, where *n* is the numerical value and *u* is the unit. The **SI system** defines seven **base quantities**: length (metre, m), mass (kilogram, kg), time (second, s), electric current (ampere, A), thermodynamic temperature (kelvin, K), amount of substance (mole, mol), and luminous intensity (candela, cd). Every other quantity — speed, force, energy, pressure — is a **derived quantity** built algebraically from these.

For NABTEB Physics (Objective + Essay), expect 3–4 short questions on least counts, unit conversions, and identifying base vs derived quantities. Memorise the prefixes **kilo (k = 10³), milli (m = 10⁻³), micro (μ = 10⁻⁶), mega (M = 10⁶)**. Use **0.01 mm** as the least count of a **micrometer screw gauge** and **0.1 mm** for a **vernier caliper**.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Base vs Derived Quantities
The seven SI base units are independent — none can be expressed in terms of the others. A **derived unit** arises from multiplying or dividing base units. Examples:

| Derived quantity | Formula | Derived unit |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | distance / time | m s⁻¹ |
| Acceleration | velocity / time | m s⁻² |
| Force (Newton's 2nd law) | mass × acceleration | kg m s⁻² (N) |
| Work / Energy | force × distance | kg m² s⁻² (J) |
| Pressure | force / area | kg m⁻¹ s⁻² (Pa) |

#### Errors and the Mean
Every measurement carries an **error**. The best estimate of the true value is the **arithmetic mean**: **x̄ = (x₁ + x₂ + … + xₙ)/n**. The **absolute error** of each reading is **Δxᵢ = |xᵢ − x̄|**, and the **mean absolute error** is the average of these. The **percentage (relative) error** is **εᵣ = (Δx̄/x̄) × 100%**, the form NABTEB examiners use when grading "compare the accuracy of two experiments."

**Systematic errors** shift all readings the same way (zero error on a metre rule, miscalibrated balance). **Random errors** scatter readings unpredictably and are tamed by repetition. **Accuracy** = closeness to the true value; **precision** = closeness of repeated readings to each other. A result can be precise but inaccurate.

#### Dimensional Analysis
Every derived quantity has a **dimensional formula** in terms of **M, L, T, I, K, mol, cd**. Two physical quantities can be added, subtracted, or equated **only if their dimensions match** — this is the **principle of homogeneity**. It is used to (i) check an equation's correctness, (ii) convert units, and (iii) derive relations between quantities.

#### Measuring Instruments
A **metre rule** reads to the nearest mm (least count 1 mm). A **vernier caliper** has 10 vernier divisions spanning 9 mm, giving least count **0.1 mm** = **0.01 cm**; use it for inner/outer diameters and depths up to ~15 cm. A **micrometer screw gauge** has 50 thimble divisions on a 0.5 mm pitch, so least count = 0.5/50 = **0.01 mm**; use it for thin wires and sheet thickness. Volumes of regular solids come from **V = L × A** (for a cylinder, A = πr²); irregular solids use the **displacement method** with a measuring cylinder.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases in Dimensional Analysis
Dimensional analysis **cannot** distinguish between quantities that share the same dimensions — work and torque both have dimensions [M L² T⁻²], yet they are physically different. It also **fails** for dimensionless constants (π, e) and for equations containing trigonometric, logarithmic, or exponential functions. When deriving a relation of the form **y = k xᵃ yᵇ**, equate dimensions on both sides to find *a* and *b*; the constant *k* remains undetermined and must come from experiment or theory.

#### Common NABTEB Traps
1. Confusing **mass** (kg, measured by a beam balance) with **weight** (N, measured by a spring balance; W = mg, g ≈ 10 m s⁻²). Mass is invariant; weight depends on local gravity.
2. Misreading a **vernier zero error**: if the vernier zero lies to the **right** of the main-scale zero, the error is **positive**, so subtract it; if to the left, the error is negative, so add it. Always state the sign explicitly.
3. Adding metres to seconds is dimensionally illegal — but converting **72 km h⁻¹ to m s⁻¹** is legal because both express the *same* quantity (speed), just in different units. Multiply by (1000 m / 1 km) and (1 h / 3600 s).

#### Connections
Measurements underpin every later topic: kinematics uses the second and metre, electric circuits the ampere, thermodynamics the kelvin. Mastering unit conversion and error propagation now prevents lost marks across the entire syllabus.

#### Practice Prompts
1. A wire is measured five times with a vernier caliper as 2.14, 2.16, 2.15, 2.14, 2.16 cm. Compute the mean and the percentage error.
2. The period *T* of a simple pendulum depends on length *L* and acceleration due to gravity *g*. Using dimensional analysis, show that T = k√(L/g) and state the dimensions of *k*.
