# Renal and Respiratory Pharmacology

In our review of recent FMGE examination patterns, renal and respiratory pharmacology accounts for three to four high-yield questions in every session. We recommend that candidates master the specific electrolyte footprints of each diuretic class and the stepwise escalation pathways for bronchial asthma.

## Diuretics and Renal Pharmacology

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your FMGE screening examination.

Diuretics promote the excretion of sodium and water by targeting specific transport proteins along the nephron. Because different nephron segments possess distinct transport capacities, the diuretic potency and electrolyte consequences vary predictably by site of action.

#### Summary of Diuretic Classes and Electrolyte Patterns

| Diuretic Class | Primary Representative | Molecular Target & Site | Fractional Excretion of $Na^+$ | Classic Electrolyte Footprint | Primary Clinical Indication |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Loop Diuretics** | Furosemide, Torsemide, Bumetanide | NKCC2 symporter in Thick Ascending Limb | High (20% to 25%) | Hypokalemia, hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, metabolic alkalosis | Acute pulmonary edema, congestive heart failure volume overload |
| **Thiazides & Thiazide-Like** | Hydrochlorothiazide, Chlorthalidone | NCC symporter in Distal Convoluted Tubule | Moderate (5% to 8%) | Hypokalemia, hypercalcemia, hyperuricemia, hyponatremia | Essential hypertension, nephrolithiasis due to idiopathic hypercalciuria |
| **Aldosterone Antagonists** | Spironolactone, Eplerenone | Mineralocorticoid receptor in Cortical Collecting Duct | Mild (2% to 3%) | Hyperkalemia, normal-anion-gap metabolic acidosis | HFrEF mortality reduction, hepatic cirrhosis with ascites |
| **ENaC Blockers** | Amiloride, Triamterene | Apical Epithelial Sodium Channel in Principal Cells | Mild (1% to 2%) | Hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis | Counteracting thiazide-induced hypokalemia, Liddle syndrome |
| **Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitors** | Acetazolamide | Apical CA IV and cytosolic CA II in Proximal Tubule | Mild (3% to 5%) | Bicarbonaturia, hypokalemic metabolic acidosis | Open-angle glaucoma, acute mountain sickness prophylaxis |
| **Osmotic Diuretics** | Mannitol (IV only) | Luminal hyperosmolality in PCT and Descending Limb | High (osmotic drag) | Transient hypernatremia followed by hyponatremia | Acute intracranial hypertension, acute angle-closure glaucoma |

⚡ **High-Yield FMGE Rule:** Loop diuretics cause hypocalcemia by collapsing the lumen-positive potential in the thick ascending limb, making them useful in treating severe acute hypercalcemia. In contrast, thiazides increase distal calcium reabsorption, producing hypercalcemia, making them useful in reducing recurrent calcium oxalate kidney stones.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Systematic preparation for the National Board of Examinations screening test.

#### 1. Detailed Pharmacodynamics of Diuretics

1. **Loop Diuretics (High-Ceiling Diuretics):**
   - **Mechanism:** Inhibit the $Na^+/K^+/2Cl^-$ cotransporter (NKCC2) on the luminal membrane of the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle.
   - **Hemodynamic Effect:** Intravenous furosemide stimulates intrarenal prostaglandin synthesis within five minutes, increasing renal venous capacitance and reducing left ventricular filling pressures before diuresis begins. Co-administration of NSAIDs blunts this response.
   - **Adverse Effects:** Ototoxicity (highest with ethacrynic acid, synergistic with aminoglycosides), hyperuricemia (competes with uric acid for organic anion transporters in proximal tubule), and hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis.
2. **Thiazide Diuretics:**
   - **Mechanism:** Inhibit the electroneutral $Na^+/Cl^-$ cotransporter (NCC) in the early distal convoluted tubule.
   - **Metabolic Effects (The Four Hypers):** Hyperuricemia, Hypercalcemia, Hyperglycemia (due to inhibition of insulin release from pancreatic beta cells via potassium-channel hyperpolarization), and Hyperlipidemia.
   - **Efficacy Limitation:** Thiazides lose natriuretic efficacy when estimated GFR falls below 30 mL/min, with the notable exception of metolazone, which retains activity in advanced chronic kidney disease.

## Respiratory Pharmacology and Asthma Therapeutics

#### 1. Bronchodilators and Anti-Inflammatory Agents

Medical management of obstructive airway diseases (asthma and COPD) targets bronchoconstriction and airway inflammation through distinct receptor families:

| Drug Class | Examples | Mechanism of Action | Clinical Role in Asthma / COPD | Distinct Adverse Reactions |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Short-Acting Beta-2 Agonists (SABA)** | Salbutamol (Albuterol), Levalbuterol | $G_s$ protein activation elevates intracellular cAMP, relaxing bronchial smooth muscle | Rapid rescue for acute bronchospasm (onset 3-5 min, duration 4-6 h) | Muscle tremor, sinus tachycardia, transient hypokalemia |
| **Long-Acting Beta-2 Agonists (LABA)** | Salmeterol, Formoterol, Vilanterol | Highly lipophilic beta-2 agonists with sustained 12- to 24-hour bronchodilation | Maintenance therapy; must always be combined with an inhaled steroid in asthma | Tachyphylaxis, palpitations, boxed warning against monotherapy |
| **Inhaled Corticosteroids (ICS)** | Fluticasone, Budesonide, Beclomethasone | Nuclear receptor binding suppresses transcription of inflammatory cytokines (IL-4, IL-5) | Primary first-line anti-inflammatory controller for persistent asthma | Oropharyngeal candidiasis, dysphonia; preventable by using spacers and mouth rinsing |
| **Short-Acting Anticholinergics (SAMA)** | Ipratropium bromide | Blocks muscarinic M3 receptors, preventing vagally mediated bronchoconstriction | Add-on rescue in acute severe asthma; first-line bronchodilator in acute COPD | Dry mouth, bitter metallic taste, urinary retention |
| **Long-Acting Anticholinergics (LAMA)** | Tiotropium, Glycopyrronium, Umeclidinium | Dissociates slowly from M3 receptors, providing 24-hour bronchodilation | Preferred maintenance therapy in COPD; add-on in severe persistent asthma | Dry mouth, urinary hesitancy in prostatic hyperplasia |
| **Leukotriene Receptor Antagonists** | Montelukast, Zafirlukast | Competitive antagonists at CysLT1 receptor, blocking LTC4, LTD4, and LTE4 | Prophylaxis for aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease and exercise-induced asthma | Neuropsychiatric changes (sleep disturbances, agitation), Churg-Strauss syndrome |
| **Methylxanthines** | Theophylline, Aminophylline | Non-selective phosphodiesterase (PDE3/4) inhibition and adenosine receptor blockade | Add-on maintenance in refractory asthma and COPD; narrow therapeutic index (10-20 mcg/mL) | Nausea, vomiting, multifocal atrial tachycardia, refractory seizures |

## Worked Clinical Calculations and Exam Scenarios

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Quantitative pharmacokinetics, clinical vignettes, and therapeutic drug monitoring.

#### 1. Theophylline Therapeutic Drug Monitoring Calculation

Theophylline exhibits a narrow therapeutic window (10 to 20 mcg/mL or mg/L). Concentrations exceeding 20 mg/L cause cardiac dysrhythmias and generalized seizures.

The steady-state serum concentration ($C_{ss}$) is calculated by:

$$C_{ss} = \frac{F \cdot \text{Dose}}{Cl \cdot \tau}$$

##### Worked Quantitative Problem

**Clinical Scenario:**  
A 48-year-old chronic asthmatic male weighing 60 kg receives oral theophylline at 300 mg every 12 hours ($F = 1.0$, $\tau = 12\text{ h}$). His baseline clearance is $2.5\text{ L/h}$.

Calculate baseline steady-state concentration:

$$C_{ss} = \frac{1.0 \times 300\text{ mg}}{2.5\text{ L/h} \times 12\text{ h}} = \frac{300}{30} = 10.0\text{ mg/L}$$

This concentration is therapeutic. The patient then contracts bacterial pneumonia and is prescribed oral erythromycin (500 mg QID). Erythromycin inhibits hepatic CYP1A2 and CYP3A4, reducing theophylline clearance by $40\%$ to $1.5\text{ L/h}$.

Calculate the new steady-state concentration:

$$C_{ss\text{ (new)}} = \frac{1.0 \times 300\text{ mg}}{1.5\text{ L/h} \times 12\text{ h}} = \frac{300}{18} = 16.67\text{ mg/L}$$

If the clearance drops by $60\%$ to $1.0\text{ L/h}$ (as seen with ciprofloxacin):

$$C_{ss\text{ (toxic)}} = \frac{1.0 \times 300\text{ mg}}{1.0\text{ L/h} \times 12\text{ h}} = \frac{300}{12} = 25.0\text{ mg/L}$$

**Clinical Interpretation:**  
A level of 25.0 mg/L exceeds the 20 mg/L toxic threshold. The patient requires therapeutic drug monitoring and a 50% dose reduction when starting macrolides or fluoroquinolones.

#### 2. High-Frequency FMGE Clinical Vignettes

1. **Aspirin-Exacerbated Respiratory Disease (Samter's Triad):** Consists of bronchial asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, and acute bronchospasm upon ingestion of aspirin or other non-selective COX inhibitors. Shunting of arachidonic acid down the 5-lipoxygenase pathway leads to overproduction of cysteinyl leukotrienes. Montelukast (CysLT1 blocker) or Zileuton (5-lipoxygenase inhibitor) represents targeted therapy.
2. **Spironolactone Gynecomastia vs Eplerenone:** Spironolactone is a non-selective mineralocorticoid antagonist that also blocks androgen receptors and inhibits 17-alpha-hydroxylase, precipitating painful gynecomastia and erectile dysfunction in up to 10% of male patients. Eplerenone is a selective mineralocorticoid receptor blocker with negligible anti-androgenic affinity, resolving gynecomastia while maintaining cardioprotective benefits.
