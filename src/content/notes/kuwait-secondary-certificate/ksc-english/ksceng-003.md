# Unit 9 — New Ways and Old — Kuwait Secondary Certificate English Notes

Unit 9 of the Kuwait MOE Grade 12 "Over To You" textbook (second term, 1 March to 26 March 2026) is "New Ways and Old" — a thematic unit on changing approaches to work, life and the comparison between old and new methods. The listening input is three conversations about different ways of doing things. The reading input is a biography about a Kuwaiti female figure. The unit's grammar focus is causative verbs across present, past and future (have/get something done). Vocabulary covers suffixes and sports idioms. The writing focus is planning and writing a biography. Speaking focus is comparing the past and present and giving biographical information.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Causative structure:** have/get + object + past participle. Example: "I had my hair cut." / "I got my car repaired."
- **Tense combinations:** "I am having my hair cut tomorrow." / "I had my hair cut yesterday." / "I will have my hair cut next week."
- **Theme vocabulary:** tradition, heritage, modern, contemporary, legacy, ancestor, customs, innovation, technology, generational, change.
- **Sports idioms:** "level playing field," "a game changer," "the ball is in your court," "off the bench," "fair play," "in good form," "a slam dunk," "jump the gun."
- **Biography structure:** early life, education, career milestones, achievements, legacy, conclusion.
- **Tense comparisons:** present perfect (have/has done) vs past simple (did); present perfect continuous (have/has been doing) vs present continuous (am/is/are doing).

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing active and passive in the causative — "I cut my hair" (active, you did it yourself) vs "I had my hair cut" (passive, someone did it for you).
- Using "make" instead of "have/get" for services — "I made my hair cut" is wrong; it is "I had my hair cut".
- Confusing "raise" (bring up, lift) with "rise" (go up) — "temperatures rise" (intransitive), "raise the temperature" (transitive).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Causative verbs

Causative constructions describe having something done by someone else. The two forms are "have + object + past participle" (more common in British English) and "get + object + past participle" (more common in American English, more informal).

Tense usage:

- Present simple: "I have my hair cut every month."
- Present continuous: "I am having my hair cut right now."
- Past simple: "I had my hair cut yesterday."
- Future simple: "I will have my hair cut tomorrow."
- Modal: "I should have my car serviced soon."
- Perfect: "I have had my hair cut."

The agent (the person doing the work) can be mentioned with "by": "I had my hair cut by Maria." When the agent is obvious or unimportant, omit it.

#### Suffixes

Suffixes change the part of speech and the meaning of a base word. Common suffixes:

- -tion/-sion: create → creation, decide → decision, expand → expansion, confuse → confusion.
- -ment: develop → development, move → movement, achieve → achievement.
- -ity: similar → similarity, popular → popularity.
- -al: nature → natural, culture → cultural.
- -ive: act → active, create → creative.
- -ful: peace → peaceful, harm → harmful.
- -less: care → careless, harm → harmless.
- -ous: fame → famous, danger → dangerous.

Note that not all base + suffix combinations are valid: "beautiful" exists but "beautyful" does not. Memorise the established forms.

#### Sports idioms

Sports idioms work in conversational English, business writing, journalism and most narrative prose. They are inappropriate in formal academic writing.

Common ones:

- "Level playing field" — equal conditions for all competitors.
- "Game changer" — an innovation or event that alters the situation fundamentally.
- "The ball is in your court" — it's your turn to act.
- "Off the bench" — out of reserve, into active participation.
- "Fair play" — just treatment.
- "In good form" — performing well.
- "Slam dunk" — a guaranteed success; (basketball: a high-percentage close-range shot).
- "Jump the gun" — act too soon, before the signal.

#### Biography writing

A biography is a non-fiction account of someone's life written by another person. The structure:

1. **Hook** — a striking detail, achievement, or quote that draws the reader in.
2. **Early life and education** — born, raised, schooling, formative influences.
3. **Career milestones** — the major achievements, in chronological order, with dates and specifics.
4. **Challenges and setbacks** — difficulties faced, and how they were overcome.
5. **Legacy** — lasting impact, why this person matters.
6. **Conclusion** — a forward-looking or reflective ending.

Use past simple for events at specific times ("She was born in 1950"), past continuous for background ("She was studying at the time"), and present perfect for ongoing relevance ("She has inspired generations"). Avoid present simple for biographical events.

#### Comparing past and present

Use a range of comparison structures:

- "In the past, X was common, but today it is rare."
- "Compared to the past, X has changed dramatically."
- "While X used to be Y, today it is Z."
- "Whereas X was once Y, it is now Z."

For the language function expression of comparing: "Whereas," "Compared to," "In contrast," "On the other hand," "Similarly," "Likewise," "Just as," "Unlike."

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Where Unit 9 appears across the terminal English paper

- **Reading comprehension:** a biographical text or report on changing customs, with vocabulary items drawn from the unit.
- **Grammar items:** causative transformations (active → causative, or tense changes).
- **Writing item:** a biographical writing prompt, possibly about a Kuwaiti or Arab figure.
- **Vocabulary items:** sports idioms in context.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "The causative is just another passive." No — the causative means "I arranged for someone to do it", not "something was done to me". "I had my wallet stolen" is both passive and causative; "I had my car washed" is causative but not really passive.
- "All sports idioms work in any context." No — they are informal. In a CV or formal letter, prefer literal language.
- "Biography uses present tense throughout." No — biography uses past simple for past events and present perfect for ongoing relevance.

#### Exam technique

- For causative transformations, identify the agent and the action, then build "have + object + past participle" — the past participle is the method mark.
- For biography writing, plan the timeline first — list the key dates and events before writing paragraphs.
- For sports-idiom items, read the full context — the idiom that fits is usually determined by the surrounding meaning.
