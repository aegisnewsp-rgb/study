# Motion in 1D

Motion in one dimension is the study of how a position on a single straight axis changes with time. Every problem here reduces to four quantities — position x, velocity v, acceleration a and time t — connected by two differentiators, v = dx/dt and a = dv/dt. Learn the graph reading and the three uniformly-accelerated equations and the chapter is finished; the only place students lose marks is sign convention.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

**The vocabulary, with what each one can and cannot do:**

| Quantity | Symbol | Type | Definition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Position | x | vector | coordinate on the axis, with a chosen origin |
| Displacement | Δx = x₂ − x₁ | vector | change in position, signed |
| Distance | — | scalar | total path length, never negative |
| Average velocity | Δx/Δt | vector | displacement over time |
| Instantaneous velocity | v = dx/dt | vector | slope of the x–t graph |
| Average acceleration | Δv/Δt | vector | change in velocity over time |
| Instantaneous acceleration | a = dv/dt | vector | slope of the v–t graph |

**The three equations of uniformly accelerated motion.** Valid only when a is constant:

- v = u + at
- s = ut + ½at²
- v² = u² + 2as

u is initial velocity, v final velocity, s displacement, a constant acceleration, t time. Use the first when t is known or wanted, the second when s and t are wanted, the third when t is absent. Average velocity over such an interval is (u + v)/2, which saves a step.

**Free fall.** a = g = 9.8 m s⁻². If upward is positive, g = −9.8 m s⁻²; if downward is positive, g = +9.8. Pick one and stay with it. Time to fall from rest through height h is t = √(2h/g); impact speed is v = √(2gh).

**Graph rules — worth more marks than the equations:**

- Slope of x–t graph = velocity
- Area under v–t graph = displacement
- Slope of v–t graph = acceleration
- Curvature of the x–t graph = acceleration

**Relative velocity in 1D:** v_AB = v_A − v_B. Two cars at 60 km h⁻¹ driving toward each other approach at 120 km h⁻¹, because in one car's frame the other's velocity is −60.

**n-th second formula:** displacement during the n-th second is sₙ = u + (a/2)(2n − 1). It is a special case of s = ut + ½at², not a new law.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

**Distance and displacement.** Distance is the length of the path actually travelled; it is a scalar, so it can only be positive, and adding it always increases the total. Displacement is the straight-line vector from initial to final position. Throw a ball straight up to a height of 20 m and let it fall back to the thrower's hand: distance = 40 m, displacement = 0. Drive a car round a circular track of radius R at constant speed: distance = 2πR, displacement = 0 after one lap, and the *average* velocity over the lap is zero even though the speedometer never reads zero. The two agree only for motion that is entirely in one direction with no reversal.

**Average and instantaneous.** Average velocity is Δx/Δt over a finite interval. Instantaneous velocity is the limit of that quotient as the interval shrinks to zero, written v = dx/dt. An average equals an instantaneous value only when the quantity is genuinely constant over the interval — for uniform acceleration, the average velocity (u + v)/2 equals the instantaneous velocity at the mid-time t/2, not at t. Instantaneous acceleration is a = dv/dt; when acceleration is non-zero, the velocity of an object at rest at an instant is zero, but the acceleration at that instant need not be. A ball at the top of its flight has v = 0 and a = −9.8 m s⁻². Conflating "at rest" with "not accelerating" is the most expensive single mistake in this topic.

**The three equations and how to pick one.** They come from integrating a = constant: first multiply by dt and integrate to get v = u + at; multiply that by dt again to get s = ut + ½at²; eliminate t between the two to get v² = u² + 2as. A useful practical rule: if the problem gives you t or asks for t, use v = u + at. If it asks for s with t given, use s = ut + ½at². If t is nowhere in sight, use v² = u² + 2as. If a is not constant, none of the three is available — go to v dv = a dx or to energy methods in the Work, Energy and Power chapter.

**The n-th second.** For uniform acceleration from rest, the distance in the n-th second is sₙ₋ₙ₊₁ = na − ½a², and for a general u it is sₙₜₕ = u + (a/2)(2n − 1). Derive it in one line if you forget it: displacement in n seconds minus displacement in (n − 1) seconds is ut + ½at² evaluated twice, and the difference collapses to u + a(n − ½) = u + (a/2)(2n − 1).

**Reading the graphs properly.**

- **x–t graph.** A straight line means constant velocity; a horizontal line means the body is at rest; a curve opening upward means positive acceleration, curving downward means negative. The steepness of a section is how fast the body is going there, so a graph that steepens means speeding up. A graph that flattens means slowing down. Two points at the same height on the graph are at the same position, not necessarily at rest — a body at the apex of its path is momentarily stationary while a is non-zero.
- **v–t graph.** The slope is a. A straight sloping line is constant acceleration; a horizontal line is constant velocity; a curve means a is changing. The area under the graph between two times is the displacement, counted with sign. If the graph crosses the axis, the body reverses direction, and the area below the axis must be subtracted. A body returns to its start exactly when the net signed area is zero.
- **Sign of area.** Area above the axis is forward displacement; area below the axis is backward. Adding them with the right sign is the only way to answer a "total distance versus net displacement" question.

**Free-fall symmetry — the facts worth memorising.** Thrown up with speed u in gravity g, ignoring air resistance: time to apex u/g, maximum height u²/2g, total time of flight 2u/g, impact speed equal and opposite to the launch speed, and total distance travelled 2 × height = u²/g. A stone dropped from height h hits the ground at √(2gh) and takes √(2h/g). Two objects released together reach the ground together regardless of mass, which is the content of the equivalence principle that later chapters use for a non-inertial frame.

**Relative velocity with sign discipline.** Fix one axis and write each velocity with a sign before subtracting. If A moves right at 10 m s⁻¹ and B moves right at 4 m s⁻¹, A pulls away from B at 6 m s⁻¹. If B moves left at 4 m s⁻¹ while A moves right at 10, they close at 14 m s⁻¹. If both move right and B is faster, A falls behind at 6 m s⁻¹ even though the magnitudes are the same — this is the case where "faster" and "approaching" come apart, and it is exactly what a sign mistake breaks.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

**Where the three equations stop working.** They rest on a = constant. If a depends on position, for example the drag force on a falling object, write v dv = a dx and integrate. If a depends on velocity, the simple separation also works, giving the standard result that a body falling with quadratic drag approaches a terminal speed v_t = √(mg/k) for F_drag = kv². If a depends on time, integrate a(t) first to get v(t), then integrate again. The general recipe is: a = dv/dt = d²x/dt², so integrate twice, and the constant-of-integration is fixed by the initial conditions. Students who memorise the three equations and reach for them regardless of the actual acceleration lose most of the harder questions in this topic.

**Instantaneous velocity as a limit — worked.** A body moves so that x = 4t³ − 2t + 1 (SI units). v = dx/dt = 12t² − 2, so at t = 1 s, v = 10 m s⁻¹. a = 24t, so at t = 1 s, a = 24 m s⁻². Displacement from t = 1 s to t = 2 s is x(2) − x(1) = (32 − 4 + 1) − (4 − 2 + 1) = 29 − 3 = 26 m. On the x–t graph this is a cubic that steepens continuously, so the body accelerates throughout — the graph alone tells you the sign of a without any calculation.

**Area under a v–t graph that changes sign — worked.** A body has v(t) = 4 − 2t in m s⁻¹. From t = 0 to t = 3 s, displacement = ∫(4 − 2t)dt from 0 to 3 = [4t − t²] from 0 to 3 = 12 − 9 = 3 m. The graph crosses v = 0 at t = 2 s. Forward displacement (0 to 2 s) = area of a triangle with base 2 s and height 4 m s⁻¹ = 4 m. Backward displacement (2 to 3 s) = area of a triangle with base 1 s and height 2 m s⁻¹ = 1 m. Total distance = 4 + 1 = 5 m, while net displacement = 5 − 1... recompute: net = 4 − 1 = 3 m, which agrees with the integral. Distance 5 m, displacement 3 m — the two differ because the body reversed at t = 2 s. Reporting displacement as the area without the sign is the classic error.

**Stopping distance.** A vehicle braking with constant deceleration a reaches from speed v to rest over s = v²/(2a). Doubling the speed quadruples the stopping distance. With a = μg on a level road, s = v²/(2μg). This is the entire physics of the braking-distance rule used in road-safety training, and it is a favourite numerical because the time is irrelevant. Worked: a car at 20 m s⁻¹ braking with a = 5 m s⁻² stops in 400/10 = 40 m and takes 4 s.

**Velocity as a function of position.** When a depends on v rather than t, v dv = a dx. Two consequences. First, if a = −kv with a = F/m, you get exponential decay of velocity, not a straight line on a v–t graph. Second, the chain rule v = dx/dt and a = dv/dt combine into a = v dv/dx, which is the cleanest route to "find v as a function of x" questions. A ball falling from rest under gravity is the standard case: g = v dv/dx gives v² = 2gx starting from x = 0, and a body launched upward with u obeys v² = u² − 2gx until it stops at x = u²/2g.

**The average-velocity identity and why it fails.** For uniform acceleration, v_avg = (u + v)/2 and it also equals the instantaneous velocity at the midpoint in time. Both facts collapse for non-uniform acceleration. A body that accelerates for the first half of an interval and coasts for the second half has a much higher average velocity than (u + v)/2 suggests, because the high speeds are weighted more. A v–t graph that is concave upward — a car pulling away in traffic — has an average above the mid-interval value; a concave-down graph, a car braking, has it below.

**Integrating when the graph is not a straight line.** When a is constant the v–t graph is a straight line and the area is a triangle or trapezoid. When a is not constant, read the area from the curved graph, or integrate the v(t) expression. Do not apply the trapezoid formula to a curve; that is a common silent error worth several marks.

**Motion under non-constant acceleration — worked with terminal velocity.** A body of mass m falls with drag F = kv² opposing motion, so m dv/dt = mg − kv². Terminal speed is reached when mg = kv_t², i.e. v_t = √(mg/k). It is approached asymptotically and never exceeded. On a v–t graph this produces a curve that rises and flattens, so a = dv/dt decreases steadily — the exact opposite of the straight line the three equations assume. This single example separates the students who understand the chapter from those who have memorised it.

**Linking forward.** Motion in 1D is the scaffold for three later chapters. Motion in a Plane needs the same graph reading with two axes. Laws of Motion supplies the force that makes a non-zero, and Newton's second law a = F/m is the reason a is constant in most textbook problems. Work, Energy and Power removes time entirely: W = ½mv² − ½mu² is the integral of F dx over the same interval, so a "no time given" problem can often be finished without ever finding t.

```mermaid
    A --> G[Is a graph involved]
    G --> H[x vs t] --> I[Slope is velocity, curvature is acceleration]
    G --> J[v vs t] --> K[Slope is acceleration, signed area is displacement]
    G --> L[Area above axis is forward, below axis is backward]
    A --> M[Two objects asked about]
    M --> N[Fix one axis, sign every velocity, then v_AB = v_A - v_B]
```

- **"Slope, area, slope."** x–t: slope = v. v–t: area = s, slope = a. The triplet is worth memorising as a rhythm.
- **"v = 0 is not a = 0."** The top of a throw: velocity zero, acceleration −9.8.
- **"Same position, not same rest."** Two points at equal height on an x–t graph can be a body at the apex and a body genuinely parked.
- **"Time given → first equation. Time absent → third equation."**
- **"Double the speed, quadruple the stopping distance."** v² = u² + 2as with u = 0 says s ∝ v².
- **"Sign everything before you subtract."** v_AB = v_A − v_B fails in exactly the way you failed to sign.
- **"Distance accumulates, displacement cancels."** Reversal is the only thing that separates them.
- **Flashcard Q&A:**
  - *Average velocity of a uniformly accelerated body?* → (u + v)/2, and equal to the instantaneous velocity at t/2.
  - *Displacement in the n-th second?* → u + (a/2)(2n − 1).
  - *Ball thrown up at 20 m/s, time to apex?* → 20/9.8 ≈ 2.04 s.
  - *Same ball, total distance before return?* → u²/g = 400/9.8 ≈ 40.8 m.
  - *What does a horizontal v–t line mean?* → constant velocity, a = 0, and displacement grows linearly.
  - *Body returns to start — net area?* → zero. Positive area equals negative area.
  - *A = 0 but v ≠ 0?* → uniform velocity, e.g. a puck on frictionless ice.

### 🎯 Exam Traps & Error Log

1. **Mixing the sign convention for g.** Writing g = +9.8 m s⁻² while treating upward as positive produces a ball that never comes down. Decide the axis first, then write g with its sign, then never touch it again.
2. **Using the three equations when a is not constant.** They are derived from a = constant. If the problem gives a spring force, a drag force or a variable force, integrate instead.
3. **Reporting distance when the question asks for displacement,** or averaging their magnitudes. In a throw-and-return, displacement over the whole flight is zero.
4. **Reading the area under a v–t graph as unsigned.** The part below the axis is backward motion and must be subtracted.
5. **Calling a horizontal x–t segment "constant velocity."** Horizontal on x–t means v = 0; horizontal on v–t means v is constant. The two graphs look the same and mean opposite things.
6. **Confusing average speed with the magnitude of average velocity.** Average speed = total distance/total time and can be large when the body reverses; |displacement|/t cannot exceed average speed.
7. **Dropping the sign in v_AB.** Two objects moving in the same direction can be closing or separating depending on which is faster — magnitude alone never answers it.
8. **Solving a projectile-style question with 1D equations.** A trajectory with a horizontal and a vertical component is Motion in 2D, and 1D equations apply to each axis separately with its own sign convention.
9. **Forgetting that the equations give displacement, not distance,** in a "total distance covered" question with a reversal in the middle.
10. **Assuming a = 0 whenever the graph is a straight line.** A straight line on v–t does mean a = 0, but a straight line on x–t means constant *velocity*, and a straight line on a v–x plot means constant acceleration. Check which axes are drawn.

### 🧪 Self-Test — 8 Questions with Worked Answers

Attempt each on paper first. Every one uses a form this chapter actually uses.

1. **A body moves with $x = 4t^{3} - 2t + 1$ (SI units). Find its velocity and acceleration at $t = 2$ s, and its average velocity over the first 2 s.**
   v = dx/dt = 12t² − 2, so at t = 2, v = 48 − 2 = 46 m s⁻¹. a = dv/dt = 24t, so a = 48 m s⁻². Average velocity = [x(2) − x(0)]/2 = [(32 − 4 + 1) − 1]/2 = 28/2 = 14 m s⁻¹. Note the average is nowhere near the instantaneous value at t = 2, because the acceleration is large and rising.
2. **A train travelling at 72 km h⁻¹ brakes to rest over 50 m. Find the deceleration and the stopping time, and check the two equations agree.**
   u = 72/3.6 = 20 m s⁻¹, v = 0, s = 50. From v² = u² + 2as: 0 = 400 + 100a, so a = −4 m s⁻². From v = u + at: 0 = 20 − 4t, so t = 5 s. Check with s = ut + ½at² = 20(5) + ½(−4)(25) = 100 − 50 = 50 m. All three agree; if one does not, the error is arithmetic, not physics.
3. **A ball is thrown vertically upward at 49 m s⁻¹. Find the maximum height, the time to reach it, and the time to return to the thrower's hand.**
   t = u/g = 49/9.8 = 5 s. Height = u²/2g = 2401/19.6 = 122.5 m. Return time = 2 × 5 = 10 s. Impact speed = −49 m s⁻¹, equal and opposite to launch. Total distance = 245 m while displacement over the flight is 0.
4. **From the $v$–$t$ graph of $v(t) = 4 - 2t$ (SI), find the displacement from $t=0$ to $t=3$ s, the total distance, and the time of reversal.**
   Graph is a straight line from v = 4 at t = 0, crossing zero at t = 2 s, reaching −2 at t = 3 s. Displacement = area = ½(2)(4) − ½(1)(2) = 4 − 1 = 3 m. Total distance = 4 + 1 = 5 m. Reversal at t = 2 s. Confirm by integration: [4t − t²] from 0 to 3 = 12 − 9 = 3 m. The commonest wrong answer here is 5 m, which is the distance, not the displacement.
5. **Two cars A and B move in a straight line. A: 40 km h⁻¹ east. B: 60 km h⁻¹ west. What is the velocity of A relative to B?**
   Take east as positive. v_A = +40, v_B = −60. v_AB = v_A − v_B = 40 − (−60) = 100 km h⁻¹ east. They close at 100 km h⁻¹, which is the sum of the speeds, because they travel in opposite directions. The error to avoid is writing 40 − 60 = −20 and concluding A is slower, which happens only if you forget B's sign.
6. **A body has velocity $v = 6t^{2} - 4t$ (SI). Find the distance travelled between $t = 1$ s and $t = 2$ s, and its acceleration at $t = 1.5$ s.**
   v(1) = 6 − 4 = 2 m s⁻¹; v(2) = 24 − 8 = 16 m s⁻¹. Velocity is positive throughout the interval, so there is no reversal and distance = displacement. s = ∫(6t² − 4t)dt from 1 to 2 = [2t³ − 2t²] from 1 to 2 = (16 − 8) − (2 − 2) = 8 m. a = 12t − 4 = 18 − 4 = 14 m s⁻² at t = 1.5 s. Always check the sign of v before you treat an integral as a distance.
7. **An object is dropped from rest from a height of 45 m. Take g = 10 m s⁻². Find the time to reach the ground and the impact speed, and state the sign of each.**
   With downward positive: s = ½at² gives 45 = 5t², so t = 3 s. v = at = 30 m s⁻¹, positive, i.e. downward. Mass is irrelevant — that is the point of the result. With upward positive instead, s = −45, a = −10, t = 3 s and v = −30 m s⁻¹, meaning downward. Either convention gives the same physical answer; mixing them does not.
8. **A car accelerates uniformly from rest to 25 m s⁻¹ in 5 s. How far does it travel in the first 2 s, and in the 5th second?**
   a = (25 − 0)/5 = 5 m s⁻². First 2 s: s = ½at² = ½(5)(4) = 10 m. The 5th second: s₅ = u + (a/2)(2·5 − 1) = 0 + 2.5 × 9 = 22.5 m. Check: distance in the 5th second should exceed the average per-second distance; total after 5 s is ½(5)(25) = 62.5 m, and the last second alone accounts for 22.5 m of that, which is what speeding up looks like.

### 💡 Pro Tips

1. **Write the sign convention on the question paper before you write anything else.** One line: "up positive, g = −9.8". It removes the largest single source of error in the chapter.
2. **Use the graph first, the equation second.** A rough sketch of the v–t graph tells you the sign of a, whether the body reverses, and the approximate answer, in fifteen seconds. Then compute.
3. **Count the area with a ruler-free method.** For a straight-line v–t graph, split it into triangles and rectangles rather than reaching for a trapezoid formula.
4. **When a is not constant, switch method immediately.** v dv = a dx for position-dependent acceleration, or go to energy. Do not apply the three equations and hope.
5. **Sanity-check free-fall numbers.** A stone dropped from 45 m in 3 s is right; the same stone in 30 s is not. Order-of-magnitude checks catch factor-of-ten slips instantly.
6. **Memorise u/g, u²/2g and 2u/g together** as the apex time, apex height and total flight time. One line of recall, three numbers.
7. **In every relative-velocity question, write both velocities with signs on the same axis before subtracting.** That one habit prevents every error in this sub-topic.
8. **Practise the n-th second formula by deriving it once.** Two lines of algebra, and it stops being something you look up.
9. **When a graph is given without an equation, answer with the graph, not with the equations.** The question is testing reading, and substituting a formula you invented for the shape is how you lose it.
10. **Keep a one-page sheet with three graphs** — a straight sloping x–t line, a parabolic x–t curve, and a v–t line crossing the axis — each captioned in one line. Two minutes of revision with that sheet fixes most of this chapter.
