# Rotational Motion

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Rotational motion** describes a rigid body turning about a fixed axis; every particle traces a circle of radius equal to its distance from that axis. The three kinematic variables mirror linear motion: **angular displacement θ** (rad), **angular velocity ω = dθ/dt** (rad/s), and **angular acceleration α = dω/dt** (rad/s²). Dynamics rests on **torque τ = rF sinθ** (the rotational analogue of force) and **moment of inertia I = Σ mᵢrᵢ²** (the rotational analogue of mass). Newton's second law becomes **τ_net = Iα**, and angular momentum is **L = Iω**. Rolling combines translation and spin: **KE_total = ½Mv²_cm + ½I_cmω²**. For CUET UG: memorise standard I values (ring MR², solid sphere ⅖MR², disc ½MR²) and the **parallel-axis theorem I = I_cm + Md²** — these appear in roughly one out of every three rotational questions.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Rigid Body and the Centre of Mass
A **rigid body** preserves the distance between any pair of its particles. When no axis is fixed externally, rotation occurs about the **centre of mass (CM)**. Locating the CM is the first step before applying torque equations.

#### Moment of Inertia — The Rotational Mass
I = Σ mᵢrᵢ² measures how mass is distributed relative to the rotation axis. The same body can have different I values for different axes, which is why a spinning skater tucks her arms in (mass moves closer to axis, I drops, ω rises since L is conserved). The **radius of gyration k** is defined by I = Mk², so k is a *characteristic length*, not the actual radius of the body.

#### Standard Moments of Inertia (must memorise)
| Body | Axis | I |
|---|---|---|
| Thin ring | Through centre, perpendicular to plane | MR² |
| Solid disc / solid cylinder | Through centre, perpendicular to plane | ½MR² |
| Solid sphere | Through centre | ⅖MR² |
| Hollow sphere (thin shell) | Through centre | ⅔MR² |
| Uniform rod | Through centre, perpendicular to length | ML²/12 |
| Uniform rod | Through one end | ML²/3 |

#### Two Theorems That Save Time
- **Parallel-axis theorem:** I = I_cm + Md², where d is the distance between a parallel axis and the CM axis. This is why a rod about its end is ML²/12 + M(L/2)² = ML²/3.
- **Perpendicular-axis theorem (planar lamina only):** I_z = I_x + I_y. It does NOT apply to 3D solids.

#### Torque, Angular Momentum, and Equilibrium
**Torque** is the vector τ = r × F, magnitude τ = rF sinθ. The rotational form of Newton's second law is **τ_net = Iα**, equivalent to τ_net = dL/dt where L = Iω. **Equilibrium** of a rigid body requires BOTH ΣF = 0 (translational) AND Στ = 0 (rotational) about any point. Assign clockwise and anticlockwise torques opposite signs — sign errors are the most common reason students lose marks on seesaw/beam problems.

#### Rotational Kinematics
ω = ω₀ + αt, θ = ω₀t + ½αt², ω² = ω₀² + 2αθ — identical in form to linear kinematics, with θ in place of x, ω in place of v, and α in place of a.

#### Rolling Without Slipping
A wheel of radius R rolling on a flat surface has v_cm = Rω, and its total kinetic energy is ½Mv²_cm + ½I_cmω². For a solid sphere this gives KE = (7/10)Mv².

#### CUET Question Patterns
- Numerical: compute I using parallel-axis theorem, then find α from a given τ.
- Conceptual: "Which has the largest I about its symmetry axis?" — solid or hollow sphere of equal mass and radius.
- Assertion-Reason: distinguish τ = Iα from F = ma, or test whether angular momentum is conserved when a skater extends her arms.
- Equilibrium: find unknown mass/position on a lever using Στ = 0.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Why I Depends on Axis — A Deeper Look
Moment of inertia is a **tensor** in general, but for symmetric bodies about symmetry axes it reduces to a scalar. The CM axis is special because it minimises I for a given orientation — any other parallel axis gives a larger I by Md². This is why the parallel-axis theorem can never reduce I below I_cm.

#### Angular Momentum as a Vector
L = Iω points along the axis by the right-hand rule for symmetric bodies. For a single particle, L = r × p, and only the component of L along the rotation axis is preserved when the particle's path curves in a plane. For a rigid body, when **no net external torque** acts, L is conserved in both magnitude and direction — this is why a spinning gyroscope resists tilting.

#### Couple and Angular Impulse
A **couple** is two equal, opposite, parallel forces producing pure rotation (net force = 0, net torque ≠ 0). The **angular impulse** J = ∫τ dt = ΔL ties torque-time history to change in angular momentum, mirroring linear impulse-momentum theorem.

#### Work and Power in Rotation
Work done by torque over angular displacement θ is **W = τθ**, and instantaneous power is P = τω. The work-energy theorem in rotation reads W_net = ½Iω_f² − ½I_i², equivalent to the linear version after the substitutions m→I, v→ω.

#### Connections to Other CUET Topics
- **Gravitation:** orbital angular momentum L = mvr uses the same L = r × p form.
- **Work, Energy, Power:** the ½Iω² expression is tested alongside ½mv² in rolling problems.
- **Newton's Laws:** tension in a string wrapped around a pulley of moment of inertia I satisfies τ = TR = Iα, linking linear and rotational dynamics in the same problem.

#### Common Mistakes
- Writing L = mωr for a rigid body (use L = Iω; the mωr form is only for a point particle).
- Using the perpendicular-axis theorem for 3D objects like a solid cylinder.
- Forgetting the ½ in ½Iω²; equating KE of a hollow and solid sphere of equal M and R.
- Treating I as if it were mass when adding rotational and translational energies.

#### Worked Micro-Example
A solid cylinder (M = 4 kg, R = 0.2 m) is released from rest and rolls without slipping down a 3 m incline (θ = 30°). Using energy conservation: Mgh = ½Mv² + ½Iω² with I = ½MR² and v = Rω. So Mgh = ½Mv² + ¼Mv² = (3/4)Mv², giving v = √(4gh/3) = √(4·9.8·3·0.5/3) ≈ 4.43 m/s. Note the acceleration a = g sinθ/(1 + I/MR²) = (9.8·0.5)/(1 + 0.5) ≈ 3.27 m/s².

#### Practice Prompts
1. Three identical solid spheres roll down the same incline. Compare their final speeds and explain why mass and radius do not affect the outcome.
2. A turntable rotating freely has a person standing at its edge. Describe qualitatively — and justify quantitatively with angular momentum conservation — what happens to ω when the person walks toward the centre.
