# Autonomic Nervous System Drugs

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Autonomic Nervous System (ANS) drugs** modulate the sympathetic (adrenergic) and parasympathetic (cholinergic) divisions by acting on cholinergic receptors (muscarinic **M1–M5**, nicotinic **Nn/Nm**) and adrenergic receptors (**α1, α2, β1, β2, β3**).

- **Cholinergic division** = acetylcholine (ACh) at all preganglionic + postganglionic parasympathetic synapses.
- **Adrenergic division** = norepinephrine (NE) at most postganglionic sympathetic synapses; ACh innervates sweat glands and some skeletal-muscle vasculature.
- **Muscarinic** receptors are G-protein coupled: M1, M3, M5 → Gq (↑IP3/DAG/Ca²⁺); M2, M4 → Gi (↓cAMP).
- **Nicotinic** receptors are ligand-gated Na⁺/K⁺ channels: Nn at autonomic ganglia, Nm at the neuromuscular junction (NMJ).
- **Adrenergic** coupling: α1 (Gq, ↑IP3/Ca²⁺ — pressor), α2 (Gi, ↓cAMP — presynaptic brake), β1/β2/β3 (Gs, ↑cAMP — cardiac, bronchodilator, lipolysis).
- **NE blood-pressure effect:** α1 vasoconstriction raises MAP; at low doses, β2 vasodilation may drop diastolic pressure (the classic "epinephrine reversal" is seen after α-blockade).

| Drug class | Example | Mechanism | Key use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct muscarinic agonist | Bethanechol | M3 stimulation, AChE-resistant | Postoperative urinary retention |
| Reversible AChE inhibitor | Neostigmine, Pyridostigmine | ↑ACh at synapse | Myasthenia gravis |
| Irreversible AChE inhibitor | Organophosphates (malathion) | Covalent AChE binding | Insecticide poisoning |
| Muscarinic antagonist | Atropine, Glycopyrrolate | Blocks M1–M3 | Bradycardia, pre-anaesthetic |
| Non-depolarising NMJ blocker | Vecuronium, Pancuronium | Antagonises Nm | Surgical paralysis |
| Depolarising NMJ blocker | Succinylcholine (SCh) | Sustained Nm depolarisation | Rapid intubation |
| α1-antagonist | Prazosin, Tamsulosin | Blocks α1 | Hypertension, BPH |
| β1-selective blocker | Metoprolol, Atenolol | ↓cAMP in heart | Angina, post-MI, HF |
| Mixed α/β-blocker | Carvedilol, Labetalol | α1 + β block | Heart failure, hypertensive emergency |
| Direct β2-agonist | Salbutamol, Salmeterol | Bronchial smooth-muscle relaxation | Asthma, COPD |
| Indirect sympathomimetic | Tyramine, Amphetamine | Releases stored NE | Hypertensive crisis with MAOIs |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** *"Sympathetic = **S**tress — **S**peed, Sweating, Sugar up"* (β1 ↑HR, α1 vasoconstrict, β2 bronchodilate, M3 cut sweat). For receptor coupling: **"QISS & QISQ"** — **α1-Q** (Gq), **α2-I** (Gi), **β1-S** (Gs), **β2-S** (Gs), **β3-S** (Gs). Receptor location: **"α1: Aorta (vasoconstrict); α2: Autoreceptor; β1: Bathroom (heart); β2: Bronchi; β3: Belly fat."**

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Receptor Pharmacology at a Glance
Every ANS drug question reduces to three variables: **receptor**, **agonist vs antagonist**, and **location**. The table below maps coupling, location, and net effect — the framework most MCQs exploit.

| Receptor | G-protein | Second messenger | Key location | Agonist effect | Antagonist effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | Gq | ↑IP3/DAG/Ca²⁺ | CNS, gastric parietal | Cognition, gastric acid | Dry mouth, confusion |
| M2 | Gi | ↓cAMP | Heart SA/AV node | Bradycardia | Tachycardia (atropine) |
| M3 | Gq | ↑IP3/DAG/Ca²⁺ | Smooth muscle, glands | Contraction, secretion | Mydriasis, urinary retention |
| Nn | Ion channel | Na⁺/K⁺ flux | Autonomic ganglia | Ganglia stimulation | Ganglion blockade (trimethaphan) |
| Nm | Ion channel | Na⁺/K⁺ flux | Skeletal NMJ | Fasciculation → flaccid paralysis | Competitive paralysis |
| α1 | Gq | ↑IP3/Ca²⁺ | Vascular smooth muscle | Vasoconstriction | Orthostatic hypotension |
| α2 | Gi | ↓cAMP | Presynaptic terminals | ↓NE release | — (yohimbine rarely used) |
| β1 | Gs | ↑cAMP | Heart, juxtaglomerular | ↑HR, ↑contractility, ↑renin | Bradycardia, negative inotropy |
| β2 | Gs | ↑cAMP | Bronchi, uterus, vessels | Bronchodilation, uterine relaxation | Bronchospasm (non-selective β-blockers) |
| β3 | Gs | ↑cAMP | Adipose, detrusor | Lipolysis, bladder relaxation | Overactive bladder (mirabegron) |

#### Cholinergic Agents — Mechanism Traps
- **Bethanechol** carries a β-methyl group making it **resistant to acetylcholinesterase** — therefore useful when systemic AChE inhibition is unwanted. It is the drug of choice for **postoperative non-obstructive urinary retention** and **neurogenic atony of the bladder**.
- **Neostigmine** and **pyridostigmine** are **reversible AChE inhibitors** with quaternary ammonium structure (poor CNS penetration). Combined with **glycopyrrolate**, they reverse non-depolarising NMJ blockade without bradycardia.
- **Organophosphates** (malathion, parathion, sarin) phosphorylate the serine-OH at the AChE active site. **Pralidoxime (2-PAM)** must be given **before "aging"** (>24 h) to reactivate the enzyme; atropine treats muscarinic symptoms only.
- **Atropine kinetics:** t½ ≈ 2–4 h; at toxic levels hepatic glucuronidation is **saturated**, producing **zero-order elimination** — important for poison management timelines.

#### Adrenergic Agents — Clinical Pearls
- **Phenylephrine** is a **direct α1-agonist** (raises BP reflexively slows HR), unlike **prazosin** which is an **α1-antagonist** (lowers BP). NEET PG regularly frames these as paired options.
- **Clonidine** and **methyldopa** are **centrally acting α2-agonists** (↓ sympathetic outflow) used in pregnancy hypertension; **prazosin, terazosin, doxazosin** are **peripheral α1-blockers** with **first-dose orthostatic hypotension** — initiate at bedtime.
- **β-blocker hierarchy:** propranolol (non-selective, ISA 0) → metoprolol/atenolol (β1-selective, lower bronchospasm risk) → carvedilol/labetalol (additional α1 block, metabolically neutral). **Contraindications:** asthma, severe bradycardia, 2°/3° AV block, decompensated HF (until stabilised).
- **Selective β2-agonists** (salbutamol, salmeterol, formoterol) cause **tremor (β2 on intrafusal fibres), hypokalaemia, and reflex tachycardia** at high doses — useful to recognise in toxicity stems.
- **Tyramine reaction:** MAO-A inhibition (tranylcypromine, phenelzine) + tyramine (cheese, wine, cured meat) → unopposed NE release → **hypertensive crisis**. Treatment: phentolamine (α-blocker).

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Distinction | Often-confused pair | Correct answer |
|---|---|---|
| Mydriasis — for ocular exam | Phenylephrine (α1) vs Atropine (M3 block) | Phenylephrine is short-acting and reversible; atropine lasts 7–14 days |
| Glaucoma | Pilocarpine (M-agonist, miosis, opens trabecular meshwork) vs Timolol (β-blocker, ↓aqueous production) | Both lower IOP but via opposite mechanisms |
| BPH | Tamsulosin (selective α1A blocker) vs Prazosin (non-selective α1) | Tamsulosin spares vascular α1B → less hypotension |
| Heart failure | Carvedilol (α+β block) vs Metoprolol succinate (β1 selective) | Both are evidence-based; carvedilol additionally ↓afterload |
| Myasthenia | Neostigmine (reversible AChE-I) vs Pyridostigmine | Pyridostigmine has longer t½, fewer GI cramps |
| Malignant hyperthermia | Dantrolene (RyR1 antagonist) vs Bromocriptine (D2 agonist) | Dantrolene = MH; bromocriptine = NMS |
| Organophosphate | Pralidoxime (regenerates AChE) vs Atropine (muscarinic antagonist) | Pralidoxime must precede aging; atropine treats symptoms only |
| NMJ reversal | Atropine alone vs Neostigmine + glycopyrrolate | Atropine does NOT reverse NMJ block |

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem
**Question:** A 32-year-old farmer is brought to the emergency department with excessive salivation, lacrimation, bronchospasm, bradycardia (HR 42/min), and muscle fasciculations after spraying insecticide. His pupils are miotic. The treating resident administers atropine 2 mg IV and notes improvement in bronchospasm and salivation. However, fasciculations persist and weakness worsens over the next 6 hours. What is the most appropriate next step, and why is pralidoxime (2-PAM) most effective if given within 24 hours?

#### Solution:
1. **Diagnosis** = Organophosphate poisoning (muscarinic + nicotinic features: SLUDGE + muscle fasciculations).
2. **First step** = continue atropine (titrated to drying of secretions), and **add pralidoxime 30 mg/kg IV bolus over 15–30 min**, then infusion 8 mg/kg/h.
3. **Mechanism** = Pralidoxime binds the anionic site of AChE and displaces the phosphate group, regenerating active enzyme *before* "aging" (dealkylation of the phosphoester bond, which becomes irreversible after ~24 h).
4. **Why combine with atropine?** Atropine competitively blocks muscarinic effects but does **not** restore neuromuscular function — only pralidoxime reactivates AChE at the NMJ.
5. **Supportive care:** benzodiazepines for seizures, decontamination, oxygenation; **avoid** succinylcholine (also metabolised by AChE) and theophylline.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Many students answer "atropine is enough" or delay 2-PAM believing fasciculations will self-resolve. **Aging occurs within 24 hours** (faster with dimethyl vs diethyl phosphates); after aging, pralidoxime **cannot** reactivate AChE and mortality rises sharply. Also, succinylcholine is contraindicated for intubation because plasma cholinesterase is inhibited.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Mastery Framework — How NEET PG Frames ANS Questions
- **Image-based:** ECG strips showing bradycardia (atropine use), QT changes from sotalol, or ECG with peaked T-waves (organophosphate-induced hyperkalaemia).
- **One-liners from KD Tripathi:** MAD as a hatter, BLIND as a bat, RED as a beet, HOT as a hare, DRY as a bone (atropine poisoning); reversed by **physostigmine** (tertiary amine crosses BBB).
- **Clinical vignettes:** Asthma patient on propranolol → contraindication; diabetic on insulin + propranolol → masking of tachycardia warning + delayed recovery from hypoglycaemia.

#### Edge Cases & Pharmacokinetic Pitfalls
1. **Atropine in infants** → hyperthermia ("atropine fever"); dose calculated by weight, not by age.
2. **Succinylcholine apnoea** → butyrylcholinesterase deficiency prolongs paralysis (10–60 min vs 5 min); not reversed by neostigmine.
3. **Phase I vs Phase II block with succinylcholine:** Phase I = sustained depolarisation (fasciculations); Phase II = desensitisation after prolonged infusion → antagonist-reversible.
5. **Tyramine-rich foods** (aged cheese, soy sauce, smoked meat, fermented products) + MAOI → hypertensive crisis within 30–60 min; treat with phentolamine or nitroprusside.
6. **Clonidine rebound hypertension** after abrupt stop → restart and taper; **β-blockers alone worsen it** (unopposed α).
7. **Prazosin first-dose syncope** → start 1 mg at bedtime, titrate up.
8. **Dantrolene** → hepatotoxic at chronic doses (monitor LFT); **contraindicated in active liver disease**.
9. **Mecamylamine** → centrally acting ganglion blocker historically used for hypertensive emergencies; now mostly investigational.
10. **Bethanechol** contraindicated in **mechanical bowel/bladder obstruction** and **asthma** (worsens bronchospasm via vagal tone).

#### Advanced Traps Table

| Trap | Scenario | Correct pharmacology point |
|---|---|---|
| "Phenoxybenzamine is reversible" | Pheochromocytoma pre-op | Phenoxybenzamine = **irreversible**, non-selective α-block; phentolamine = reversible |
| "Propranolol is safest in asthma" | Asthma + angina | Use **metoprolol** (β1-selective); propranolol causes bronchospasm |
| "Atropine reverses all NMJ blockers" | Post-op paralysis | Atropine reverses **muscarinic** effects only; use **neostigmine + glycopyrrolate** |
| "Succinylcholine is safe in burns" | Burn patient intubation | SCh causes **hyperkalaemia** in burns, denervation, crush injuries — use rocuronium |
| "Dantrolene treats NMS" | NMS question | Dantrolene treats **MH**; NMS first-line is **dantrolene OR bromocriptine**, but bromocriptine is more specific |
| "Tyramine reaction → β-blocker" | MAOI + cheese crisis | Use **α-blocker (phentolamine)**, never β-blocker |
| "Methyldopa in eclampsia is unsafe" | Pregnancy HTN | Methyldopa + hydralazine + labetalol are the **safe agents** in pregnancy |
| "Glycopyrrolate crosses BBB" | Premedication choice | Glycopyrrolate is **quaternary** → no CNS effects; atropine crosses and causes confusion |

#### Adjacent Topic Connections
- **Local anaesthetics:** Cocaine blocks NE reuptake → indirect sympathomimetic; adrenaline prolongs LA action via α1 vasoconstriction.
- **Antipsychotics:** Chlorpromazine blocks α1, M1, H1 → postural hypotension, dry mouth, sedation — same triad as TCAs.
- **Antiarrhythmics:** Class II = β-blockers; Class III = amiodarone/sotalol (sotalol is non-selective β-blocker + K⁺-channel blocker — torsades risk).
- **Glaucoma pharmacology:** Pilocarpine (M-agonist), timolol (β-blocker), latanoprost (PGF2α analogue), acetazolamide (CAI) — commonly combined in NEET PG stems.
- **Parkinson's disease:** Trihexyphenidyl, benztropine = antimuscarinics; central M4 blockade reduces cholinergic-dopaminergic imbalance.

#### Two Advanced Practice Prompts
1. A 45-year-old man with pheochromocytoma is posted for adrenalectomy. Explain the **pre-operative α-blockade protocol** (phenoxybenzamine → β-blocker only after adequate α-blockade), why β-blocker-alone is dangerous, and the role of intra-operative **nitroprusside/phentolamine** for hypertensive surges.
2. A patient on **tranylcypromine** for refractory depression eats aged cheddar at a party and develops severe headache, BP 220/130 mmHg, sweating, and pounding chest within an hour. Outline the **mechanism**, **immediate management** (IV phentolamine 5 mg bolus), and the dietary restrictions you must counsel before discharge.
