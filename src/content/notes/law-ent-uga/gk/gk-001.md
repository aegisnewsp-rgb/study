# Constitutional Supremacy and Sources of Law

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> The single line that decides this topic on the paper: the Constitution sits above every other law, and anything that clashes with it collapses.

Under Article 2(1) of the 1995 Constitution, the Constitution is the supreme law of Uganda. Article 2(2) then states the consequence — any law, act, or piece of conduct inconsistent with it is void to the extent of that inconsistency. Article 2(3) forces every court and parliament to interpret laws in a way that preserves the Constitution.

Uganda's sources of law, in descending authority:

| Rank | Source | Article / Basis |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Constitution of Uganda 1995 | Article 2 |
| 2 | Acts of Parliament | Article 91 |
| 3 | Subsidiary legislation (statutory instruments) | Article 41, Interpretation Act |
| 4 | Common law and doctrines of equity | Article 9 |
| 5 | Customary law | Article 246, Fifth Schedule |
| 6 | Ratified international law (where domesticated) | Article 123 |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook — "P-S-S-C-C":** Picture the stack as **P**arliament on top of **S**tatutes, with **S**ubsidiary underneath, then **C**ommon law, then **C**ustomary law. The Constitution caps the tower. Mnemonic line: "**C**onstitution crushes everything **b**elow."

Three quick traps:
- Treaties do not bind Uganda automatically — they need an Act of Parliament.
- Customary law only applies if it passes the Constitution-and-statute test in Article 246.
- Only superior courts (Supreme Court, Court of Appeal) bind inferior courts through *stare decisis*.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Constitutional supremacy is not a slogan; it is the rule that lets courts strike down Acts of Parliament, executive action, and customary practice that collide with the 1995 Constitution.

#### How Supremacy Operates in Practice

Article 2 gives the Constitution three jobs. First, it declares supremacy (clause 1). Second, it nullifies conflicting rules (clause 2). Third, it obliges courts and Parliament to read every other law in a Constitution-friendly way (clause 3). This is the engine behind every constitutional petition, including matters such as *Attorney General v. David Sejusa* (Constitutional Petition No. 1 of 2011), where the Supreme Court used Article 2 to resolve conflict between executive conduct and constitutional rights.

The High Court, Court of Appeal and Supreme Court each exercise **judicial review** under Article 128. Their orders bind every person and institution in Uganda, including the President — there is no executive override.

#### Sources and Their Limits

Common law and doctrines of equity apply only where the Constitution and any written law are silent (Article 9). Customary law applies only where it is not inconsistent with the Constitution or an Act of Parliament (Article 246, Fifth Schedule). Subsidiary legislation — rules, regulations, orders, by-laws made under powers conferred by an Act — sits below its parent Act and is **ultra vires** if it exceeds the enabling power.

International law is dualist in Uganda: ratification alone is not enough. Article 123 lists categories (peace, defence, foreign affairs, boundaries, trade) that require ratification by Parliament; other treaties become binding only on incorporation through domestic legislation.

#### Comparing Easily Confused Concepts

| Concept | What it really means | Common mistake to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Binding precedent | Decision of a superior court that inferior courts must follow | Confusing with persuasive precedent from foreign jurisdictions |
| Persuasive precedent | Decisions from foreign or peer courts that guide but do not bind | Treating UK or US rulings as automatically binding in Uganda |
| Customary law | Local, traditional rules recognised by communities | Assuming it overrides statute — Article 246 bars inconsistency |
| Subsidiary legislation | Rules made under an Act of Parliament (SIs, by-laws) | Treating it as having the same force as the Act itself |
| Doctrine of equity | Equitable remedies supplementing common law | Treating equity as overriding statute |
| Ultra vires | Beyond the legal powers of the maker | Confusing with *mala fides* or unfairness |

#### Worked Exam-Style Question

**Question (4 marks):** A village council applies a customary rule that bars women from inheriting clan land. The rule conflicts with Article 21 (equality) and Article 26 (property rights) of the 1995 Constitution. Advise whether the rule applies in Uganda.

#### Step-by-step resolution:
1. The Constitution is supreme under Article 2(1); any inconsistent law is void under Article 2(2).
2. Customary law is a source under Article 246 but only applies where it is consistent with the Constitution and any written law.
3. The customary rule excludes women from inheritance on a gender basis — this directly contradicts Article 21 (equality and freedom from discrimination) and Article 26 (protection from deprivation of property).
4. The Fifth Schedule applies a repugnancy test: custom repugnant to natural justice or inconsistent with the Constitution must be excluded.
5. Conclusion — the customary rule is void to the extent of the inconsistency. Succession under the Succession Act and the Constitution governs inheritance.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students often write that customary law was "overridden" without naming the exact article. Credit goes to those who cite **Article 246** plus the **Fifth Schedule** and frame the answer around Article 2 supremacy.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Push past Article 2 — supremacy also generates downstream doctrines that decide how courts handle rights, conflicts, and silent statutes.

#### Where Supremacy Breaks Down

1. **The "not unjust, just inconsistent" rule.** Courts strike down Acts only for inconsistency with the Constitution, not because a law is unfair or unwise. Policy choices belong to Parliament.
2. **The interpretive duty.** Article 2(3) is mandatory. A statute with two possible readings must be read the way that preserves the Constitution, not the way that strikes it down.
3. **The hierarchy ceiling.** Customary law and common law cannot climb above Acts of Parliament. The Constitution is the only rule above an Act.
4. **The treaty gateway.** A treaty signed but not domesticated cannot create enforceable rights in Ugandan courts — *Twinomukama v. Attorney General* is the classic illustration of this dualist position.
5. **Subsidiary limits.** Where a statutory instrument contradicts its parent Act, the SI fails; the parent Act is read as written.

#### Connections Worth Noting

- **Locus standi** — under Article 50, any person may petition the Constitutional Court for redress of a constitutional violation, broadening access beyond traditional standing rules.
- **Separation of powers** (Chapter 8) keeps Parliament's law-making role distinct from the Judiciary's interpreting role. Courts do not draft statutes; Parliament cannot reverse a court's constitutional interpretation.
- **Harmonisation of laws** under Clause 6 of the Sixth Schedule required bringing pre-1995 statutes into line with the Constitution; old laws that could not be harmonised lapsed.

#### Advanced Traps and Exceptions

| Trap | Why it catches students |
|---|---|
| Reading Article 2 as giving courts "veto power" over Parliament | Courts invalidate only the inconsistent part — they do not rewrite statutes |
| Believing ratified treaties automatically bind | Dualism: domestication through an Act of Parliament is required |
| Assuming persuasive precedent equals binding | Persuasive precedent from the UK, India, or South Africa guides reasoning but does not bind |
| Treating equity as above statute | Equity yields to statute; it supplements, never overrides |
| Citing customary law without testing against the Constitution | Article 246 consistency test is mandatory, not optional |

#### Two Practice Prompts

1. Parliament passes an Act allowing warrantless searches of journalists' homes. A journalist is arrested under it. Trace the constitutional arguments using Article 2, Article 22 (privacy), and Article 28 (freedom of the press). Identify which source ranks highest and which doctrine authorises the court to act.
2. Uganda signs the African Continental Free Trade Area agreement but does not enact a domesticating Act. A trader sues the government to enforce a tariff concession given under the treaty. Decide the case, citing the relevant source-of-law rule and the appropriate Ugandan precedent.
