# Number — GCSE Mathematics (AQA 8300) Notes

The Number strand is the foundation of every other topic at GCSE. Four operations, fractions, decimals, percentages, indices and standard form all appear across Paper 1 (non-calculator), Paper 2 and Paper 3, and a mistake in this strand pulls marks out of Ratio, Algebra, Geometry and Statistics questions that depend on it. AQA splits the strand into four sub-areas at the higher tier: structure and calculation, fractions, decimals and percentages, indices and surds, and approximation and estimation.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Place value** for integers and decimals, including multiplying/dividing by powers of 10 (the decimal point moves the number of places equal to the exponent).
- **Four operations** with integers, fractions and decimals, including BIDMAS order of operations.
- **Indices** laws: aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ; aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ; (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ; a⁰ = 1; a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ.
- **Standard form**: a number written as a × 10ⁿ where 1 ≤ a < 10.
- **Surds**: √a × √b = √(ab); √(a/b) = √a/√b; (√a + √b)(√a − √b) = a − b.
- **Bounds**: use the smallest value of the lower bound and the largest value of the upper bound when calculating with measurements.

#### Examiner traps

- Forgetting to convert units (cm to m, g to kg) before dividing — a 4-mark ratio question becomes 0 if you divide grams by kilograms.
- Treating (√2 + √3)² as 5 instead of 5 + 2√6 — expand, do not cancel.
- Rounding too early in multi-step problems — keep the full calculator display and round only the final answer to the required precision.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Structure and calculation

Place value is the value of a digit determined by its position. In 3,402 the 4 is in the hundreds place, worth 400. Multiplying a decimal by 10 moves each digit one place to the left; dividing by 10 moves each digit one place to the right.

**BIDMAS** is the agreed order of operations:

1. **B**rackets
2. **I**ndices (powers and roots)
3. **D**ivision and **M**ultiplication (left to right)
4. **A**ddition and **S**ubtraction (left to right)

A long calculation like 4 + 3 × 5 = 19, not 35 — multiplication binds tighter than addition.

#### Fractions, decimals and percentages

**Equivalent forms.** Fractions, decimals and percentages all represent the same proportional relationship and must move freely between forms:

| Fraction | Decimal | Percentage |
|----------|---------|------------|
| 1/2 | 0.5 | 50% |
| 1/3 | 0.333… | 33.3…% |
| 1/4 | 0.25 | 25% |
| 1/5 | 0.2 | 20% |
| 1/8 | 0.125 | 12.5% |
| 1/10 | 0.1 | 10% |

**Fraction arithmetic.** To add or subtract fractions, find a common denominator first. To multiply, multiply numerators and denominators straight across. To divide, flip the second fraction (the divisor) and multiply.

**Percentage change.** "Increase by r%" means multiply by (1 + r/100). "Decrease by r%" means multiply by (1 − r/100). For a repeated percentage change, multiply the factors: a 10% increase followed by a 10% decrease is × 1.10 × 0.90 = × 0.99, so the final value is 1% lower, not 0% (a common AQA trap).

#### Indices

**Laws of indices** (a, b ≠ 0; m, n are integers):

- aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ
- aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ
- (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ
- a⁰ = 1
- a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ
- a^(1/n) = ⁿ√a
- a^(m/n) = ⁿ√(aᵐ) = (ⁿ√a)ᵐ

**Fractional indices** at higher tier: a^(1/2) = √a, a^(1/3) = ∛a, a^(2/3) = ∛(a²) = (∛a)². Do not confuse with roots of sums: √(a² + b²) is NOT a + b.

#### Standard form

A number is in standard form when written as a × 10ⁿ where 1 ≤ a < 10 and n is an integer. For example:

- 4500 = 4.5 × 10³
- 0.00032 = 3.2 × 10⁻⁴
- 602,200,000,000,000,000,000,000 = 6.022 × 10²³ (Avogadro's number)

To multiply in standard form, multiply the a-parts and add the exponents. To divide, divide the a-parts and subtract the exponents.

#### Surds

A surd is an irrational root that cannot be simplified to a whole number, like √2, √3, √5. Surds are kept in exact form unless the question asks for a decimal answer.

**Simplifying surds.** Find the largest square factor: √12 = √(4 × 3) = 2√3. **Adding surds.** You can only add like surds: 3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2, but 3√2 + 5√3 stays as 3√2 + 5√3.

**Rationalising the denominator.** Rewrite 1/√3 as √3/3, and (2 + √3)/(5 − √3) as (2 + √3)(5 + √3) / ((5)² − (√3)²) = (10 + 2√3 + 5√3 + 3) / (25 − 3) = (13 + 7√3) / 22. Always multiply top and bottom by the conjugate.

#### Bounds and approximation

A measurement rounded to the nearest unit has a lower bound (just below) and an upper bound (just above). For example, a length given as 4.3 cm to 1 decimal place lies in the range 4.25 ≤ x < 4.35 cm.

**Calculating with bounds.** To get the upper bound of a product or quotient of rounded measurements, use the largest possible value of each factor. For the lower bound, use the smallest. For sums and differences, use opposite bounds (smallest minus largest, or largest minus smallest) to give the greatest range.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Why Number underpins every other strand

- **Algebra** depends on confident manipulation of directed numbers, fractions in equations, and indices in laws like (x²)³ = x⁶.
- **Ratio** uses the unitary method: divide to find "one share", then multiply to find any share. Decimal-place errors destroy the answer.
- **Geometry** uses Pythagoras (a² + b² = c²) where a, b, c can be surds, and the cosine rule where the result is rounded correctly.
- **Statistics** uses the mean of grouped data and percentage change for compound growth, both relying on the four operations and rounding rules.

#### Exam technique

- Always write the units in the answer unless the question explicitly says "give your answer as a number".
- Show every step of BIDMAS in long calculations; partial marks on a 5-mark question depend on visible working.
- When a question asks for an exact answer, do not round intermediate steps — keep √2 and π in surd form.
- Use the answer line to do a sanity check: is the order of magnitude right? A speed of 0.0003 m/s for a car is wrong by inspection.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Multiplying always makes a number bigger." Only true for positive factors > 1. 0.5 × 10 = 5 (bigger); 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2 (smaller).
- "−3² = 9." It is −9. −3² means −(3 × 3) = −9. To get 9 you must write (−3)².
- "Adding two negatives makes a smaller number." Adding −3 + −5 = −8, which is indeed smaller than −3, but the operation is still addition.
- "0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25, which is bigger than 0.5." Counter-intuitive but true: multiplying two numbers between 0 and 1 always gives a smaller number.

#### Specification reference

This strand is assessed across all three papers at both tiers. Higher tier extends into upper and lower bounds in calculations, fractional indices, and the use of standard form in context (e.g., very large or very small quantities in science).
