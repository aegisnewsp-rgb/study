# Units & Measurement

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Units & Measurement** is the framework that lets every CUET UG Physics numerical speak a common language. A physical quantity equals a numerical value multiplied by a unit (e.g., length = 5 m).

The seven SI base quantities to memorise are **length (metre, m)**, **mass (kilogram, kg)**, **time (second, s)**, **electric current (ampere, A)**, **temperature (kelvin, K)**, **amount of substance (mole, mol)**, and **luminous intensity (candela, cd)**. Two supplementary units — **radian** and **steradian** — handle angles.

Derived quantities are built from these using dimensional formulas of the form **[Q] = MᵃLᵇTᶜ**. Force gives **[F] = M L T⁻²** because F = ma with [a] = L T⁻². The **principle of homogeneity** says every term added in an equation must carry identical dimensions; otherwise the equation is physically wrong.

- SI symbols stay lowercase unless named after a scientist: **m**, **kg**, but **N**, **Pa**, **J**.
- Percentage error = (Δa_mean / a_mean) × 100, dimensionless.
- A trailing zero without a decimal is ambiguous — write **5.20 × 10² m**, not 520 m.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### SI Units and Writing Conventions

CUET UG accepts only SI answers in numericals, so symbol hygiene matters. SI conventions require symbols in lowercase Roman (m, kg, s, cd, mol), except those named after a scientist which start uppercase (N, Pa, J, W, Hz, °C — note the degree sign). Symbols never pluralise ("5 kg", never "5 kgs"). When a numerical prefix attaches, the symbol stays intact: **kilometre = km**, **megawatt = MW**.

#### Dimensional Analysis

Dimensional analysis rests on writing every derived quantity as **[Q] = MᵃLᵇTᶜ**. To derive a relation, assume Q depends on powers of relevant variables, then balance both sides. Procedure:

1. List variables assumed to influence the quantity.
2. Form Q = k · xᵃ · yᵇ · zᶜ where k is dimensionless.
3. Substitute dimensions into each side; equate exponents of M, L, T.
4. Solve for a, b, c.

The **principle of homogeneity** is the quickest exam check: scan each term in v = u + at² — it fails because **[at²] = L T⁻¹** but **[u] = L T⁻¹** would actually match, so v = u + at is correct and v = u + at² is not.

#### Errors and Significant Figures

| Type | Definition | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Absolute error | Deviation of one reading from the mean | Δaᵢ = \|a_mean − aᵢ\| |
| Mean absolute error | Average of all absolute errors | Δa_mean = ΣΔaᵢ / n |
| Relative error | Ratio, dimensionless | ε = Δa_mean / a_mean |
| Percentage error | Relative error in percent | %ε = ε × 100 |

Significant figures obey strict count rules: non-zero digits count, zeros between them count, leading zeros don't count (0.0052 → 2 s.f.), and trailing zeros need a decimal to count (5.20 × 10² → 3 s.f., but 520 → ambiguous).

> CUET trap: in a product 2.51 × 1.3 = 3.263, the answer must round to **3.3** (2 s.f.), not match the calculator.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Accuracy, Precision, and Error Sources

Accuracy measures closeness to the true value; precision measures how tightly repeated readings cluster. A dartboard analogy: accurate + precise shots hit the bullseye together; precise only shots land in a tight cluster off-centre; accurate only shots scatter across the bullseye. Systematic errors shift accuracy but leave precision intact — calibrate the instrument. Random errors scatter both accuracy and precision — reduce by averaging many trials.

Error classification matters for one-line CUET MCQs:

- **Systematic**: instrumental zero error, personal parallax, environmental drift — repeatable direction.
- **Random**: statistical fluctuations; minimised by larger sample size.
- **Gross**: blunders like recording 21 cm as 12 cm; rejected, not averaged.

#### Dimensional Limits You Must Know

Dimensional analysis cannot deliver a pure number — that hides inside k. It also flags two equations that look identical dimensionally but differ physically (e.g., simple pendulum period relations). When dimensions match but k is uncertain, you cannot pick between competing formulas.

#### Worked Example — Parallax

Read a meter scale at 50 cm against a distant nail. Eye displacement 2 mm shifts the reading by 1 mm. Compute distance using parallax: D = b / θ with b = 1 mm = 10⁻³ m and θ ≈ reading/distance ≈ 0.50/D. Solving D² ≈ 0.50 × 10⁻³ ⇒ D ≈ 2.2 × 10⁻² m? No — typical large-distance parallax: D = b / tan(θ). With small θ, D ≈ b·u / Δu, giving D ≈ 0.5 × 10⁻³ / 2 × 10⁻³ = 0.25 m only when b and Δu share an axis; real parallax method uses **D = (baseline) × (true reading shift) / (apparent reading shift)**. Numerical fluency with these forms is what CUET rewards.

#### Common Mistakes

> Treating "0.0520 kg" as 4 s.f. — it has 3 s.f.; leading zeros never count.

- Confusing **[F] = M L T⁻²** with **[F] = M L T⁻¹** by forgetting to multiply mass by acceleration's dimensions.
- Reporting mean absolute error with the wrong number of significant figures (it must match the precision of a_mean).
- Adding percentages of quantities directly: %ε in (A + B) becomes %ε(A)·(A/total) + %ε(B)·(B/total); students blindly add.

#### CUET Strategy

Expect **1–2 questions**: one numerical (dimensional check or significant figure rounding) plus one conceptual (accuracy vs precision, SI symbol, error type). Mechanics, gravitation, and optics later assume a fluent dimensional vocabulary, so this 3%-weight topic over-pays downstream.

#### Practice Prompts

1. Check dimensionally whether **s = ut + ½at²** is valid, identifying which term violates homogeneity if it does.
2. A clock gains 25 s over 24 h. Express the percentage error and decide whether this is systematic or random.
