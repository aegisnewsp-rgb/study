# Data Interpretation (Charts/Tables)

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Data Interpretation (DI)** in NAT-I Quantitative Reasoning tests your ability to read numbers off a chart or table and answer questions on **ratios, percentages, averages, and trends** without relying on a calculator-heavy method. Each item gives you a pre-drawn **bar chart, pie chart, line graph, histogram, or frequency table**, and a stem asks for a specific derived value.

Core formulas to memorise:
- **Percentage change** = ((New − Old) / Old) × 100
- **Arithmetic mean** = Sum of values ÷ Number of values
- **Pie chart angle** = (Category value ÷ Total) × 360°
- **Ratio a:b** = a/b, simplified by dividing both terms by GCD

**High-yield pointers:** (1) Always check the axis unit — thousands, millions, or lakhs change the answer 1000×. (2) For pie charts, never add slice angles directly without converting to values first. (3) Percentage change questions always use the **older** value as the denominator.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Chart and Table Types You Will Meet

NAT-I typically uses **five visual formats**, and recognising the format is half the solution:

- **Bar chart** — discrete categories on the x-axis, magnitude on the y-axis; compare heights directly or read values off the gridlines.
- **Line graph** — continuous data over time; the **slope** between two points equals the rate of change.
- **Pie chart** — each slice’s central angle is proportional to its share of the whole (360° total).
- **Histogram** — continuous class intervals on the x-axis; bar **area** (not height alone) equals frequency when intervals are unequal.
- **Cumulative frequency curve (ogive)** — read the **median** at the 50% mark on the y-axis.

#### Conversion Between Formats

A table can be redrawn as a bar chart, and a bar chart’s values can be collapsed into a pie chart. The conversion rule is the **pie chart angle** formula: a category whose value is 1/5 of the total occupies (1/5 × 360°) = 72°. Going the other way, an 80° slice = 80/360 = 22.22% of the total.

#### Central Tendency From Frequency Tables

For grouped data, the **mean** uses class midpoints: Mean = Σ(f × midpoint) ÷ Σf. The **median class** is the one containing the (N/2)-th observation, and the **modal class** is the one with the highest frequency. The **range** is the difference between the largest and smallest class midpoints.

#### Worked Micro-Example

A company’s quarterly sales (in million Rs) are: Q1 = 20, Q2 = 25, Q3 = 30, Q4 = 45.
- **Mean** = (20+25+30+45) / 4 = **30 million**.
- **Q4 percentage of annual total** = 45/120 × 100 = **37.5%**.
- **Pie angle for Q4** = (45/120) × 360° = **135°**.
- **Percentage change Q1 → Q4** = (45−20)/20 × 100 = **125% increase**.

#### Common NAT-I Traps

- Adding percentages across slices instead of weighting by raw values.
- Misreading a **non-zero-origin** axis (e.g., the y-axis starts at 50, not 0).
- Using the **new** value as the base when calculating percentage change.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Advanced Patterns

**Non-uniform class intervals** in histograms require you to compute **frequency density** = frequency ÷ class width before comparing bar heights — a wider bar with the same height represents more observations than a narrower one.

**Weighted averages** appear when each data point carries a different weight (e.g., average price across regions with different sales volumes). Formula: **Weighted Mean = Σ(weight × value) ÷ Σ(weight)**. NAT-I tests this by giving you two group means and asking for the combined mean — the simple arithmetic average of the two means is almost always **wrong**; you must weight by group size.

**Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR)** appears in line-graph questions asking for the average yearly growth over n years: **CAGR = ((End/Start)^(1/n) − 1) × 100**. This is distinct from simple percentage change, which uses only two endpoints.

#### Index Numbers and Trend Reading

When a line graph plots an **index** (e.g., 2018 = 100), every other year’s reading is a ratio relative to that base. A reading of 115 in 2023 means a 15% cumulative rise since 2018, not 15% absolute value.

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics

DI in NAT-I overlaps directly with **Percentages, Ratios & Proportions, and Averages** — the three other heavy sub-areas of Quantitative Reasoning. Mastering DI forces you to revisit those fundamentals because almost every DI question is a percentages/ratios/averages calculation applied to chart-extracted numbers.

#### Two Practice Prompts

1. *A pie chart shows expenditure: Rent 90°, Food 120°, Transport 60°, Savings 90°. If total income is Rs 200,000, how much is spent on Food, and what is the Rent-to-Transport ratio by value?*
   (Hint: Food = 120/360 × 200,000 = Rs 66,667; Rent:Transport = 90:60 = 3:2.)

2. *Sales were 50 in Year 1 and 80 in Year 4. Calculate the simple percentage change and the CAGR, then state which is larger and why.*
   (Hint: Simple change = 60%; CAGR = (80/50)^(1/3) − 1 ≈ 16.99% per year. Simple change is larger because growth compounds downward when spread over multiple years.)

#### Exam Strategy

Allocate **45–60 seconds per DI MCQ**. Scan the chart for the exact values the question references — do not waste time reading every label. If a question asks for an approximate answer, round intermediate values to the nearest whole unit before dividing.
