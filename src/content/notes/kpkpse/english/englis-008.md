# Comprehension Passages: reading the KPPSC English paper properly

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> The PMS English paper is not a general English exam. Read what the official syllabus actually asks for.

The KPPSC Provincial Management Service syllabus (PMS Syllabus 2025, published on kppsc.gov.pk) describes the compulsory **English (Précis & Composition)** paper as 100 marks, testing three things: "grammatical structure, reading comprehension and analysis and précis writing and composition". There is no separate vocabulary paper and no prescribed course of grammar — the syllabus says so directly: "There shall be no prescribed course for this purpose."

The comprehension half is specified just as precisely:

- **Two unseen passages** with a **fixed reading time**.
- **Multiple-choice questions placed at the end**, answered after the passages.
- The comprehension passage "shall be **fairly technical**".
- Passages are "selected from writings on **economic, social, cultural** subjects and **international affairs**".

The précis half draws on "current, economic, social, cultural and international affairs" as well.

| Paper | Marks | What it tests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| English (Précis & Composition) | 100 | Grammar and structure, comprehension and analysis, précis and composition |
| English Essay | 100 | One or more essays in English, from a wide choice of subjects |

Two separate 100-mark English papers. That is 200 compulsory marks out of a 900-mark examination — English is not a subsidiary subject in this exam, and candidates who treat it as one lose 200 marks before the optionals begin.

**One rule that ends careers:** answer in the language the paper demands. The syllabus states that all papers other than the language papers must be answered in English, and that "violation of this instruction shall incur cancellation of the concerned paper(s) and consequently award of Zero". Writing a good essay in Urdu because you think better in Urdu costs you the whole paper.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Turning a syllabus paragraph into a daily practice routine.

#### What "fairly technical" actually means

The syllabus does not say "difficult". It says technical, and it names the source register: economic, social, cultural writing and international affairs. In practice that means your passages look like editorial and analytical journalism — a trade-policy piece, a demographic argument, a foreign-affairs commentary — rather than a story or a general-interest essay.

Practise that register deliberately. Three months before the exam, replace your leisure reading for six weeks with two pieces a week from that register: an economics explainer, a foreign-affairs column, a long-form social policy article. Not because you will see them in the exam, but because your reading speed in the target register is the thing being tested.

#### The fixed reading time is the whole game

Two passages, fixed reading time, questions at the end. That structure means the passage is a *budgeted input*, not a discussion. You get one pass to understand it and a second to answer. Candidates who read the passage three times carefully and then discover they have no time for the second passage fail the paper in the reading, not in the answering.

Build the habit: read the passage once for structure, mark the topic sentence of each paragraph, write the main idea in one line, then move on. Come back to answer. If you cannot write the one-line main idea after the first read, you have not understood the passage well enough to answer its questions, and no amount of extra time in the answer stage will fix that.

#### Questions at the end means the questions cannot drive your reading

Multiple-choice questions are placed **at the end**. So you cannot read the passage while watching for what will be asked. That kills the most common exam tactic — hunting for keywords. What replaces it is structure-reading: if you know the shape of the argument you can answer most questions without re-reading.

Practise the four structural moves:

- **Topic sentence** — usually first, occasionally last, occasionally the whole paragraph if it is short.
- **Discourse markers** — "however", "therefore", "in contrast", "moreover", "that is to say". These mark the argument turning, and the questions love the turn.
- **Hedging and emphasis** — "tends to", "is often argued", "significant". Attributed claims are not the writer's claims, and a question asking what the writer *asserts* is testing exactly this.
- **The last paragraph** — in analytical journalism this usually carries the conclusion or the recommendation. It is the highest-value paragraph per line.

#### Précis is not summarising

The syllabus asks for "an acceptable précis of a given passage" drawn from current, economic, social, cultural and international affairs. A précis has rules. Fix its length before you write, compress every sentence, keep the passage's proportions, and preserve the relative emphasis — what the writer treats as important, you treat as important.

The single most common failure is paraphrase at original length. If your précis runs as long as a third of the passage, you have written a summary. Set yourself a hard ceiling — a précis is measured in a small fixed number of words — and enforce it.

#### Grammar without a prescribed course

The syllabus is unusually direct: items are "framed based upon grammatical categories that usually create problems for foreign students", and "there shall be no prescribed course for this purpose".

Read that twice. It means no textbook covers what will be asked, and it names the actual target: the categories that create problems — tense consistency, subject–verb agreement, articles, prepositions, and word order. Those five are where the marks are. Practise them as error-spotting on real sentences rather than by reading a grammar chapter.

#### Where comprehension sits in the overall PMS structure

The written portion totals 900 marks including a 300-mark viva voce. Compulsory subjects: English (Précis & Composition) 100, English Essay 100, General Knowledge 300 (Current Affairs 100, Every Day Science 100, Pakistan Affairs 100), Islamiat 100. Candidates then choose three optional subjects totalling 600 marks, with not more than 200 from a single group.

To qualify for the viva voce you need at least 33 percent in each optional subject, 40 percent in each compulsory subject, and 50 percent in the aggregate of the written portion. Non-Muslim candidates may take Islamiat or, in its place, Pakistan Affairs (General Knowledge Paper III) treated as 200 marks in lieu.

So English carries a **40 percent per-subject hurdle**, not just share of a total. A candidate clearing the 50 percent aggregate while slipping below 40 percent in either English paper still does not reach the viva voce. That is the practical reason to prepare English properly rather than adequately.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> For candidates aiming at a précis-and-composition band that stands out.

#### Register analysis as a preparation method

Because the syllabus fixes the register — economic, social, cultural, international affairs — you can reverse-engineer the passage set. Take one month, read twenty pieces from that register, and keep a register log with four columns: source type, discourse structure, dominant mood, and the rhetorical move that carries the argument.

What this does in practice:

- **Source type** tells you what arguments recur (trade, demography, energy security, institutions) and therefore which domain vocabulary is worth building.
- **Discourse structure** tells you how argument-bearing paragraphs are shaped in this register, which is what you are reading for under time pressure.
- **Dominant mood** (analytic, prescriptive, descriptive) tells you how far to trust an inference question. A descriptive passage resists inference; an analytic one is built on it.
- **Rhetorical move** (contrast, concession, causal claim, definition) is the answer key to "why does the writer say X here".

That log is also your précis practice, because a précis is a compression of exactly this structure.

#### Précis technique: the four-pass method

1. **Pass one — skeleton.** Paragraph numbers, topic sentences, the conclusion. Roughly one minute. This is your outline.
2. **Pass two — proportion.** Mark each point as central, secondary or incidental. Incidental material is what you drop first under pressure.
3. **Pass three — compress.** One sentence per central point, one clause per secondary point, nothing for incidental. Use different words from the passage wherever possible.
4. **Pass four — check.** Does it read as English? Does it preserve the passage's emphasis? Does it fit the word count? A précis that is grammatical and accurate but over-length is marked down.

Practise on editorial passages with a stopwatch. The skill is not knowing how to précis; it is being able to finish a précis in the time the paper allows.

#### The composition half

The same paper carries composition alongside comprehension. The syllabus lists it in the same breath as the rest: candidates must be capable of "using English correctly and efficiently as a vehicle of communication". Notice *efficiently* — that is a test of economy, not decoration.

Write, do not compose in your head. A weekly loop of one précis and one 250-word paragraph on a current-affairs subject produces more improvement in three months than a grammar book read once. Have it marked against three checks only: structure, accuracy, economy.

#### Error-spotting as the highest-yield grammar work

Because grammar items are "framed based upon grammatical categories that usually create problems for foreign students", the efficient method is to collect real sentences rather than invent exercises. Take ten sentences a week from your own writing, find every error, name the category, and fix it. Track categories in a small table.

In most candidates' logs, five categories account for most of the errors: tense consistency, subject–verb agreement, articles, prepositions, and subject–word order. Once your log shows you fixing those automatically, extra grammar study has sharply diminishing returns.

#### Language compliance is a scoring issue, not a style issue

Re-read the language instruction in the syllabus: language papers in the language concerned, Islamiat in Urdu or English, all other papers in English, and violation means cancellation and Zero. Given English Essay is 100 marks and English (Précis & Composition) is another 100, a single slip of language habit has a 200-mark price tag.

Write one full paper under the language rule every week. The rule is easy to forget precisely because it is easy to honour in normal life — you switch to Urdu at home, and the habit is live before you sit down.

#### Common mistakes and exam strategy

1. **Reading the passage three times and running out of time on the second one.** Read once for structure, move on, come back to answer.
2. **Hunting for keywords because the questions are at the end.** You cannot see them. Read structure instead.
3. **Writing a précis at a third of the passage's length.** Set a hard word ceiling and enforce it.
4. **Answering in Urdu because you think in Urdu.** Cancellation and Zero applies to the paper.
5. **Treating English as a subsidiary subject.** Two compulsory papers, 200 marks, and a 40 percent per-subject hurdle to reach the viva voce.

> **Exam strategy:** on the day, allocate the reading of both passages first and keep to it. A candidate who finishes both passages with three minutes left will answer all the questions; a candidate who understands them beautifully with no time left will answer none. Understanding is not the constraint. Clock management is.

#### Practice prompts

1. *"Take any 350-word editorial passage on economic affairs. Produce a précis of no more than one third its length, preserving the writer's emphasis, in fifteen minutes."* — Then compare against the original paragraph by paragraph and mark every point you dropped.
2. *"The PMS syllabus specifies technical passages from economic, social, cultural and international-affairs writing. What does that rule tell you about how to read?"* — Answer from the structural consequences: no keyword hunting, one structure pass, questions answered at the end.
3. *"Why does the syllabus specify that there is no prescribed course for grammar, and what should a candidate do instead?"* — Name the categories that create problems for foreign students and describe an error-log routine against them.
