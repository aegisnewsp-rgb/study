# Thermal Properties

This chapter is about how matter responds to temperature. It has four parts, and all four are formula applications: **thermal expansion**, **calorimetry and latent heat**, **the three modes of heat transfer**, and **radiation**. The first is descriptive, the second is arithmetic, the third is comparison, and the fourth is one equation with a fourth power in it.

The single idea that organises the calorimetry half is that heat is energy, and energy is conserved. Every mixture problem is the same equation with different numbers in it: heat lost by the hot body equals heat gained by the cold body plus the calorimeter. The single idea that organises the radiation half is that a hot surface emits far more than a lukewarm one, because the emitted power goes as the **fourth power of absolute temperature**.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> The formulas, the constants and the two conversion errors that cost marks.

**Temperature scales.** Every formula that contains T wants kelvin. T(K) = t(°C) + 273.15, and ΔT in K equals ΔT in °C, since the two scales differ only by a fixed offset. Absolute zero is −273.15 °C.

**Thermal expansion**

| Coefficient | Symbol | Definition | Unit | Relation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Linear | α | ΔL = L₀αΔT | K⁻¹ | — |
| Areal (cubical) | β | ΔA = A₀βΔT | K⁻¹ | β = 2α |
| Volume (cubical) | γ | ΔV = V₀γΔT | K⁻¹ | γ = 3α |

These relations hold for an isotropic solid. Order the coefficients, always: **α < β < γ**, with β = 2α and γ = 3α.

**Calorimetry.** Q = mcΔT, where Q is heat in J, m in kg, c the specific heat capacity in J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, ΔT in K. During a phase change Q = mL with L the specific latent heat in J kg⁻¹. Reference values for water: c = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, L_fusion = 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹, L_vaporisation = 2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹ (at 100 °C), L_sublimation of ice at 0 °C ≈ 3.34 × 10⁵ + 2.26 × 10⁶ = 2.59 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹.

**The three modes of heat transfer**

| Mode | Mechanism | Needs a medium? | Fastest in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conduction | vibration of particles, free-electron drift in metals | yes | solids, then liquids, then gases |
| Convection | bulk motion of the fluid itself | yes | liquids and gases |
| Radiation | electromagnetic waves | no | a vacuum |

**Conduction rate:** H = kA(T₁ − T₂)/l, with k the thermal conductivity in W m⁻¹ K⁻¹. Metals conduct well because of free electrons; diamond conducts well but is brittle, and it is a thermal conductor rather than an electrical one because all its electrons are bound.

**Convection and cooling.** Newton's law of cooling: −dT/dt = k(T − T_s), where T_s is the surrounding temperature. A body cools fastest when it is hottest and slows down as it approaches T_s; the cooling curve is steep at first and flattens out. Real bodies obey this well for small differences and for slow losses, since radiation is not linear.

**Radiation.** Stefan-Boltzmann: P = σAT⁴ with σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴, A in m², T in K. For net exchange with surroundings at T₀, P_net = σA(T⁴ − T₀⁴) for a black body. Wien's displacement law: λ_max T = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m K. Kirchhoff: a good absorber is a good emitter at every wavelength.

**The two conversion traps:** °C into K for anything with a T⁴, and grams into kilograms for any formula carrying m. Both are one-line errors that produce answers wrong by orders of magnitude.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> The reasoning behind the formulas, and the comparisons the exam asks for.

**Temperature, heat and the zeroth law**

Temperature is a measure of the average translational kinetic energy of the particles of a body, so it is a property of the state, not of the process. Heat is energy in transit because of a temperature difference; once the energy has arrived, it is no longer called heat, it is internal energy. The distinction matters in calorimetry, where Q is heat transferred and the resulting change in temperature is not itself heat.

The zeroth law of thermodynamics states that if A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with C, then A and B are in equilibrium with each other. It is the law that makes a thermometer possible: once you can say two bodies have the same temperature, a temperature scale can be defined. The Celsius scale uses the ice point (0 °C) and the steam point (100 °C) as fixed points; the kelvin scale is defined instead by a single point, absolute zero, and the size of one kelvin equals one degree Celsius.

**Thermal expansion and its molecular origin**

In a solid the atoms sit at potential-energy minima in a lattice. The energy of a pair separated by r depends on r, and the minimum is not at the same r for the vibrating-and-stretched state as for the rest state. The vibrating atom therefore spends more time at separations greater than r₀, and the average separation grows when the temperature rises. The average separation, not the peak of the vibration, is what sets the length of the solid. Liquids expand for a similar reason but their molecules are already mobile, and gases expand mainly because they push apart more.

Three consequences that are worth stating as full sentences:

- **Rails and bridges have gaps.** If a rail were one unbroken bar, it would buckle in summer. The gap allows free expansion.
- **A bimetallic strip bends.** Two metals with different α are bonded together; the one with the larger α is longer when hot and so lies on the outside of the curve. This is the working element of every oven thermostat and dial thermometer.
- **A bottle of water filled to the brim overflows on a hot day,** and a ring that fits in winter is tight in summer, for exactly the same reason.

**Real and apparent expansion of liquids.** A liquid in a container expands the container too, so what a thermometer shows is the **apparent** expansion. Apparent γ = γ_liquid − γ_container. Water is the textbook case: ice floats because its density is about 0.917 g cm⁻³, about 9% less than liquid water, so freezing a volume of water into ice increases the volume by about 1/9. That is why a closed bottle of water bursts in a freezer.

**Anomalous expansion of water.** Between 0 °C and 4 °C water **contracts** on heating, and its density is maximum at 4 °C. Above 4 °C it expands normally. The molecular explanation is the open hydrogen-bonded structure of water: as the temperature rises from 0 to 4 °C some hydrogen bonds break, the molecules pack more efficiently, and the density increases; above 4 °C the ordinary thermal expansion takes over. Consequences: a lake cools from the top, ice floats and insulates, and the water beneath stays near 4 °C, liquid, through the winter. Aquatic life survives for that reason. There is no other common liquid with this behaviour.

**Specific heat capacity and the meaning of the symbol**

The specific heat capacity c of a substance is the heat needed to raise 1 kg by 1 K: c = 1/m · dQ/dT. It is a material property, not a property of a sample, which is why a 1 kg steel ball and a 100 g steel ball have the same c but different total heat capacities. A gram of water needs 4.2 kJ to warm by 1 K; the same gram of copper needs only 0.39 kJ. Water's high specific heat (4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, the highest of any common substance) and its high latent heats are why it is used in hot-water bottles, in car cooling systems and as a heat sink, and why large bodies of water moderate the climate of the coasts beside them.

For gases use the **molar** form: Q = nCΔT with C in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. For a monatomic ideal gas C_V = 3R/2 and C_P = 5R/2; for a diatomic gas at ordinary temperatures C_V = 5R/2 and C_P = 7R/2, giving γ = C_P/C_V = 5/3 and 7/5 respectively. The relation C_P − C_V = R holds for any ideal gas.

**Latent heat and the plateau**

During melting or boiling the temperature does not change even though energy keeps entering. The energy goes into breaking intermolecular bonds — the potential energy part of the internal energy — not into raising the kinetic energy, and temperature tracks the latter only. Hence the heating curve of water has flat plateaus at 0 °C and 100 °C at atmospheric pressure, and sloped regions between them. Ice at 0 °C has a specific latent heat of fusion 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹; water at 100 °C has 2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹. The vapourisation value is nearly seven times the fusion value because separating molecules completely from each other costs far more than loosening them.

**Principle of mixtures and the calorimeter**

An insulated calorimeter exchanges no heat with the surroundings, so by conservation of energy:

m₁c₁(T₁ − T_f) = (m₂c₂ + C_cal)(T_f − T₂)

for a hot solid dropped into a cold liquid. The calorimeter's own heat capacity is written as a **water equivalent** w, meaning the mass of water with the same heat capacity, so C_cal = w·c_water. Mixing up the two ways of writing the calorimeter term is a common error; w has units of kg, C_cal has units of J K⁻¹.

The sign discipline is simple: always subtract T_f from the initial temperature of the body that **loses** heat, and subtract T₂ from T_f for the body that **gains** it. Write the equation that way rather than moving signs around.

**Newton's law of cooling**

The rate of loss of heat is proportional to the excess of temperature over the surroundings, which gives dT/dt = −k(T − T_s). The cooling curve is steepest at the start and flattens exponentially as T approaches T_s. Two laboratory consequences: hot food cools faster in a wide shallow dish than in a deep bowl, and a small body cools faster than a large one because surface area per unit volume is larger. Newton's law is an approximation for moderate temperature differences; for large differences radiation is proportional to (T⁴ − T_s⁴), not to (T − T_s).

**Radiation from a black body**

A black body absorbs everything incident on it, and Kirchhoff's law says that means it also emits the most at every wavelength. The total power emitted per unit area is σT⁴, which is why a piece of charcoal glows while a piece of white paper at the same temperature does not. The total power from an area A is P = σAT⁴, and the energy radiated in a time t is σAT⁴t.

Wien's law gives the wavelength of maximum emission, λ_max = 2.898 × 10⁻³/T, which is how the surface temperature of the Sun is measured. It also explains the sequence as a body cools: red heat, then orange, then yellow, then white, because the peak moves from the long wavelengths into the visible band.

Kirchhoff's law has a second half that matters. Solar radiation is largely visible and passes through the atmosphere; outgoing terrestrial radiation is infrared, and CO₂, H₂O and CH₄ have infrared-active vibrational modes that absorb it. The atmosphere is therefore a fairly good emitter in the infrared. This selective absorption of outgoing radiation is the greenhouse effect; it is not the same as the warming inside a glasshouse, which is mostly the suppression of convection. Silvered surfaces reflect, and a polished metal surface is a poor emitter, which is why vacuum flasks are silvered on both walls.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> The derivations, the quantitative applications and the assumptions worth testing.

**Where α, β and γ come from**

If the volume of a solid is V and each linear dimension scales by (1 + αΔT), then V scales by (1 + αΔT)³ ≈ 1 + 3αΔT, giving γ = 3α for small expansions. The surface area scales as (1 + αΔT)² ≈ 1 + 2αΔT, giving β = 2α. These are approximations that hold for αΔT ≪ 1, which is why a metal bar expands to a nearly constant rate per degree and the relationship can be treated as linear over ordinary ranges.

For a fluid in a container, ΔV_apparent = V(γ_fluid − γ_container)ΔT, and a liquid may even show a negative apparent coefficient if the container expands faster than the liquid does.

**Deriving Newton's law of cooling from the radiation law**

Since the net emitted power is σA(T⁴ − T_s⁴), and T⁴ − T_s⁴ = (T − T_s)(T³ + T²T_s + TT_s² + T_s³), we can write dT/dt ∝ −(T − T_s) × [a roughly constant bracket] as long as T stays within a limited range near T_s. That bracket is why Newton's linear law is a good approximation for laboratory cooling but not for a body cooling from white heat to room temperature.

**Solar constant and the Earth's energy balance**

The solar constant is the solar power received per unit area perpendicular to the Sun's rays at the top of the atmosphere, about **1.4 × 10³ W m⁻²**. Two standard consequences:

1. **Power intercepted by the Earth.** The Earth intercepts light over a disc of radius R = 6.4 × 10⁶ m, area πR² = 1.29 × 10¹⁴ m². P = 1.4 × 10³ × 1.29 × 10¹⁴ = 1.8 × 10¹⁷ W.
2. **Average flux over the whole surface.** The same power is spread over 4πR², so the mean is 1.4 × 10³/4 = **350 W m⁻²**, the figure quoted for global average insolation.

The Earth's radiating surface area is 4πR², so the outgoing power is σ(278 K)⁴ × 1.29 × 10¹⁴ ≈ 1.2 × 10¹⁷ W, which is the basis of the Stefan-Boltzmann greenhouse estimate.

**Worked Example — mixing a hot solid with cold water**

A 0.5 kg copper block (c = 390 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) at 200 °C is dropped into 1 kg of water at 20 °C in a calorimeter of water equivalent 0.05 kg. Find the equilibrium temperature. Take c_water = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

1. Heat lost by copper: 0.5 × 390 × (200 − T) = 195(200 − T)
2. Heat gained by water plus calorimeter: [1 × 4200 + 0.05 × 4200](T − 20) = 4410(T − 20)
3. Set them equal: 195(200 − T) = 4410(T − 20)
4. 39 000 − 195T = 4410T − 88 200
5. 127 200 = 4605T → **T ≈ 27.6 °C**

Sanity check: the copper has a heat capacity of 195 J/K and the water 4200 J/K, so the water's thermal mass is 21 times larger and the final temperature must sit very close to 20 °C. Anything near 31 °C would need a copper block with far less heat capacity than 0.5 kg of copper.

**Worked Example — steam condensing into water**

100 g of steam at 100 °C condenses and then cools with the resulting water to 40 °C. Find the heat released. (L_v = 2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹, c_water = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.)

1. Condensation: Q₁ = mL = 0.1 × 2.26 × 10⁶ = 2.26 × 10⁵ J, at constant 100 °C.
2. Cooling from 100 °C to 40 °C: Q₂ = mcΔT = 0.1 × 4200 × 60 = 2.52 × 10⁴ J.
3. Total = 2.26 × 10⁵ + 2.52 × 10⁴ = **2.51 × 10⁵ J**.

The two stages have different formulas, and mixing them up is the standard error. Note the ΔT = 60 K — a temperature difference in °C is numerically the same in K.

**Worked Example — net radiation from a sphere**

A black sphere of radius 5 cm is at 727 °C in surroundings at 27 °C. Find the net power radiated. σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴.

1. T = 727 + 273 = 1000 K, T₀ = 27 + 273 = 300 K.
2. A = 4πr² = 4π(0.05)² = 3.14 × 10⁻² m².
3. T⁴ − T₀⁴ = (1000)⁴ − (300)⁴ = 1.0 × 10¹² − 8.1 × 10⁹ = 9.919 × 10¹¹ K⁴.
4. P_net = σA(T⁴ − T₀⁴) = (5.67 × 10⁻⁸)(3.14 × 10⁻²)(9.919 × 10¹¹)
5. P_net = **1.77 × 10³ W ≈ 1.77 kW**.

A common wrong answer subtracts the two temperatures and then takes the fourth power, giving a much smaller number. The fourth powers must be taken **first** and then subtracted.

**Worked Example — colour of a glowing body**

A metal is heated until the peak of its emission is at 600 nm. Find the temperature, and state the colour.

λ_max T = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m K → T = 2.898 × 10⁻³/6 × 10⁻⁷ = 4830 K. A peak at 600 nm is in the orange-red part of the spectrum, so the object appears dull red-orange. A peak at 500 nm (green) would be a body near 5800 K, the effective surface temperature of the Sun.

**Expansion gaps and the numerical reality**

For a 12 m steel rail with α = 12 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹, a 40 °C rise in summer gives ΔL = 12 × 12 × 10⁻⁶ × 40 = 5.76 × 10⁻³ m, about 6 mm per rail. Over a long track with rails laid end to end, the accumulated length is what forces the gaps, which is why rails are laid in sections with a gap between them and why the gap is narrowest in winter.

**The apparent specific heat and phase-change mixtures**

A more advanced calorimetry variant mixes three stages into one: a solid cools to its melting point, melts fully, then the resulting liquid heats up. Each stage uses Q = mL for the middle one and Q = mcΔT for the outer two, with c the specific heat of the solid for the first stage and of the liquid for the last. Writing one equation for the whole thing with three terms on each side is the reliable method, because it keeps the signs visible.

```mermaid
    E --> K

```

- **"Kelvin for anything that is powered."** T⁴, gas laws and Stefan-Boltzmann all want kelvin. ΔT is the exception — degrees and kelvins are the same size.
- **"Alpha, two alpha, three alpha."** Linear, areal, volume. If a question offers options, choose in that order; the coefficients are never equal to each other.
- **"Latent heat raises bonds, not temperature."** A plateau on a heating curve is a real physical statement, not a measurement artefact.
- **"Water is the moderator."** High c and high L, which is why the sea, a hot-water bottle and a car radiator all do the same job.
- **"Water is strange only near 4 °C."** It expands from 0 to 4 °C, is densest at 4 °C, and then behaves normally. There is no other common liquid like it.
- **"Fourth power, then subtract."** Net radiation is σA(T⁴ − T_s⁴), not σA(T − T_s)⁴.
- **"Absorber equals emitter."** Kirchhoff in one line; it connects a black body to the greenhouse effect.
- **Flashcard Q&A:**
  - *Why is the fourth power so important?* → P ∝ T⁴, so a 2 K rise in absolute temperature doubles the emission; small absolute temperatures are what make radiation negligible.
  - *Why does a bimetal strip bend?* → the two metals expand by different amounts; the larger α is on the outside of the curve.
  - *Why does ice float?* → the density of ice is about 0.917 g cm⁻³, roughly 9% less than water at 4 °C, so it floats on the denser liquid below.
  - *Cooling curve shape?* → steep at first, flattening as T approaches the surroundings; never actually reaches them in finite time in the ideal law.
  - *Water equivalent?* → the mass of water whose heat capacity equals the calorimeter's, so C_cal = w·c_water.
  - *Specific heat of water vs copper?* → 4200 against 390 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, a factor of about 11.

### 🎯 Exam Traps & Error Log

1. **Plugging a Celsius temperature into a T⁴.** 727 °C is 1000 K. Using 727 gives an answer wrong by a factor of nearly 4.
2. **Subtracting temperatures and then taking the fourth power.** Net radiation is σA(T⁴ − T₀⁴); the powers come first.
3. **Using Q = mcΔT during a phase change.** Between 0 °C and 0 °C while melting, ΔT = 0 and all the energy is latent, Q = mL.
4. **Using the specific heat of the solid after it has melted.** A block that melts must be heated with c_ice before, L during, and c_water after.
5. **Forgetting the calorimeter's own heat capacity.** If the problem gives a water equivalent, the gained heat is (m₂c₂ + w·c_water)(T_f − T₂).
6. **Taking γ = 2α.** It is γ = 3α, β = 2α. A vessel filled to the brim with any liquid other than water does not overflow.
7. **Writing water's maximum density as 0 °C.** It is 4 °C, and between 0 and 4 °C water contracts on heating.
8. **Confusing specific heat capacity with heat capacity.** c is per kilogram and is a material property; C = mc depends on the sample.
9. **Grams in a kilogram formula.** 0.5 g of copper is 5 × 10⁻⁴ kg, not 0.5.
10. **Applying Kirchhoff backwards.** A surface that reflects well is a poor emitter; a black body absorbs and emits best.
11. **Saying radiation needs a medium.** It is the one mode that travels through a vacuum.
12. **Saying water expands uniformly with temperature.** It does not between 0 and 4 °C, which is the whole point of the anomaly.

### 🧪 Self-Test — 8 Questions with Worked Answers

Attempt each one on paper before reading the solution. All of them use formulas from the three tiers above.

1. **A copper rod of length 2 m at 20 °C is heated to 100 °C. Find the increase in length, given α = 1.7 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹ for copper.**

   ΔL = L₀αΔT = 2 × 1.7 × 10⁻⁵ × 80 = 2.72 × 10⁻³ m = 2.72 mm. Notice ΔT in K equals ΔT in °C here, since the scale offset cancels in a difference. The copper rod lengthens by about 3 mm for an 80 degree rise.

2. **How much heat is needed to melt 500 g of ice at 0 °C and then raise the resulting water to 20 °C? (L_f = 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹, c_water = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.)**

   Melting: Q₁ = 0.5 × 3.34 × 10⁵ = 1.67 × 10⁵ J.
   Heating: Q₂ = 0.5 × 4200 × 20 = 4.2 × 10⁴ J.
   Total = 1.67 × 10⁵ + 4.2 × 10⁴ = **2.09 × 10⁵ J**. The two stages use different formulas and both must be added; the temperature of the ice does not change during the first stage.

3. **A body cools from 80 °C to 40 °C in 10 minutes in air at 20 °C. How long does it take to reach 20 °C, given Newton's law of cooling?**

   Newton's law says (T − T_s) falls geometrically, so the excess temperature is halved in a fixed interval. From 80 to 40 °C, the excess went from 60 to 20, a factor of 3, in 10 minutes. Halving 20 to 10 is the same factor of 2, not 3, so treat it as two equal steps: 20 → 10 takes the same time as 10 → 5, and the intervals are equal. From 40 °C (excess 20) to 20 °C (excess 0) is two halvings, so **20 minutes**. A well-posed version of this question should give the target excess; as written the cleanest route is the equal-interval property of the exponential decay.

4. **A black body at 1000 K has surface area 0.1 m². Find the radiated power, then the power if the temperature is raised to 1100 K.**

   P₁ = σAT⁴ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 0.1 × (1000)⁴ = 5.67 × 10³ W.
   P₂ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 0.1 × (1100)⁴ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁹ × 1.4641 × 10¹² = 8.30 × 10³ W.
   The rise is 10%, the power rise about 46%, because the factor is (1.1)⁴ = 1.46. This is the practical meaning of the fourth power.

5. **A 2 kg brass block (c = 380 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) at 100 °C is placed in 3 kg of water at 20 °C. Neglect the calorimeter. Find the equilibrium temperature.**

   Heat lost = 2 × 380 × (100 − T) = 760(100 − T).
   Heat gained = 3 × 4200 × (T − 20) = 12 600(T − 20).
   76 000 − 760T = 12 600T − 252 000.
   328 000 = 13 360T → **T ≈ 24.6 °C**. The water's thermal mass is 16.6 times the brass's, so the result must sit near 20 °C.

6. **Explain, in terms of the hydrogen bond, why ice is less dense than liquid water.**

   In liquid water each molecule forms roughly four hydrogen bonds in an open, tetrahedral arrangement. This open structure leaves empty space, so the liquid is less densely packed than one might expect. When water freezes, each molecule becomes part of a rigid, ordered hexagonal lattice with more empty space still. The ordered lattice is the less dense arrangement, which is why ice floats. The same open structure explains why liquid water between 0 and 4 °C becomes denser as it warms — the bonds break gradually and the molecules pack more closely before ordinary expansion takes over.

7. **A metal surface has an area of 20 cm², is at 500 °C, and radiates into surroundings at 25 °C. Find the net loss of energy per second.**

   A = 20 cm² = 2 × 10⁻³ m². T = 773 K, T₀ = 298 K.
   T⁴ = 3.57 × 10¹¹ K⁴, since 773² = 5.975 × 10⁵. T₀⁴ = 7.89 × 10⁹ K⁴, since 298² = 8.88 × 10⁴.
   Difference = 3.57 × 10¹¹ − 0.0789 × 10¹¹ = 3.491 × 10¹¹ K⁴.
   P = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 2 × 10⁻³ × 3.4916 × 10¹¹ = **39.6 W**.

8. **A flat plate is at 300 K, another identical plate at 600 K, in a large evacuated chamber whose walls are at 0 K. Compare the rate at which they lose heat to the walls.**

   The 300 K plate radiates σA(300)⁴ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 8.1 × 10⁹ = 459 W/m². The 600 K plate radiates σA(600)⁴ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 1.296 × 10¹¹ = 7 348 W/m². The 600 K plate loses heat **16 times** faster, since (600/300)⁴ = 2⁴ = 16. Doubling the absolute temperature quadruples nothing here — it multiplies the emission by sixteen, which is why an object at 600 K glows visibly while one at 300 K does not.

### 💡 Pro Tips

1. **Convert to kelvin the moment you see a fourth power, a gas law or an ideal-gas question.** Write the conversion on the page; it is two seconds and it prevents the most common numerical error in the chapter.
2. **Split phase-change problems into stages on paper before writing anything.** Label them "solid cools", "melts", "liquid heats", then one equation with three terms. It removes all sign anxiety.
3. **Use the thermal-mass ratio as a sanity check.** If a large mass of water absorbs heat from a small mass of metal, the answer must be much closer to the water's starting temperature than to the midpoint. A midpoint answer almost always means an arithmetic slip.
4. **Write the three coefficients in the order α, β, γ and use α.** For any question about a rod, a plate or a block, α is the one you need; reach for γ only when the shape changes uniformly.
5. **Memorise water's four reference numbers together:** c = 4200, L_f = 3.34 × 10⁵, L_v = 2.26 × 10⁶, ρ_max at 4 °C. Together they answer a large share of the numericals in this topic.
6. **In net-radiation questions, take both fourth powers, then subtract.** Writing the intermediate values T⁴ and T₀⁴ on paper kills the error.
7. **Answer "why" questions with the mechanism, not the label.** A bimetallic strip bends because of unequal linear expansion, not because it is a "thermostat"; a lake survives because of the density maximum and floating ice, not because of "the anomalous expansion of water" as a phrase.
8. **Use Kirchhoff for any question comparing a surface to a black body.** If the material absorbs well it emits well, and that single law covers absorptivity, emissivity and the greenhouse argument.
9. **Keep Newton's law of cooling and Stefan's law in separate boxes.** They are used in different questions — cooling curves versus net exchange — and mixing them produces answers with no physical meaning.
10. **Do the "what happens if" comparisons verbally before choosing an option.** Doubling T, halving a length, switching a container's material: run the sentence, then pick.
