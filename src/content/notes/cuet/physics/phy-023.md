# Ray Optics

Ray optics treats light as a straight line travelling in straight lines until it meets a boundary. That approximation is valid whenever the obstacle or aperture is much larger than the wavelength, which covers almost everything in daily life: a mirror, a spectacle lens, a camera, the shadow of a hand. The chapter is then four laws, one sign convention, and a handful of formulas — and the single biggest source of lost marks is the sign convention, not the physics.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

**The two sign conventions — use the Cartesian one, everywhere**

*For mirrors* (New Cartesian, pole as origin, incident light travels left to right, all distances measured from the pole, positive in the direction of incident light):
$$\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}$$

*For lenses* (same convention, light left to right, positive to the right):
$$\frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}$$

Because the light travels left to right in both cases, a real object always has $u < 0$. A real image in front of a mirror or behind a lens has $v > 0$ for a lens, $v < 0$ for a mirror. Convex mirror and convex lens have $f > 0$; concave mirror and concave lens have $f < 0$.

**The four sign rules, memorised as signs**

| Element | Focal length | Power | Acts as |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concave mirror | $f < 0$ | — | converging |
| Convex mirror | $f > 0$ | — | diverging |
| Convex lens | $f > 0$ | $P > 0$ | converging |
| Concave lens | $f < 0$ | $P < 0$ | diverging |

**The five formulas**

| Situation | Formula | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Mirror | $\dfrac{1}{v} + \dfrac{1}{u} = \dfrac{1}{f}$ | $f = R/2$ for a spherical mirror |
| Thin lens | $\dfrac{1}{v} - \dfrac{1}{u} = \dfrac{1}{f}$ | — |
| Magnification (mirror) | $m = -\dfrac{v}{u} = \dfrac{h_i}{h_o}$ | $m < 0$ ⇒ inverted |
| Magnification (lens) | $m = \dfrac{v}{u}$ | $m < 0$ ⇒ inverted |
| Refraction | $n_1\sin i = n_2\sin r$ | $n = c/v$ |
| Critical angle | $\sin C = \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$ | denser to rarer only |
| Apparent depth | $d_{app} = d\left(1 - \dfrac{1}{n}\right)$ | viewed from the denser side |
| Prism, minimum deviation | $n = \dfrac{\sin\left[(A + \delta_m)/2\right]}{\sin(A/2)}$ | $i = e$ at minimum |
| Lensmaker's | $\dfrac{1}{f} = (n-1)\left(\dfrac{1}{R_1} - \dfrac{1}{R_2}\right)$ | $R_1$ first surface, $R_2$ second |
| Lenses in contact | $\dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{1}{f_1} + \dfrac{1}{f_2}$ | powers add |

**Total internal reflection** happens only when light goes from denser to rarer *and* the incidence angle exceeds the critical angle. From glass (n = 1.5) to air, $C = 41.8°$. Below that angle the light refracts out; above it, all the light is reflected internally — the reason a diamond sparkles and optical fibre works.

**The spectral order** is red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet from long wavelength to short. In every dispersing medium, violet bends most and red least, because $n$ decreases with wavelength. This single fact gives the position of every colour in a spectrum and the order of the colours in a rainbow.

**The two least-distance values**

$D = 25$ cm is the least distance of distinct vision. Normal near point is 25 cm for a relaxed eye.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

**Reflection and the ray model**

A ray striking a plane or spherical surface is reflected with angle of incidence equal to angle of reflection, both measured from the normal. The normal to a spherical mirror is along the radius, so the pole, the centre of curvature $C$ at $2f$, and the focus $F$ at $f$ all sit on the same axis, and a ray through $C$ or $F$ retraces its path. Those three ray rules let you draw any spherical-mirror image, and drawing the image is faster and more reliable than algebra when a question is conceptual.

**The mirror object-position map** — learn this table and the mirror chapter is finished

| Object position (concave) | Image position | Nature | Size |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beyond C | Between F and C | real, inverted | diminished |
| At C | At C | real, inverted | same |
| Between C and F | Beyond C | real, inverted | magnified |
| At F | at infinity | real, inverted | highly magnified |
| Inside F | behind the mirror | virtual, erect | magnified |

A **convex mirror** always gives a virtual, erect, diminished image behind the mirror, wherever the object is. That is why it is used as a rear-view mirror: the field of view is always wider, and the image is always upright and small.

**Refraction and Snell's law**

$n_1 \sin i = n_2 \sin r$, with $n = c/v$ the refractive index (dimensionless, $M^0L^0T^0$). Snell's law follows from the wavefront picture: the part of the wavefront entering the second medium first leaves part of it behind, so the wavefront bends. If the incoming wavefront makes angle $\theta_1$ with the normal, Huygens' construction gives $\sin i/\sin r = v_1/v_2$, which is Snell's law. Light bends **towards** the normal entering a denser medium and **away from** it entering a rarer one.

**The one-glance sign rule for a refracting spherical surface** is the lens formula with $n$ inserted:

$$\frac{n_2}{v} - \frac{n_1}{u} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R}$$

This is the building block the lensmaker's formula is made of. It is worth learning because it lets you solve a single-surface problem — a glass sphere, a hemisphere, a fish bowl — that has no direct lens analogue.

**Lens types and the lensmaker's formula**

A convex lens is thicker in the middle: $R_1 > 0$, $R_2 < 0$, so $(1/R_1 - 1/R_2)$ is positive, and $1/f > 0$, i.e. $f > 0$. A concave lens is thinner in the middle, the bracket is negative, and $f < 0$. The formula says a lens gets stronger as its surfaces get more curved (smaller $R$) and as $n$ rises. **Power** is $P = 1/f$ in dioptres; a +4 D lens is a convex lens of focal length 0.25 m. Spectacle prescriptions are exactly this: minus lenses for myopia (the eye's power is too strong), plus lenses for hypermetropia.

**Thick lenses and the lensmaker's correction**

The simple lensmaker's formula assumes a thin lens. For a lens of thickness $t$, a thickness term is added: $\dfrac{1}{f} = (n-1)\left[\dfrac{1}{R_1} - \dfrac{1}{R_2} + \dfrac{(n-1)t}{nR_1R_2}\right]$. The physical content: the ray refracts at four surfaces, not two, and the extra two do work. This is why thick glass spheres have shorter focal lengths than the thin formula predicts, and why thick-lens problems in the syllabus are not just arithmetic.

**Total internal reflection**

Refraction at the critical angle puts the refracted ray along the boundary ($r = 90°$), giving $\sin C = n_2/n_1$. Since $\sin C < 1$ requires $n_1 > n_2$, TIR can only occur from denser to rarer. Examples in the syllabus: glass-to-air at 41.8°, diamond-to-air at 24.4° (n = 2.42), water-to-air at 48.6°.

**Optical fibre** is a total internal reflection device. A core of index $n_1$ inside a cladding of lower index $n_2$ traps light by repeated TIR along the core. The **numerical aperture** is $\text{NA} = \sqrt{n_1^2 - n_2^2}$, and light will enter the fibre only from within the acceptance angle $\theta_0$ where $\sin\theta_0 = \text{NA}/n_0$. Larger NA means a wider acceptance cone and easier coupling — which is why low-water-peak and graded-index designs, with high NA, were developed for long-haul links.

**Apparent depth and parallax**

An object at depth $d$ under water appears at $d(1 - 1/n)$, i.e. shallower by $d(1 - 1/n)$. It looks shallower because the light reaching the eye has bent away from the normal on its way out, and the eye traces it back in a straight line that meets the surface higher up. Parallax — the apparent shift of an object when the eye is moved sideways — is how the shift is *measured* in the laboratory, by matching it against a reference needle. The formula, note, assumes viewing from the denser side, which is the usual case for a submerged object seen from above. Viewed instead from the rarer side — an object in air seen from underwater — the object appears *deeper* than it is, because the light bends towards the normal on the way in, and the eye traces the ray back to a point further away. The general relation covering any viewing angle is $d_{app}/d = \dfrac{\cos i}{\sqrt{n^2 - \sin^2 i}}$ with $i$ the angle of incidence in the denser medium and $n$ the index of the denser medium.

**Prisms**

A prism deviates light because each face is inclined to the other, and a ray leaves at a different angle to its normals than it entered. The **angle of the prism** $A$ is the angle between the two refracting faces. The **angle of deviation** $\delta$ is the angle between the incident and emergent rays' original directions. $\delta$ depends on $i$, but it has a minimum, occurring when $i = e$, the ray passing symmetrically. At that angle,

$$n = \frac{\sin\left[(A + \delta_m)/2\right]}{\sin(A/2)}$$

and because $i = e$, the internal refraction angles are both $A/2$. If the prism is in air the standard result for a thin prism is $\delta_m \approx (n - 1)A$. The practical importance: a prism in the minimum-deviation position is the *only* stable orientation for a prism spectrometer, and a prism spectroscope — unlike a grating spectroscope — does not suffer from second-order overlap, so it is used to study the far-ultraviolet where grating reflectivity has fallen off.

**Optical instruments**

- **Simple microscope (magnifying glass)**: $M = 1 + D/f$ with the final image at the near point; $M = D/f$ for a relaxed eye at infinity. Maximum magnification for a lens in air is $1 + D/f_{\min}$ with $f \approx D$, giving about 2.
- **Compound microscope**: $M \approx \dfrac{D}{f_o}\left(\dfrac{L}{f_e} + 1\right)$, with objective focal length $f_o$ small and eyepiece focal length $f_e$ small but larger than $f_o$. It resolves features about 200 nm across, which is the practical limit set by the wavelength of visible light.
- **Astronomical telescope**, normal adjustment (final image at infinity, objective and eyepiece focal lengths positive): $m = -f_o/f_e$. The minus sign means the image is **inverted** — which is why a telescope view is upside down. Magnifying power in normal adjustment is $f_o/f_e$; in near point it is $1 + f_o/f_e$.
- The telescope's light-gathering power goes as $D^2$ (area), and its resolving power as $D$ — both a step-function effect, which is why telescopes are built big.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

**Why the normal is at the pole, and how a ray diagram is read**

The normal to a spherical surface at a point is the line from the centre of curvature to that point. In the paraxial region all these normals are nearly parallel to the axis, which is exactly why the paraxial (Gaussian) approximation is a small-angle approximation: the mirror formula, the lens formula and the lensmaker's formula are all derived under $i, r \ll 1$ in radians. A ray diagram is a geometric statement about angles, and the formulas are the algebraic translation of that statement. A ray through $C$ is normal to the surface and retraces; a ray through $F$ reflects parallel to the axis; a ray parallel to the axis reflects through $F$. These three, applied in any order, fix the image of any point.

**Spherical aberration, and why mirrors are parabolic**

A spherical mirror brings rays to a point only approximately; marginal rays focus closer to the mirror than paraxial rays. This is **spherical aberration**, and it blurs the image of a wide-aperture mirror. A **parabolic** mirror reflects every ray parallel to the axis to exactly one focus, which is why large telescope primaries are parabolic. The trade-off: a parabolic mirror focuses parallel rays to a point but *not* a range of angles to that point, so it is not used off-axis.

**Chromatic aberration in lenses**

A lens has a different focal length for each wavelength, because $n$ varies with wavelength ($n_v > n_r$ for glass). The image of a white object is therefore a spread of coloured images, and the **chromatic aberration** is measured by the difference $\Delta f = f_v - f_r$, and more usefully by the **power dispersion** $\omega = (f_v - f_r)/f_d$, where $d$ is the mean of the two. A convex lens is called a converging lens and a concave a diverging lens regardless of wavelength, but the *amount* of convergence depends on colour.

Two designs fix it. An **achromatic doublet** is a convex lens of crown glass (low dispersion) cemented to a concave lens of flint glass (high dispersion), with the dispersions cancelling while the net power stays positive. The condition is $\omega_1/\omega_2 = (n_{d1} - 1)/(n_{d2} - 1)$ in terms of dispersive powers. An **aplanatic lens** corrects spherical aberration as well, using specially chosen curvatures. An achromat still has residual "secondary spectrum" — a small spread of the blue end — because two glass types cannot cancel dispersion at three wavelengths exactly.

**A worked mirror problem, all the algebra**

An object stands 30 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 15 cm. Under the Cartesian convention with light left to right and the mirror facing left, the object is to the left of the pole so $u = -30$ cm, and the concave mirror has $f = -15$ cm (focal length is negative because the focus is to the left of the pole, in the direction of incident light).

$\dfrac{1}{v} = \dfrac{1}{f} - \dfrac{1}{u} = \dfrac{1}{-15} - \dfrac{1}{-30} = -\dfrac{2}{30} + \dfrac{1}{30} = -\dfrac{1}{30}$, so $v = -30$ cm.
$m = -\dfrac{v}{u} = -\dfrac{-30}{-30} = -1$.

So the image is 30 cm in front of the mirror, real, inverted, and the same size — the object at $C = 2f$ gives its image at $C$. Now do the trap: if you write $u = +30$, the formula gives $1/v = 1/(-15) - 1/30 = -1/10$, so $v = -10$ cm, $|m| = 1/3$, "diminished" — and the answer is wrong. The single missing minus sign changed the image from same-size to diminished. Fix $u$ first, always.

**A worked lens problem, sign by sign**

An object 20 cm from a convex lens of focal length 10 cm, image on a screen at 20 cm behind it. $u = -20$ cm, $f = +10$ cm.
$\dfrac{1}{v} = \dfrac{1}{f} + \dfrac{1}{u} = \dfrac{1}{10} - \dfrac{1}{20} = \dfrac{1}{20}$, so $v = +20$ cm. $m = v/u = 20/(-20) = -1$.
Real, inverted, same size, and the object is at $2f$ because $2f = 20$ cm. Consistent.

Now the concave case. A concave lens of focal length $-10$ cm, object at 20 cm, $u = -20$:
$\dfrac{1}{v} = \dfrac{1}{-10} + \dfrac{1}{-20} = -\dfrac{3}{20}$, so $v = -\dfrac{20}{3} = -6.7$ cm.
$m = v/u = (-6.7)/(-20) = +0.33$.
Virtual, erect, diminished, on the same side as the object — the signature of a diverging lens for any object position. Note the lens formula: $1/v = 1/f + 1/u$, and the mirror formula: $1/v = 1/f - 1/u$. Do not carry one into the other.

**Silvered lens — refraction twice, reflection once**

A thin convex lens of focal length $f$ is silvered on one side. Light refracts at the first surface, again at the second, reflects off the silvering, and refracts back out. Each pass through the lens contributes $1/f$ and the reflection contributes $1/f_m = 2/R$, so the equivalent focal length satisfies

$$\frac{1}{f_{eq}} = 2\times\frac{1}{f} + \frac{1}{f_m}$$

and the system behaves as a concave mirror. For a plano-convex lens of refractive index $n$ with the plane side silvered, $f_m = \infty$ (a plane mirror) and $f_{eq} = f/2$, so the system is twice as strong. This is a standard two-mark idea and the algebra is one line once you accept that "each traverse of a lens counts once".

**The prism at minimum deviation, worked**

An equilateral prism ($A = 60°$) has minimum deviation $\delta_m = 60°$ for some material. Then $i = e$, and the internal angles are $r_1 = r_2 = A/2 = 30°$.

$n = \dfrac{\sin[(A + \delta_m)/2]}{\sin(A/2)} = \dfrac{\sin[(60+60)/2]}{\sin 30°} = \dfrac{\sin 60°}{0.5} = \dfrac{0.866}{0.5} = 1.73$.

Sanity check by the thin-prism approximation: $\delta_m \approx (n-1)A = 0.73 \times 60° = 44°$, which is less than the exact 60° because the approximation ignores the finite apex. Both are the right order; if a question's answer is far from $(n-1)A$, suspect the wrong branch.

**Ray tracing versus algebra — when to use which**

Ray tracing wins for "real or virtual, erect or inverted, magnified or diminished" and for the object-position map. Algebra wins for "find the exact position and the exact magnification". A multiple-choice question usually only needs the classification, and a ray diagram takes thirty seconds where the algebra takes two minutes and can go wrong on a sign.

**The camera, projected from the lens equations**

A camera is just a converging lens with a film or sensor at a distance $v$ behind it. For a distant object $u \to -\infty$, $v \to f$ and the image is real, inverted and (nearly) the same size as the object at $f$. Bringing the object closer makes $v$ exceed $f$, and the image grows — which is why a macro setting magnifies. The blur circle from a point not at the focus is $c = \frac{D}{v - f}\delta$ where $D$ is the aperture and $\delta$ the defocus; stopping down (small $D$) sharpens at the cost of needing a longer exposure. That trade-off is aperture, and it is physics, not a camera manual.

**Binoculars and periscopes**

In a telescope, the objective forms a real inverted image in its focal plane and the eyepiece magnifies that. An erecting prism or a pair of mirrors folds the path and turns the image upright, at the cost of inverting it laterally and reversing it front-to-back, which is exactly what a pair of mirrors does twice. In a periscope, two plane mirrors at 45° separated by a distance equal to the tube length return the view along the original direction with a lateral displacement, and the image is laterally inverted — which is why text in a periscope reads backwards. A submarine periscope adds a convex lens to also correct the upright orientation.

- **"Same side as object is virtual, opposite side is real."** A virtual image always forms on the object's side of the element; a real image forms on the other side, where light actually goes. That one sentence classifies every image in the chapter.
- **"Angle to the normal, not to the surface."** Every angle in optics — incidence, refraction, deviation, critical angle — is measured from the normal. Measured from the surface, it is the complement, and the answer changes.
- **"Convex converges, concave diverges"** — but only for a lens surrounded by air. A convex lens in water is *weaker*, because the relative index drops. Lens power depends on the surrounding medium, and the formula is $1/f = (n_{lens}/n_{medium} - 1)(\ldots)$.
- **"Red bends least."** Dispersion order VIBGYOR from the violet end; in every medium $n_v > n_r$, so violet deviates most.
- **"Achromat cancels dispersion, not colour."** A doublet brings two wavelengths to a common focus; the rest still fan out slightly.
- **"230 V, $D = 25$ cm."** The least distance of distinct vision is 25 cm and it sets every magnifier formula; a relaxed eye looking through a magnifier at $D$ gets $1 + D/f$.
- **"Telescope images are inverted."** $m = -f_o/f_e$; the minus is physical, and it is why every astronomical instrument has an erecting prism or mirror.
- **Flashcard Q&A:**
  - *Object at C in a concave mirror?* → image at C, real, inverted, same size.
  - *Object at F in a concave mirror?* → image at infinity.
  - *Convex mirror, any object position?* → virtual, erect, diminished, behind the mirror.
  - *Sign of $u$ for a real object?* → negative, always, in the Cartesian convention.
  - *Sign of $m$ and what it means?* → $m < 0$ means inverted, which means real.
  - *Apparent depth in water (n = 4/3)?* → $d/4$, so a 4 m pool looks 1 m deep.
  - *Critical angle glass-to-air?* → $\arcsin(1/1.5) = 41.8°$.
  - *Which colour has the largest refractive index?* → violet.
  - *Telescope in normal adjustment?* → final image at infinity, $M = f_o/f_e$, inverted.
  - *Why two rainbows?* → the outer one is from internal reflection, giving one phase reversal less, so the colours reverse.

### 🎯 Exam Traps & Error Log

1. **Writing $u = +20$ cm for a real object.** It must be negative in the Cartesian convention. This one sign error changes a same-size image into a diminished one and is the most common error in the whole chapter.
2. **Using $1/v - 1/u = 1/f$ for a mirror** or $1/v + 1/u = 1/f$ for a lens. The two have opposite signs because the reflected ray reverses direction. A mnemonic that works: work it out once for a case you know (object at C) and the sign follows.
3. **Reading a magnification of $m < 0$ as a shrink factor.** $m = -1$ means inverted and the *same size*; the magnitude gives the size, the sign gives the orientation.
4. **Measuring angles from the surface instead of the normal.** A ray at 30° to the surface is at 60° to the normal.
5. **Claiming TIR happens in water or glass going into a denser medium.** It requires denser to rarer and $i > C$; going into a denser medium, the ray always refracts out.
6. **Writing $\sin C = n_1/n_2$** instead of $n_2/n_1$. Since $\sin C < 1$, the ratio must be the one less than 1, which is the rarer over the denser.
7. **Using the thin-prism formula $\delta = (n-1)A$ at minimum deviation for a large-angle prism** and treating it as exact. It is an approximation; the exact relation is $n = \sin[(A+\delta_m)/2]/\sin(A/2)$.
8. **Assuming the image of a convex mirror can be real** for any object position. It cannot; the image is always virtual, erect, diminished.
9. **Confusing the sign of the magnifying power of a telescope.** It is negative, the image is inverted, and putting a minus in the wrong place flips the description.
10. **Forgetting that $D = 25$ cm is the near point, not the focal length,** or dropping the "+1" in $M = 1 + D/f$ for a simple microscope.
11. **Assuming chromatic aberration affects all colours equally.** Dispersion varies with wavelength, so it is the blue end that fans out; the achromatic doublet exploits exactly this.
12. **Treating a silvered lens as a plain mirror.** It refracts at each pass as well as reflecting, so the power is $2/f + 1/f_m$ — roughly double for a thin lens.

### 🧪 Self-Test — 8 Questions with Worked Answers

**1. A convex lens of focal length 15 cm forms a real image of an object placed 25 cm in front of it. Find the image distance and magnification, and state the image's nature.**
$u = -25$ cm, $f = +15$ cm. The lens formula in the form $1/v = 1/f + 1/u$: $1/v = 1/15 - 1/25 = (5 - 3)/75 = 2/75$, so $v = 37.5$ cm.
$m = v/u = 37.5/(-25) = -1.5$.
$v > 0$ so the image is real and behind the lens; $m < 0$ so it is inverted; $|m| = 1.5 > 1$ so it is magnified. The image is 1.5 times the object's height, inverted, on a screen 37.5 cm behind the lens.
Check: the object is between $f$ (15 cm) and $2f$ (30 cm), which is exactly the region that gives a real, inverted, magnified image. The object-position map and the algebra agree.

**2. An object is placed 12 cm from a concave mirror of focal length 20 cm. Find the image position, magnification and nature.**
$u = -12$ cm, $f = -20$ cm.
$\dfrac{1}{v} = \dfrac{1}{f} - \dfrac{1}{u} = -\dfrac{1}{20} + \dfrac{1}{12} = \dfrac{-3 + 5}{60} = \dfrac{2}{60}$, so $v = 30$ cm.
$m = -\dfrac{v}{u} = -\dfrac{30}{-12} = +2.5$.
Positive $m$ means erect, and since the object was inside the focus, the image is virtual and forms **behind** the mirror, magnified 2.5 times. This is the "face in a shaving mirror at 12 cm with 20 cm focal length" case, which is why a shaving mirror is concave and not convex: you want a big, upright, virtual image of your face.

**3. A ray of light passes from glass (n = 1.5) to water (n = 1.33) at an incidence angle of 30°. Find the angle of refraction, and state whether total internal reflection is possible at this interface.**
$n_1 \sin i = n_2 \sin r$: $1.5 \times \sin 30° = 1.33 \sin r$, so $\sin r = 0.75/1.33 = 0.564$, and $r = 34.3°$.
The two conditions for TIR are both needed here. Glass is denser than water, so TIR is possible in principle, with a critical angle given by $\sin C = 1.33/1.5 = 0.887$, i.e. $C = 62.5°$. But the incidence angle is only 30°, and $30° < 62.5°$, so no TIR occurs and the ray refracts into the water. The lesson: a rarer second medium makes TIR *possible*, not automatic; the incidence angle must still exceed the critical angle.

**4. A coin lies at the bottom of a 6 m deep water tank (n = 4/3). Viewed from directly above, how deep does it appear?**
$d_{app} = d\left(1 - \dfrac{1}{n}\right) = 6\left(1 - \dfrac{3}{4}\right) = 6 \times \dfrac{1}{4} = 1.5$ m.
So the tank looks 1.5 m deep instead of 6 m. The apparent shift is $6 - 1.5 = 4.5$ m.
This is why it is dangerous to enter a shallow-looking pool, and why a diver's apparent depth is less than the true depth. For general viewing the formula is $d_{app}/d = (\cos i)/(\sqrt{n^2 - \sin^2 i})$ with $i$ the viewing angle, and the simple $1 - 1/n$ result is the near-normal case.

**5. An equilateral prism (A = 60°) of refractive index 1.5 has a minimum deviation $\delta_m$. Find $\delta_m$.**
$\delta_m = 2i - A$ with $i = e$ and the internal angle $r_1 = A/2 = 30°$.
$\sin i = n \sin 30° = 1.5 \times 0.5 = 0.75$, so $i = 48.6°$.
$\delta_m = 2(48.6°) - 60° = 97.2° - 60° = 37.2°$.
Cross-check with the exact formula: $n = \sin[(A + \delta_m)/2]/\sin(A/2) \Rightarrow \sin[(60+37.2)/2] = 1.5 \times \sin 30° = 0.75 \Rightarrow (60+37.2)/2 = 48.6°$. Consistent. The thin-prism approximation would give $(n-1)A = 30°$, lower than the exact 37.2° because it ignores the finite apex.

**6. A convex lens of focal length 20 cm and a concave lens of focal length 30 cm are in contact. Find the equivalent focal length and power, and describe the combination.**
$\dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{1}{20} - \dfrac{1}{30} = \dfrac{3 - 2}{60} = \dfrac{1}{60}$, so $f_{eq} = 60$ cm.
Power: $P = 1/f$ in dioptres $= 1/0.6 = 1.67$ D. Or directly $P = P_1 + P_2 = 1/0.20 - 1/0.30 = 5 - 3.33 = +1.67$ D.
The combination is **converging** (net positive power) and weaker than the convex lens alone. Note that powers in dioptres add linearly while focal lengths do not — the commonest slip is to add 20 and 30 to get 50 cm, which is wrong.
Had the two been a convex and a concave lens of *equal* focal length, the powers would cancel exactly and the combination would be a plane parallel slab with no net power.

**7. A telescope has an objective of focal length 100 cm and an eyepiece of focal length 5 cm. Find the magnifying power in normal adjustment, state the image nature, and find the magnifying power when the final image is at the least distance of distinct vision.**
Normal adjustment (final image at infinity): $m = -f_o/f_e = -100/5 = -20$. The image is **real, inverted and highly diminished**, as an astronomical image must be, so the eye or a camera must present it the right way round.
Final image at the near point: $m = 1 + f_o/f_e = 1 + 20 = 21$, still inverted.
The increase from 20 to 21 is small — the classic "work the eyepiece at the near point if you are myopic" adjustment. The angular magnification is 20, so a crater a hundredth of the Moon's apparent size becomes visible, which is what a telescope is for.

**8. A convex lens of focal length 10 cm is silvered on its plane side. Find the equivalent focal length of the system and its power.**
A plane mirror has $f_m = \infty$, so $1/f_m = 0$. The light passes through the lens, reflects off the plane, and passes back:
$\dfrac{1}{f_{eq}} = \dfrac{2}{f} + \dfrac{1}{f_m} = \dfrac{2}{10} + 0 = 0.2$, so $f_{eq} = 5$ cm.
Power $P = 1/0.05 = 20$ D — twice the lens's own 10 D. The system behaves as a concave mirror of focal length 5 cm, i.e. as a strong converging reflector. This is the standard "silvered lens" result and the "twice the traverse" reasoning is the whole of it.

### 💡 Pro Tips

1. **Write the sign convention line before you write any numbers.** "Light travels left to right, so a real object has $u < 0$" takes five seconds and prevents the single most frequent error in the chapter.
2. **Classify the object position first.** "The object is between $f$ and $2f$, so a real, inverted, magnified image" takes five seconds and tells you most of the answer before the algebra starts.
3. **Check $m$ against the classification.** If the map says magnified and $|m| < 1$, you have a sign error, not a physics error.
4. **Draw the ray diagram for any "state the nature" question.** Three rules — through $C$, through $F$, parallel to the axis — take less time than the algebra and cannot go wrong on a sign.
5. **Use $1/v = 1/f \pm 1/u$ deliberately.** Plus for a lens, minus for a mirror, and the reason is the direction the light travels in the formula. Write which one you are using on every line.
6. **Add powers in dioptres, not focal lengths.** Converting to $1/f$ before combining is the fix, and it costs one line.
7. **Memorise $\sin C = n_2/n_1$ and always sanity-check that the right side is less than 1.** If it is not, the ratio is inverted and the answer is nonsense.
8. **Keep $D = 25$ cm visible in every instrument problem.** It is the pivot between the "at the near point" and "at infinity" versions of nearly every formula in that section.
