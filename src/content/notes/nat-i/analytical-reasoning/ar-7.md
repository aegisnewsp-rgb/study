# Seating Arrangements

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Seating Arrangements** is the Analytical Reasoning sub-topic where candidates decode the spatial order of people (and occasionally objects) around linear rows, circular or hexagonal tables, rectangular dual-row grids, or facing-centre circles, using only the written clues. Success depends on translating every sentence into a fixed positional relation — left of, right of, opposite, immediate neighbour, or between — before drawing the frame.

The fastest solving tool is the **directional grid method**: anchor one person to a definite seat from an absolute clue (e.g., "A sits at the left end" or "P faces the centre from the north seat"), then layer each relative clue onto that frame.

- Anchor first with an absolute clue, never start with a relative clue.
- Mirror left-right when the circle is facing the centre — your right becomes the viewer's left.
- Use gender/profession filters to eliminate options before full placement.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Setup Logic
Every seating puzzle reduces to six positional relations: immediate left, immediate right, second-to-left, opposite, end-of-row, and between. A clue such as "B sits immediately to the left of C" places B and C adjacent in that exact order. A weaker clue such as "D sits two places to the right of E" only fixes their **relative** gap, not their absolute seat, so the frame must already have an anchor before such clues become useful.

#### Frame-Building Order
The reliable sequence is: (1) extract every definite-position clue, (2) place those people in the diagram, (3) attach relative clues one by one, (4) apply qualitative filters (gender, profession, hobby) last to disambiguate. Reversing this order is the single most common reason candidates run out of time on NAT-I.

#### Grid Method for Circular and Facing-Centre Tables
For unidirectional circles, treat two diametrically opposite seats as the axis and slot the known person there. When the puzzle specifies *facing the centre*, the rule flips: a person sitting to your right is actually on the viewer's left, because you and the viewer face opposite directions.

#### Typical NAT-I Question Pattern
| Pattern | Clue style | Anchor needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Linear single row | "A sits at the left end" | Yes, one end |
| Linear double row | "P sits behind Q" | Yes, front-row person |
| Circular (facing outside) | "P sits to the immediate left of Q" | Yes, one person |
| Circular (facing centre) | Same phrasing, mirrored logic | Yes, one person |
| Mixed with blood relations | Combine family tree with seat order | Yes, then map relations |

- Always count seats clockwise from the anchor; anticlockwise counts only when stated.
- Two-row rectangular: align front and back rows column-wise, back-row person sits directly behind the front-row person.
- When two arrangements are both consistent, the answer is the one **uniquely** determined by every clue.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Mirror Traps
Circular arrangements admit mirror-image ambiguity: a clockwise order A-B-C-D is indistinguishable from the anticlockwise order A-D-C-B **unless** a facing-direction clue breaks the symmetry. Candidates routinely lose marks by treating two mirror layouts as distinct, or by treating two genuinely different layouts as identical because they ignored the facing rule.

#### Integrating Qualitative Filters
Blood-relations, profession, nationality, and hobby clues rarely place a person directly; instead they narrow the candidate pool. The efficient move is to build a small elimination table on scratch paper and cross off anyone who violates a gender/profession constraint before attempting the seat diagram. This compresses a 6-person permutation (720 possibilities) to a handful within seconds.

#### Worked Micro-Example
Six friends — A, B, C, D, E, F — sit around a circle facing the centre. A sits opposite D. B sits immediately to the right of A. C is not adjacent to D. F sits between E and B.

Placing A at the north seat facing the centre, D goes south. Facing-centre rule: B at A's right means B is on the viewer's left, so B sits west. C cannot be east or south, leaving north-east or south-west as options; the "F between E and B" clue forces the unique layout: clockwise from A — A, B, F, D, E, C.

#### Practice Prompts
1. Five managers sit on a bench facing north. P sits at the right end. Q is second from the left. R is immediately to the left of S. T sits between P and R. Who sits in the middle?
2. Eight members of a family sit around a circular table facing outside, in two married couples alternating. A is opposite H. B is three places to the right of C. Where does D sit relative to A?
