# Dialogue Selections — OSSLT Reading

Dialogue is one of the four reading selection types on the OSSLT. The EQAO Framework describes it as a two- or three-speaker exchange between characters. Dialogue selections are paired with multiple-select items that target who-said-what tracking, speaker purpose, and the relationship between speakers. Inference items (Reading 2) are common in Dialogue because the writer signals character and relationship through subtext, not labels.

### Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

#### What a Dialogue selection looks like

- Two or three named speakers, each turn labelled (e.g., "Marcus:" then "Lin:").
- A short setting sentence or two at the start, identifying where and when the conversation happens.
- A central issue or topic the speakers are working through — making a decision, resolving a misunderstanding, exchanging information, or giving advice.
- A clear ending — agreement, disagreement, or a pause that signals more to come.
- Length set so the selection fits the OSSLT screen without scrolling for most items.

#### How Dialogue items are organised

- Reading 1 items: track who said a specific line, recall a stated fact, identify the order of the exchange.
- Reading 2 items: infer the speaker's purpose (to inform, to persuade, to request, to deflect), infer the relationship between speakers, infer what a subtextual line implies.
- Reading 3 items: connect the dialogue to the reader's experience of a similar conversation or to another selection on the test.
- One conventions item: a sentence-level edit, often a punctuation fix (a missing comma, an incorrect quotation mark).

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing the speakers. Dialogue items often refer to a specific line by speaker name. Track the speaker labels as you read.
- Reading the literal words without reading the subtext. Dialogue Reading 2 items ask what the speaker *means*, not what the speaker *says*. The literal line and the intended meaning often differ.
- Missing the punctuation cue. Dialogue selections are heavy on quotation marks and commas. A conventions item may depend on the punctuation of the dialogue itself.

### Standard — Regular Study (2d-2mo)

#### Strategy for Dialogue selections

**Read the setting sentence first.** The setting sentence usually identifies where the conversation happens (in a kitchen, on a bus, in a school hallway) and often signals the relationship (between siblings, between teammates, between a customer and a clerk). Reading 2 items depend on this signal.

**Track who-said-what as you read.** For each line, mark in your mind who the speaker is. Many Reading 1 items ask "according to the selection, who says..." — the answer is in the speaker label, not in the line content.

**Note the verbs of speech.** "Asked," "replied," "mumbled," "shouted," "sighed" signal tone and attitude. Reading 2 items ask what the speaker feels; the verb often carries the answer.

**Identify the central issue.** Dialogue selections usually revolve around a decision, a misunderstanding, or a piece of advice. Reading 3 items often connect the central issue to the reader's experience.

#### Practice routine for Dialogue

- Take one EQAO-style Dialogue selection. Read it once. Then list: speakers, setting, central issue, ending.
- For each speaker, write one sentence: "this speaker's purpose is to..." Compare your list to the Reading 2 items.
- Pick one line that contains subtext. Write what the speaker says, then what the speaker means. Compare to the items.
- Time yourself. Target: under 7 minutes per Dialogue selection (read + answer items).

#### How Dialogue differs from the other selection types

- **Real-Life Narrative** is one narrator telling a story; Dialogue is two or three speakers in conversation. RLN items ask about the narrator's view; Dialogue items ask about who-said-what and speaker intent.
- **Information Paragraph** is exposition; Dialogue is conversation. Information Paragraph items ask about definition; Dialogue items ask about speaker purpose.
- **News Report** is factual and event-focused; Dialogue is interpersonal and purpose-focused. News Report items ask about the five Ws; Dialogue items ask about speaker intent and relationship.

### Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo-6mo)

#### How Dialogue connects to the other OSSLT items

- **The Series-of-Paragraphs long-writing task** depends on understanding how different speakers or sources present different perspectives. Dialogue practice strengthens the perspective-tracking skill that the writing task depends on.
- **Reading 3** in Dialogue selections often asks the student to connect the conversation to a similar situation in the reader's experience. This is the same skill the open-response reading item tests.

#### Common errors and the correction

- **"The speakers are saying exactly what they mean."** Dialogue subtext is common in OSSLT items. Reading 2 items often require the student to read between the lines.
- **"I should answer based on who I think is right."** Reading 2 items ask what the writer implies, not what the student thinks. The right answer is grounded in the dialogue, not in the student's view.
- **"Punctuation doesn't matter in Dialogue items."** Punctuation matters — conventions items in Dialogue often target dialogue-specific punctuation (quotation marks, commas, end punctuation).

#### Exam technique

- Read the setting sentence first. Note the relationship signal.
- Track the speaker labels as you read. Mark the speaker of each line mentally.
- For Reading 2 items, ask: "what does this speaker mean by saying this?"
- For conventions items, focus on the dialogue punctuation — quotation marks, commas, end punctuation.
- Use the platform's flag-for-review function. Do not leave blanks on selected-response items.
