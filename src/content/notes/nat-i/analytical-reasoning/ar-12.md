# Cause and Effect

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Cause-and-Effect** questions in NAT-I Analytical Reasoning test whether you can identify the relationship where one event (the **cause**) directly produces another event (the **effect**). A typical item gives 3–5 statements and asks: "Which statement is the cause of the following event?" or "Which two statements have a direct cause-effect relationship?"

The three rules to memorise:
- **Transitive Rule:** If A → B and B → C, then A → C.
- **Independent-Cause Rule:** If A → C and B → C, then A and B are independent causes of C.
- **Inverse Rule:** If A causes B, then ¬A implies ¬B (preventing the cause prevents the effect).

Watch for the **post hoc fallacy** (after ≠ because of) and never confuse **correlation** with **causation**. NAT-I usually packs 2–3 such items in a 20-question Analytical Reasoning set.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Definitions
- **Cause:** The action, condition, or factor that produces a result. Marked by the presence of "because of," "due to," "as a result of."
- **Effect:** The outcome or consequence that follows directly from the cause.
- **Direct (proximate) cause:** The immediately preceding event that triggers the effect.
- **Remote cause:** An earlier contributing factor that does not itself trigger the final effect.
- **Independent causes:** Two or more separate events that each produce the same effect on their own.
- **Common cause:** A single hidden event that produces two seemingly related outcomes (the source of correlation-without-causation traps).

#### Logical Rules of Causation

| Rule | Statement | Form |
|------|-----------|------|
| Transitive | A causes B, B causes C, therefore A causes C | A → B → C ⟹ A → C |
| Independent | A causes C, B causes C, A and B are unrelated | A → C, B → C ⟹ A ⊥ B |
| Inverse | If A causes B, blocking A blocks B | A → B ⟹ ¬A → ¬B |
| Necessary | X must be present for Y to occur | ¬X → ¬Y |
| Sufficient | X alone guarantees Y | X → Y |

#### Typical NAT-I Question Patterns
1. **"Which is the cause of the following effect?"** — Pick the most direct trigger, not the earliest event.
2. **"Which conclusion follows from the above statements?"** — Apply transitive causation across the chain.
3. **"Two of the following have a cause-effect relationship. Find them."** — Eliminate items that share only a common cause.
4. **"If the cause is removed, what happens?"** — Apply the Inverse Rule.

The post hoc ergo propter hoc fallacy ("after this, therefore because of this") is the single most-tested trap; the answer choice that links events by **time sequence alone** is almost always wrong.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Subtler Distinctions
- **Necessary vs Sufficient causes:** Rain is a *necessary* condition for plants to grow outdoors, but *not sufficient* on its own (sunlight, soil are also needed). NAT-I items sometimes flip this — always check whether removing the cause fully removes the effect.
- **Proximate vs Remote:** In a chain "Drought → Crop failure → Price rise → Famine," the *proximate* cause of famine is the price rise, not the drought. NAT-I wording such as "the most immediate cause" signals you must pick the closest link.
- **Correlation vs Causation:** When two events co-occur, the test may hide a **common cause** (e.g., ice-cream sales and drowning both rise in summer → heat is the common cause). Do not pick one as causing the other.
- **Multiple effects, single cause:** One cause can fan out into several effects (a storm causes flooding, power cuts, and transport disruption); questions may ask you to identify *all* effects, not just the first.

#### Connection to Adjacent Topics
Cause-and-Effect overlaps with **Syllogisms** (deducing a conclusion from premises), **Statement-Conclusion** (testing whether a conclusion logically follows), and **Course of Action** (deciding remedial steps). A strong chain-of-causation analysis is the foundation for all four.

#### Practice Prompts
1. *Statements:* (i) The city reduced water supply by 30%, (ii) Residents reported dry taps, (iii) The municipality blamed low reservoir levels, (iv) Hotels began using tanker water. *Identify the direct cause of (ii).* — Answer: (i); (iii) is a remote cause of (i), not (ii).
2. *Test:* "If A causes B, and C also causes B, then A and C must be linked." True or False? — **False**; the Independent-Cause Rule says A ⊥ C, not linked.

#### Common Mistakes
- Confusing a *precondition* with the *cause* (oxygen is needed for fire but is not the cause of a specific blaze).
- Stopping at the *first* link in a chain and ignoring transitive implications.
- Choosing the temporally *earliest* event instead of the *proximate* one.
