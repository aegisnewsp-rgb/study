# Legal Aptitude: Torts

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **tort** is a civil wrong giving the injured party a right to sue for **unliquidated damages**, independent of any contract. The four essentials are: a wrongful act or omission, breach of a **legal duty**, causation, and actual or constructive damage.

- **Tort vs. crime**: tort is a *private* remedy (compensation); crime is a *State* prosecution (punishment).
- **Tort vs. contract**: tort duties are imposed by law; contract duties are voluntarily assumed.
- **Damnum sine injuria** = damage without legal injury → no tort.
- **Injuria sine damno** = legal injury without measurable damage → still actionable.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Definition and Elements

A tort is a wrongful act or omission (other than a breach of contract) that causes harm recognized by law, for which the injured party can claim damages. The Latin maxim *ubi jus ibi remedium* ("where there is a right, there is a remedy") underlies the field. Unlike criminal wrongs, the State does not prosecute; the aggrieved person sues personally.

#### Negligence: The Most Tested Head

The five-element test, drawn from English authority adapted into Pakistani jurisprudence, is the highest-yield sub-topic:

1. Existence of a **duty of care** owed by the defendant to the plaintiff.
2. **Breach** of that duty by failing the *reasonable person* standard.
3. **Causation** — breach must be the proximate cause (the "but for" test).
4. **Damage** — actual harm to person or property.
5. Absence of a valid defense (e.g., contributory negligence).

| Defense / Doctrine | Effect | Pakistan-specific note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Res ipsa loquitur** | Inference of negligence from the event itself | Evidentiary rule, NOT strict liability |
| **Contributory negligence** | Reduces or bars recovery | Pakistan follows the common-law apportionment approach |
| **Volenti non fit injuria** | Plaintiff consented to risk | Narrow scope; employment-sport cases in many papers reject it |
| **Vicarious liability** | Master liable for servant's torts in *course of employment* | Out-of-course independent acts break the chain |

#### Strict Liability and Other Torts

The rule in **Rylands v. Fletcher** (1868) imposes liability for non-natural use of land, escape of accumulated things, and foreseeable damage. Pakistani courts apply it selectively rather than verbatim. **Nuisance** (public or private) balances utility against inconvenience. **Defamation** splits into **libel** (written/published, actionable per se) and **slander** (spoken, in most cases requiring special damage), with defenses of truth, fair comment, and privilege.

#### Common LAT Traps

- Treating *res ipsa loquitur* as making the defendant automatically liable.
- Forgetting that "course of employment" limits vicarious liability.
- Confusing *damnum sine injuria* with *injuria sine damno*.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Distinguishing Overlapping Wrongs

LAT principle-fact questions in many papers hinge on whether a fact pattern attracts tort, contract, or criminal liability. A hospital patient's surgical injury sounds like negligence but is contract-based if the patient signed consent and paid fees. A road-rage assault is simultaneously tort (battery) and crime (PPC § 337). The examiner rewards answers that identify the correct *head* of tort and name the precise duty breached.

#### Vicarious Liability — Worked Logic

A delivery driver, while delivering parcels, runs a red light and injures a pedestrian. The employer is vicariously liable because the act occurred in the *course of employment*. However, if the driver detours to a friend's house for two hours and causes the same injury, the detour breaks the connection — the master is not liable for the servant's *frolic*. LAT questions test this boundary directly.

#### Remedies and the "Unliquidated" Element

"Unliquidated" means the amount is not pre-fixed; the court assesses damages based on loss. Alongside damages, courts can grant **injunctions** (to restrain continuing wrongs like nuisance) or **specific restitution**. The Tortfeasor (CPC) Act 1858 governs procedure for suits in Pakistan.

#### Practice Prompts

1. A factory stores chemicals in an underground tank. The tank leaks, contaminating a neighbor's well. Identify the tort, the rule, and the likely defense.
2. A newspaper prints a false statement about a doctor. The doctor sues. Which limb of defamation applies and which defense can succeed if the statement was a fair review?
