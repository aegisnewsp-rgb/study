# One Word

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **One Word Substitution (OWS)** item gives you a multi-word phrase or descriptive sentence and asks for the single English word that captures its meaning. In SSC CGL, this tests your lexical range — recognising Greek/Latin roots, common prefixes, and context-driven synonyms.

| Term | Meaning in OWS context | Example clue → answer |
|---|---|---|
| Prefix | Bound morpheme placed before a root | `phil-` (love) + `anthropo` (humans) + `-ist` = **philanthropist** |
| Suffix | Bound morpheme placed after a root | `bibli-` (book) + `-phile` (lover) = **bibliophile** |
| Root | Core lexical carrier of meaning | `-cide` (kill), `-phobia` (fear), `-logy` (study of) |
| Synonym | Word with identical meaning (rare in OWS) | "fear of heights" ≈ **acrophobia** |
| Antonym | Word with opposite meaning | "omniscient" (all-knowing) ↔ "ignoramus" |

- Person-based clues dominate: `a person who…` → egoist, altruist, misanthropist, philatelist, lexicographer.
- Phenomenon-based clues: `a thing which…` → plagiarism, nepotism, red-tapism, pacifism.
- Place-based clues: `a place where…` → cemetery (burial), asylum (sanctuary), arena (contest ground).

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"PHIL-PHOBE-CIDE-LOGY = 4 SSC Pillars"** — `phil-` (love), `-phobia` (fear), `-cide` (kill), `-logy` (study). Memorise these four roots and you decode ~40% of OWS items on sight.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Concept: Decoding Mechanism

SSC CGL examiners design OWS items so the answer can be reached by **morpheme decomposition**. The clue rarely quotes the root; instead, it paraphrases it. Your job is to reverse-engineer the morpheme stack. For "one who collects stamps," parse `stamp` → philately (Greek *philatelos*, "stamp-lover") → suffix `-ist` (practitioner) → **philatelist**. The same method rescues "science of," "fear of," "hatred of," and "study of" clusters.

The four highest-yield morpheme families are:

1. `-phobia` (irrational fear) — acrophobia (heights), claustrophobia (enclosed spaces), xenophobia (strangers), hydrophobia (water).
2. `phil- / -phile` (lover of) — bibliophile (books), francophile (French culture), anglophile (English culture).
3. `-cide / -cidal` (killer of) — matricide (mother), patricide (father), fratricide (brother), infanticide (infant).
4. `-logy / -ologist` (study / student of) — ornithology (birds), entomology (insects), cardiology (heart), archaeology (ancient things).

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Clue word in question | Tempting wrong answer | Correct answer | Why the trap works |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Study of birds" | Ornithologist (person) | **Ornithology** (field) | Item asks for science, not the practitioner |
| "Burial place of the dead" | Sanctuary | **Cemetery** | Both sound formal but sanctuary = refuge |
| "Science of flying aircraft" | Aeroplane | **Aviation** | Concrete noun vs abstract field |
| "Intense happiness, often medical" | Elation | **Euphoria** | Elation is sudden joy; euphoria is sustained |
| "Removal of tissue for diagnosis" | Autopsy | **Biopsy** | Autopsy is post-mortem; biopsy is living tissue |
| "Cannot be corrected" | Incredible | **Incorrigible** | Incredible = unbelievable; shared `in-` prefix |
| "Female executor of a will" | Executor | **Executrix** | Gender-specific legal term, often missed |

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** *A person who has an irresistible compulsion to steal is called:*
(a) Kleptomaniac  (b) Plagiarist  (c) Pyromaniac  (d) Egomaniac

#### Solution:
1. Identify the root morphemes in the clue: "irresistible compulsion" points to a **-mania** (pathological urge) and "to steal" supplies the Greek root **klept-**.
2. Apply the person-suffix **`-iac`** (one suffering from) to **klepto-** + **mania** → **kleptomaniac**.
3. Eliminate distractors:
   - (b) Plagiarist = one who copies, not steals compulsively.
   - (c) Pyromaniac = compulsion to set fires (Greek *pyr*, fire).
   - (d) Egomaniac = compulsion centred on self (Greek *ego*, I).
4. Answer: **(a) Kleptomaniac**.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** The trap is (d) Egomaniac. Many students associate "compulsion" only with `-mania` and ignore the root that names the *object* of the compulsion. Always read the verb in the definition — here "steal" locks the root to `klept-`, not `ego-`.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Register Traps

OWS questions in SSC CGL Tier-II CBE assume a **formal, neutral register**. Everyday English approximations ("book-lover" instead of bibliophile) are never accepted because the entire question format exists to test *precision of register*. This is why "one who knows many languages" must be **polyglot**, never "multilingual person," and "a place for lunatics" must be **asylum**, never "mental hospital."

Three edge-case clusters repeatedly appear in recent SSC CGL papers:

- **Direction-sensitive verbs:** *emigrate* (leave a country) vs *immigrate* (enter a country); *import* vs *export*; *exhale* vs *inhale*. The clue's preposition decides the answer.
- **Gender-marked legal terms:** *executor / executrix*, *testator / testatrix*, *administrator / administratrix*, *benefactor / benefactress*. Tier-II frequently tests the female form.
- **Pathological vs ordinary traits:** *miserly* (pathological hoarding) ≠ *frugal* (wise saving); *curious* (inquisitive) ≠ *nosy* (prying); *courageous* (brave) ≠ *audacious* (recklessly bold).

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. **Decoding exercise.** Given the clue "a young bird that has just learned to fly," produce the technical term **fledgling** and identify the root (**fledge** = to grow feathers, Old English). Then write the parallel term for "a young animal just weaned" → **weanling**. Both share the `ling` diminutive suffix.
2. **Trap-spotting exercise.** For the clue "one who sacrifices himself for his country," examine the four-option set and reject any answer built on `suicide` (self-killing) — the correct answer is **patriot**, anchored in Greek `patria` (fatherland) plus the `-ot` suffix. The trap works because "sacrifices" misleads students toward **martyr**, which actually means "one who dies for a cause" (often religious), not specifically country.

#### Quick-Reference Master List (≤400 words)

| OWS category | Common terms | Identifying root |
|---|---|---|
| People (personality) | egoist, altruist, misanthrope, philanthropist | `-ist`, `phil-` |
| People (compulsions) | kleptomaniac, pyromaniac, egomaniac, dipsomaniac | `-mania`, `klept-`, `pyr-` |
| People (knowledge) | polyglot, polymath, omniscient, illiterate | `poly-`, `omni-`, `in-` (neg) |
| People (fears/philias) | acrophobic, claustrophobic, xenophobe, bibliophile | `-phobia`, `-phile` |
| Fields of study | cardiology, philately, lexicography, entomology | `-logy`, `lexico-` |
| Social phenomena | nepotism, plagiarism, red-tapism, capitalism | `-ism` |
| Killers / acts | matricide, genocide, infanticide, homicide | `-cide` |
| Places | cemetery, asylum, sanctuary, gymnasium | Latin/Greek place roots |
| Time / state | ephemeral (short-lived), perennial (lasting), chronic (long) | `epi-`, `per-`, `chron-` |

> 📌 **Formula Check:** No SI units apply — OWS is purely lexical. Treat each root as a *morpheme variable*: `<prefix><root><suffix>` where the root carries the semantic payload.
