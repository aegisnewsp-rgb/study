# Islamic History — Amir al-Mu'minin Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz — Qatar Secondary Certificate Islamic Studies Notes

The MoEHE Grade 12 Islamic Studies Term 2 Sira wa-al-Buhuth al-Islamiyya (السيرة والبحوث الإسلامية) domain includes the study of Amir al-Mu'minin Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz (أمير المؤمنين عمر بن عبد العزيز, 63-101 AH / 682-720 CE), the Umayyad caliph widely regarded as the model of righteous Islamic governance after the Rashidun caliphs.

### Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz (63-101 AH / 682-720 CE):** Umayyad caliph (99-101 AH / 717-720 CE), descendant of Umar ibn al-Khattab (the second Rashidun caliph) on his mother's side. Known as the fifth Rashidun caliph by Sunni tradition.
- **His reforms:** restoration of justice ('adl), abolition of the mawali (non-Arab Muslim) jizya distinction, expansion of the welfare state (diwan), support for scholarship (Tabari, al-Zuhri, Rabi'ah al-Ra'y).
- **His governance principles:** consultation (shura), accountability, transparency in the public treasury (bayt al-mal), and revival of the Prophetic and Rashidun model.
- **His death:** he died in 101 AH (720 CE) at age 38, possibly poisoned — he ruled for only two years but left a lasting legacy.

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz (Umayyad) with Umar ibn al-Khattab (Rashidun) — they are different caliphs, both named Umar.
- Confusing his rule with the longer Umayyad caliphate — he reigned only two years; his reforms did not survive his successors intact.

### Standard — Regular Study (2d-2mo)

#### His life before the caliphate

Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz was born in 63 AH in Medina, where his grandfather served as governor. His mother was the granddaughter of Umar ibn al-Khattab, the second Rashidun caliph. His father Abd al-Aziz ibn Marwan was a governor of Egypt and a son of the Umayyad caliph Marwan ibn al-Hakam.

He was raised in Medina, where he studied under the scholars of the time — Sa'id ibn al-Musayyab, Abu Bakr ibn Hazm, and others. He was appointed governor of Medina under the caliphate of his cousin Sulayman ibn Abd al-Malik. His reputation for piety and justice in Medina preceded his caliphate.

#### His caliphate (99-101 AH / 717-720 CE)

When Sulayman ibn Abd al-Malik died in 99 AH, he left a written instruction that Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz should succeed him — breaking the Marwanid tradition of hereditary succession in favour of merit. Umar accepted reluctantly and immediately began a programme of reform.

**Reforms:**

1. Restoration of justice: he dismissed corrupt governors and replaced them with qualified successors; he returned lands that had been taken unjustly.
2. The mawali distinction: he abolished the distinction in jizya between Arab Muslims and non-Arab Muslims (mawali) — restoring the original Quranic principle that all Muslims are equal before the Sharia.
3. The diwan (welfare state): he expanded the diwan to include all those eligible for state support — the poor, the orphaned, the widowed, the elderly, and the disabled.
4. Bayt al-mal: he opened the public treasury to scrutiny and ordered that all surplus revenue be returned to the Muslim community.
5. Scholarship: he supported the scholars of the time — Imam al-Zuhri in Madinah, Makhul in Syria, al-Hasan al-Basri (the elder, deceased 110 AH), and the generation of Tabi'in who compiled the hadith, the tafsir, and the early fiqh.
6. Daw'ah: he sent missions to spread Islam — to the lands of Sindh, India, and the Berbers. The tradition records that during his caliphate, Islam spread as widely as it had during the Rashidun period.
7. Public works: he ordered the construction of mosques, roads, and water infrastructure across the empire.

#### His legacy

Sunni historians (Ibn Kathir, al-Tabari, Ibn al-Jawzi, Ibn al-Qayyim) regard Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz as the fifth Rashidun caliph — the one who restored the Prophetic model after the hereditary Umayyad period. His reforms shaped the future of the Umayyad and Abbasid states, even though his successors did not always continue them.

His letters to his governors — preserved in al-Tabari's Tarikh and Ibn Abd al-Hakam's Futuh Misr — are a foundational text of Islamic governance and accountability. He wrote: "I instruct you to fear Allah in private and in public, to be just to those you govern, and to remember that you will stand before Allah for every decision you make."

### Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo-6mo)

#### Why the study of Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz matters at the MoEHE terminal

The MoEHE Term 2 Islamic History module connects the abstract ideals of Islamic governance to a concrete historical model. Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz is the bridge between the Rashidun caliphate and the later Islamic empires — the model of a ruler who combined political authority with religious commitment.

#### Exam technique

- For Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz questions, name the reforms precisely and link them to the Quranic and Prophetic basis.
- For governance questions, draw on his letters and his conduct as a model.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "He was the most powerful caliph." He was not — his reign was short. His legacy is in the principles he applied, not the duration of his rule.
- "He restored the Umayyad caliphate to its original state." He transformed it briefly; his successors did not maintain his reforms.
