# Matching Features and Sentence Endings — IELTS Reading (Qatar)

In the IELTS Academic Reading test, the matching-family questions ask you to draw a
line from each item in a list to the correct place in a passage, table, or list of
sentences. The two most common variants in this family are:

- **Matching features** — pair a list of people, dates, theories, or inventions with
  the matching description in the passage (e.g. "Which researcher proposed the
  theory?" → A / B / C / D / E).
- **Matching sentence endings** — pick the correct phrase (from a list of options) that
  completes the second half of a sentence whose first half already appears in the
  passage.

Both variants appear on IELTS Academic Reading passages 2 and 3 (the more
cognitively demanding passages), and they are graded together as one of the seven
question types in the cohort. In Qatar they commonly appear together in the same
passage to test how quickly you can scan for **specific identifiers** — names,
dates, theories — and how carefully you can read **line-by-line** to spot a
sentence half that matches a list option.

This note shows the LCA (Long-Read Cohort Appetite) version: a **Lite** (5-minute)
read, a **Standard** (15-minute) read, and the **Exam Essentials** table a Qatari
candidate can use to revise in the last hour before the test.

## Lite (4-Step Method)

1. **Read the list first, not the passage.** The list gives you the *labels* to
   look for (e.g. *Dr Ahmad al-Mansoori*, *Prof. Khalid al-Sulaiti*, *1907*,
   *1948*, *The Sedentary Threshold Hypothesis*). These labels are usually proper
   nouns — names, dates, or unique phrases — that you can locate in the passage
   by eye.
2. **For matching features, scan the passage for each label.** When you see
   *Dr Ahmad al-Mansoori* in the passage, read the sentence that mentions him
   and check whether it matches any of the *features* in the list. The match is
   usually expressed by paraphrase, not verbatim copy — IELTS will rewrite the
   feature description, so look for **meaning**, not for an exact word.
3. **For matching sentence endings, look at the grammar of the first half.** The
   ending must agree with the first half in **subject**, **verb tense**, **count**
   (singular/plural), and **logical continuation**. If the first half ends with
   *"…because the soil…"*, the ending must start with a continuation (e.g.
   *…lacked the nitrogen-binding bacteria…*), not a new clause.
4. **Eliminate used options.** Each option is used **once and only once** in
   matching sentence endings. Once you have matched an option, cross it out so
   you do not waste time re-checking it for later items.

**Insight: matching is line-reference reading, not content reading.** The test
is not asking you to understand the whole passage; it is asking you to scan for
specific identifiers and match them against a small list. You do not need to
read the whole passage top to bottom.

### 6 Examiner Traps (matching-family)

- **The double mention** — a researcher may be mentioned twice in the passage;
  only ONE mention will be the matching feature (the one that **describes** the
  research, not the one that **cites** it).
- **The synonym switch** — the list says *deforestation*, the passage says
  *clearing of forested land*. They mean the same thing; the test is whether
  you recognise the paraphrase.
- **The misplaced label** — a date or name may appear at the **start** of a
  paragraph, but the *feature it matches* may be in the **middle**. Read a
  whole paragraph, not just the sentence where the label first appears.
- **The grammatical mismatch** — for sentence endings, an option may look
  topical but fail grammatically (singular vs plural, wrong tense).
- **The all-of-the-above** — if you can argue two options are *equally* correct,
  you have probably misread the first half; re-read it with fresh eyes.
- **The order trick** — for sentence endings, IELTS often places options in
  a *different order* from the first halves, so do not assume that the option
  immediately to the right of an item is the answer.

## Standard (7-Step Method with Long-Form Guidance)

### Step 1 — Pre-read the list and the question stem

Before you touch the passage, read the **list of features** (for matching
features) or the **list of sentence endings** (for sentence endings) at the
bottom of the question. Read both columns if there are two. Note the **labels**
in the list — these are the words you will scan the passage for.

For matching sentence endings, also note the **grammatical gender / count** of
each first half. If a first half starts with *"The hypothesis…"* (singular),
the ending must be compatible with a singular subject. If a first half ends
with *"…and they…"* (plural), the ending must start with a continuation that
agrees with a plural subject.

### Step 2 — Skim the passage once

You are not reading the passage for meaning yet. You are scanning for the
**labels** in the list. Use a pencil (or the on-screen highlight tool) to
mark each label when you see it. Do not stop to read around the label yet —
just mark it.

### Step 3 — Re-visit each label in context

Once you have marked all the labels you can find, go back to each label and
read the **full sentence or paragraph** that contains it. The matching
*feature* or *ending* is usually expressed in the same sentence as the label,
or in the sentence immediately before/after.

### Step 4 — Compare the context to the list

For matching features, ask: *"Does the passage context describe the same
event / theory / person as the list option?"* Look for paraphrase — the test
will not use the same word twice.

### Step 5 — For matching sentence endings, check grammar first

Before checking meaning, check that the first half and the ending agree in
**subject**, **verb tense**, **singular/plural**, and **logical continuity**.
If they do not agree grammatically, the option is wrong even if the topic
looks plausible.

### Step 6 — Eliminate used options

Cross off each option as soon as you have matched it. This prevents you from
wasting time re-checking the same option for later items.

### Step 7 — Verify your answers

If you have time, re-read each matched pair in the passage to confirm the
match is correct. Do not change an answer unless you have a clear reason to.

## Previous Year Patterns (IELTS 2018–2025)

IELTS has used matching features / sentence endings in **at least one passage
per Reading test on the Academic module since 2018**. The Academic module uses
matching more often than the General Training module. Over 2018–2025:

- 2018–2020: matching features appeared in Passage 2 or 3, often alongside
  sentence endings in the same passage.
- 2021–2023: matching features / sentence endings commonly appeared in
  Passage 3 (the hardest passage) paired with multiple choice.
- 2024–2025: matching sentence endings increased in frequency, especially on
  texts about research, science, and history.

The pattern: matching features / sentence endings are increasingly a *Passage
3* question type on Academic Reading, paired with multiple choice or matching
headings.

## Pro Tips (10 enumeration)

1. **Read the list FIRST.** It tells you what labels to scan for.
2. **Scan, then read in context.** Skim for labels, then re-read around each
   label.
3. **Match by meaning, not by word.** Paraphrase is the test.
4. **Match by grammar first for sentence endings.** Subject + tense + count
   must agree.
5. **Eliminate used options.** Each option is used once.
6. **Watch the double mention.** A name / date may appear twice; only one
   mention has a feature.
7. **Do not assume order.** Options are often out of order.
8. **Read the whole sentence / paragraph.** The matching feature is rarely
   in the same clause as the label; it is usually in the next sentence or
   paragraph.
9. **Time budget: 1.5–2 minutes per question.** Do not dwell on any one
   question.
10. **Re-verify before submitting.** A 30-second re-check can save a 1-point
    penalty.
