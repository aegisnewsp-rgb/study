# Coordination and Control — MCAT Pakistan

This unit covers how the human body detects stimuli, decides on a response, and executes it: the nervous system (neurons, synapses, reflex arc, brain, spinal cord), the endocrine system (hormones, feedback loops, target organs), and the sense organs (eye, ear, skin). Under the PMDC 2026 Biology allotment of 81 MCQs (≈45 % of the 180-MCQ paper, PMDC Public Notice, 2026), Coordination and Control contributes roughly 6–8 MCQs per paper — enough that a single missed reflex-arc or hormone-target question costs several percentile points. Questions are MCQ, single-best-answer, no negative marking, so always attempt every item (PMDC 2026 paper-pattern notice).

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

**What to lock in within the first hour.**
- **Neuron structure.** Dendrites → cell body → axon → axon terminals. Myelinated axons (Schwann cells in the PNS, oligodendrocytes in the CNS) conduct faster via saltatory conduction; gaps between myelin segments are Nodes of Ranvier.
- **Action potential.** Resting potential ≈ −70 mV (Na⁺ outside, K⁺ inside). Depolarisation: voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open, Na⁺ rushes in. Repolarisation: K⁺ flows out. Refractory period restores the gradient via the Na⁺/K⁺ pump (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in, ATP-driven).
- **Synapse.** Electrical signal → Ca²⁺ influx into the presynaptic terminal → vesicles fuse with the membrane → neurotransmitter (acetylcholine, noradrenaline, dopamine, GABA, glutamate, serotonin) released into the synaptic cleft → binds receptors on the postsynaptic membrane.
- **Reflex arc.** Receptor → sensory (afferent) neuron → interneuron in the spinal cord → motor (efferent) neuron → effector (muscle/gland). A withdrawal reflex skips the brain for speed; the brain registers the sensation afterwards.
- **CNS divisions.** Cerebrum (cognition, voluntary movement), cerebellum (balance, posture), medulla oblongata (cardiac, respiratory, vasomotor centres), hypothalamus (homeostasis, hormone bridge), spinal cord (reflex centre, conduction pathway).
- **Major endocrine glands and their hormones.** Pituitary (GH, ACTH, TSH, FSH, LH, prolactin, ADH, oxytocin), thyroid (T₃, T₄, calcitonin), parathyroid (PTH), adrenal cortex (aldosterone, cortisol) and medulla (adrenaline, noradrenaline), pancreas (insulin from β-cells, glucagon from α-cells), gonads (testosterone, oestrogen, progesterone).
- **Negative feedback.** High blood glucose → insulin release → glucose uptake → glucose falls → insulin release stops. Same shape for thyroxine (TRH → TSH → T₃/T₄) and cortisol (CRH → ACTH → cortisol).

**One-line recap before walking in.** Stimulus → receptor → afferent neuron → CNS (interneuron + brain) → efferent neuron → effector; hormones do the same job more slowly via blood.

### 🟡 Standard — Exam Prep (3d-3w)

#### Neuron, nerve impulse, and synapse
A neuron's **resting membrane potential** is maintained by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump and the selective permeability of the membrane (more K⁺ leakage than Na⁺ leakage). A stimulus above the threshold opens voltage-gated Na⁺ channels; the resulting depolarisation is an **all-or-none action potential**. Strength of stimulus is encoded by *frequency*, not amplitude. The action potential travels along the axon, jumping between Nodes of Ranvier in myelinated fibres.

At the **synapse**, the action potential opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels; Ca²⁺ triggers vesicle fusion and neurotransmitter release. Excitatory neurotransmitters (acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction, glutamate in the CNS) depolarise the postsynaptic membrane. Inhibitory neurotransmitters (GABA, glycine) hyperpolarise it. Acetylcholinesterase breaks down ACh in the cleft to end the signal — drugs and nerve agents that block this enzyme cause continuous firing.

**Worked example.** A drug blocks voltage-gated Na⁺ channels on a sensory neuron. Predict the effect on the reflex arc. **No action potential reaches the spinal cord; the reflex fails at the afferent limb.** Compare with a GABA-agonist: the signal still arrives but the interneuron cannot excite the motor neuron, so the reflex still fails — different mechanism, same outcome.

#### Central and peripheral nervous system
The **CNS** (brain and spinal cord) processes information. The **PNS** carries signals: somatic (voluntary, skeletal muscle) and autonomic (involuntary, smooth muscle and glands). The autonomic splits into sympathetic (fight-or-flight, thoraco-lumbar, ACh at ganglia, noradrenaline at effector) and parasympathetic (rest-and-digest, cranio-sacral, ACh at both synapses). Heart rate, pupil diameter, gut motility, and bronchiolar tone flip in opposite directions under the two systems.

Brain functions: cerebrum (sensory, motor, associative areas; frontal lobe = executive function), cerebellum (coordination, motor learning, equilibrium), medulla (cardiac, respiratory, vasomotor centres — damage is fatal), pons (relay), midbrain (eye reflexes, auditory relay), hypothalamus (thirst, hunger, temperature, pituitary control).

#### Reflex arc and reflex types
The simplest reflex is a **monosynaptic stretch reflex** (e.g., patellar knee-jerk): sensory neuron from the muscle spindle synapses directly onto the motor neuron in the spinal cord, with no interneuron. The **withdrawal reflex** is polysynaptic (an interneuron crosses to the opposite side, inhibiting the antagonist muscle via a reciprocal inhibition pathway). **Conditioned reflexes** (Pavlov's dogs) are learned and depend on the cerebrum, not the spinal cord.

#### Endocrine system and feedback loops
A hormone is a chemical messenger secreted into the blood by a ductless gland; it acts on a target organ with the matching receptor. The **hypothalamic–pituitary axis** is the master switch: hypothalamus releases releasing hormones (TRH, CRH, GnRH) into a portal system that reaches the anterior pituitary; the pituitary then releases trophic hormones (TSH, ACTH, FSH, LH) that drive the downstream gland.

**Insulin and glucagon.** Blood glucose rises after a meal → β-cells in the pancreatic islets of Langerhans release insulin → insulin promotes glucose uptake by muscle and adipose tissue, glycogenesis in the liver, and lipogenesis. Blood glucose falls below set-point → α-cells release glucagon → glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis restore glucose. Diabetes mellitus type 1 = insulin deficiency (autoimmune destruction of β-cells); type 2 = insulin resistance.

**Worked example.** A patient has low plasma cortisol, high plasma ACTH, and a normal pituitary gland on imaging. Where is the lesion? — **Adrenal cortex** (primary adrenal insufficiency, e.g., Addison disease). Negative feedback loss raises ACTH, but the gland cannot respond. Compare with pituitary failure: cortisol low, ACTH low.

#### Sense organs
**Eye.** Cornea and lens focus light on the retina. Photoreceptors: rods (dim light, peripheral vision, rhodopsin) and cones (colour, bright light, three types for red/green/blue). The fovea is cone-dense and gives sharp central vision. The optic nerve carries signals; the optic chiasm swaps the nasal fibres so each visual cortex sees the opposite half-field.

**Ear.** Outer ear funnels sound to the tympanic membrane; middle ear ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes) amplify and transmit to the oval window; the cochlea in the inner ear contains the organ of Corti, where hair cells on the basilar membrane convert mechanical vibration into receptor potentials. Semicircular canals detect rotational acceleration; the utricle and saccule detect linear acceleration and gravity.

**Skin receptors.** Free nerve endings (pain), Meissner corpuscles (light touch), Pacinian corpuscles (deep pressure, vibration), Ruffini endings (skin stretch), thermoreceptors (separate hot and cold). Each receptor type responds to a specific stimulus modality — a question asking "which receptor detects vibration?" has only one correct answer.

#### Common clinical anchors
- **Myasthenia gravis:** autoantibodies against acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction → muscle weakness that worsens with use.
- **Parkinson's disease:** degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra → resting tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia.
- **Hyperthyroidism (Graves disease):** autoantibodies mimicking TSH → excess T₃/T₄ → weight loss, heat intolerance, exophthalmos.
- **Diabetes insipidus:** ADH deficiency or kidney insensitivity → polyuria, polydipsia, dilute urine (distinct from diabetes mellitus).

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (1mo+)

#### Autonomic pharmacology and MCAT-style traps
Drugs that mimic sympathetic activity (salbutamol, phenylephrine) are agonists at adrenergic receptors; β-blockers (atenolol, propranolol) are antagonists. Anticholinergics (atropine) block parasympathetic muscarinic receptors → dry mouth, dilated pupil, tachycardia. MCAT questions love the orientation trap: "atropine causes bradycardia" is wrong — it causes tachycardia. Always match the receptor and direction before naming the effect.

#### Central synaptic integration
A single neuron sums thousands of excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs and IPSPs) across its dendrites. **Spatial summation** combines inputs from many synapses; **temporal summation** combines rapid-fire inputs at one synapse. The axon hillock fires only if the net input crosses the threshold. This explains why a sedative that boosts GABA can dampen the entire CNS without blocking sensory input.

#### Neuroendocrine integration
The hypothalamus converts neural input into hormonal output: stress → CRH → ACTH → cortisol; suckling → oxytocin (efferent signal, not just hormonal); dehydration → ADH from the posterior pituitary (axonally transported, not anterior-portal). Many MCAT questions test this: ADH and oxytocin are synthesised in the hypothalamus, only released from the posterior pituitary. The anterior pituitary hormones are synthesised *in situ*.

#### Connections to other units
Coordination touches **Bioenergetics** (Na⁺/K⁺ pump consumes a large share of resting ATP), **Circulation** (adrenal medulla releases adrenaline into blood; noradrenaline is the sympathetic neurotransmitter), **Homeostasis** (thermoregulation runs through the hypothalamus), and **Immunity** (stress and cortisol suppress lymphocyte activity — the basis for steroid immunosuppression).

## Common traps

1. **Calling the motor neuron the afferent limb of a reflex arc.** Afferent = sensory, carrying the signal *into* the CNS. Efferent = motor, carrying the command *out*. A reflex-arc diagram with arrows reversed is the most common single-point loss on this unit.
2. **Confusing sympathetic and parasympathetic effects on the same organ.** Sympathetic accelerates the heart and dilates the bronchioles; parasympathetic does the opposite. A question asks "what is the effect of vagal (parasympathetic) stimulation on the heart?" — answer is *decreased* rate, not increased.
3. **Treating insulin and glucagon as interchangeable.** Insulin lowers blood glucose; glucagon raises it. A question describing hypoglycaemia (shakiness, sweating, confusion) asks which hormone is *deficient* — the answer is glucagon (or cortisol/adrenaline as backup), not insulin. Watch the direction of the imbalance.
4. **Calling ADH a "kidney hormone".** ADH is *made* in the hypothalamus and *released* by the posterior pituitary; the kidney is its target organ. A diagram with ADH labelled on the kidney is the textbook distractor.
5. **Confusing rods and cones.** Rods: scotopic (low-light) vision, monochromatic, concentrated in the peripheral retina. Cones: photopic (bright-light) vision, trichromatic, concentrated in the fovea. A question on night blindness points to vitamin A deficiency affecting rhodopsin in rods — not cones.
