# Carboxylic Acids, Acid Derivatives, and Claisen Condensation

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **carboxylic acid** carries the carboxyl group –COOH, where a hydroxyl is bound directly to a carbonyl carbon. Replacing –OH with Cl, OR′, OC(O)R, NR₂, or SR′ yields an **acid derivative** (acyl chloride, ester, anhydride, amide, thioester). The carbonyl carbon is electrophilic; nucleophiles attack it through a **tetrahedral intermediate**, expelling the leaving group X⁻ in **nucleophilic acyl substitution**.

- **Reactivity order (most → least):** acyl chloride > anhydride > acid > ester > amide.
- **Typical pKₐ:** RCOOH ≈ 4–5 (acetic acid 4.76); α-H of an ester ≈ 20–25.
- **Claisen condensation** = 2 esters + 1 eq. alkoxide base → β-ketoester + R′OH (loses an alcohol, not water).

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"ClAn's CAN'T wait"** — **C**l > **A**nhydride > a**C**id > a**N**hydride > e**S**ter > a**M**ide (German order): mnemonic for reactivity is "**CAAN-SM**" — Cl, Anhydride, Acid, then N-drift down to S, M. Equivalently remember "**Leaving-Group ability = reactivity**": Cl⁻ leaves first, R₂N⁻ last.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Structure & Acidity

The carboxyl group is a **planar sp² carbonyl** bearing an –OH that hydrogen-bonds strongly. Resonance donation from the lone pair on –OH destabilises the conjugate base far less than in alkoxide, hence pKₐ ≈ 4–5 (vs. ROH pKₐ ≈ 16–18). Adjacent electron-withdrawing groups (Cl, CF₃) lower pKₐ further (chloroacetic acid 2.87); alkyl groups slightly raise it. The α-hydrogen of any carbonyl compound is far less acidic (pKₐ ≈ 20–25) but is removable by **NaOEt** or **LDA**, generating a resonance-stabilised **enolate** that acts as the carbon nucleophile in the Claisen condensation.

#### Reactivity of Acid Derivatives

All derivatives share the same acyl core R–C(=O)–X. Reactivity tracks leaving-group ability (Cl⁻ > RCOO⁻ > HO⁻ > RO⁻ > R₂N⁻) and the electron-donation by X into the carbonyl (NR₂ donates most, lowering electrophilicity).

| Derivative | Leaving group X | Relative rate (hydrolysis) | IR C=O (cm⁻¹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acyl chloride RCOCl | Cl⁻ | ~1000 | ~1790–1815 |
| Anhydride (RCO)₂O | RCOO⁻ | ~100 | ~1740–1820 (two bands) |
| Ester RCOOR′ | R′OH | 1 (reference) | ~1735–1750 |
| Carboxylic acid RCOOH | HO⁻ (poor) | ~0.01 | ~1700–1725 + broad O–H 2500–3300 |
| Amide RCONR₂ | R₂N⁻ (very poor) | ~0.001 | ~1650 (Amide I), ~3300 N–H |

**Fischer esterification** (RCOOH + R′OH ⇌ RCOOR′ + H₂O) is **acid-catalysed and reversible**. **Saponification** (RCOOR′ + NaOH → RCOO⁻Na⁺ + R′OH) is **base-promoted and effectively irreversible** because the carboxylate is formed and cannot be re-attacked.

#### Concept Comparison: Fischer vs. Saponification vs. Claisen

| Reaction | Catalyst / base | Key step | Equilibrium | Product handling |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fischer esterification | H₂SO₄ (cat.) | Protonated acid attacked by ROH | Reversible (K ≈ 1–4); remove H₂O to drive forward | Neutral ester, distilled |
| Saponification | aq. NaOH (stoichiometric) | HO⁻ attacks ester; tetrahedral intermediate collapses to carboxylate | Irreversible (RCOO⁻ is the sink) | Acidify to obtain RCOOH |
| Claisen condensation | NaOR′ (1 eq., strong) | Enolate of one ester attacks second ester; β-ketoester deprotonated | Reversible, but driven by deprotonation of acidic β-ketoester product (pKₐ ≈ 11) | Aqueous acid work-up gives neutral β-ketoester |

#### Claisen Mechanism — Step-by-Step

1. NaOR′ removes α-H of ester A → **enolate**.
2. Enolate C attacks the **carbonyl carbon** of ester B → **tetrahedral intermediate**.
3. Intermediate expels R′O⁻ → β-ketoester.
4. Base deprotonates the β-ketoester (pKₐ ≈ 11, much more acidic than the starting ester) → drives equilibrium.
5. Aqueous **H₃O⁺ work-up** protonates the enolate to the neutral β-ketoester.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** A student mixes ethyl acetate (CH₃COOCH₂CH₃, 2.00 mol) with sodium ethoxide (NaOCH₂CH₃, 1.00 mol) in dry ethanol, then heats under reflux. After 30 min the mixture is cooled, poured into dilute HCl, and the organic layer analysed.

(a) Draw the structure of the major product.
(b) Explain **why only 0.5 equivalents** of base are theoretically required to push the reaction to completion, yet the textbook procedure uses **a full equivalent**.
(c) Give the IR absorption (cm⁻¹) you would look for to confirm product formation, and state the diagnostic peak absent in starting ethyl acetate.

#### Solution:
(a) Two CH₃COOEt molecules couple via the Claisen condensation to give **ethyl acetoacetate**, CH₃COCH₂COOCH₂CH₃ — a β-ketoester. The new C–C bond joins the α-carbon of one ester to the carbonyl carbon of the other.

(b) The β-ketoester product has pKₐ ≈ 11; the alkoxide base (EtO⁻, conjugate acid pKₐ ≈ 16) deprotonates it quantitatively, forming the stabilised enolate that drives the equilibrium. **One full equivalent of base is consumed** (one to deprotonate α-H of starting ester, then one to deprotonate the product). Using 0.5 eq. gives ~50% conversion because the second deprotonation never occurs, allowing the equilibrium to revert.

(c) Product shows a **C=O stretch near 1715–1740 cm⁻¹** (ester carbonyl, ~1735 cm⁻¹) AND a **ketone carbonyl ~1715 cm⁻¹**, plus a new **C–H stretch on the methine (–CH=) at ~3000 cm⁻¹** after enolisation. The diagnostic change vs. ethyl acetate is the appearance of a **keto C=O and an acidic methine H** (D₂O exchange test); pure ethyl acetate shows **no O–H broad band** (2500–3300 cm⁻¹, absent), confirming no acid impurity.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students often (i) draw the enolate attacking the **α-carbon** of the second ester (giving an alkoxide, not a β-ketoester — that's a Michael/alkylation, not Claisen), (ii) write "loss of H₂O" as the by-product (it is **R′OH**, ethanol), and (iii) forget the acidic work-up, leaving the sodium enolate salt instead of the neutral β-ketoester.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases & Boundary Conditions

- **No α-H → no Claisen.** Trimethyl acetate (CH₃)₃CCOOEt fails — you cannot form the enolate.
- **Mixed (crossed) Claisen.** Use a **non-enolisable acceptor** (e.g. ethyl benzoate, ArCOOEt) plus an enolisable donor (EtOAc) to obtain a single product; otherwise four β-ketoesters form.
- **Dieckmann cyclisation.** A **diester** (e.g. diethyl adipate, EtOOC–(CH₂)₄–COOEt) treated with NaOEt in dry ethanol gives a cyclic β-ketoester (cyclopentanone-2-carboxylate here) — intramolecular Claisen. Best for 5- and 6-membered rings; 3- and 4-membered rings are too strained.
- **Acetoacetic ester synthesis.** Hydrolyse and decarboxylate the β-ketoester (loss of CO₂, ~pH 5) to produce a methyl ketone — a workhorse in IEB synthesis problems.
- **IR fingerprint shifts:** conjugation lowers C=O by ~20–30 cm⁻¹ (e.g. ethyl benzoate ~1715 vs. ethyl acetate ~1735). Amide I/II bands (~1650, ~1550 cm⁻¹) uniquely identify amides.
- **¹H NMR acid derivatives:** ester OCH₂ quartet ~4.1 ppm (δ); α-CH₂ of β-ketoester enol ~3.4 ppm (vinyl-H) and keto form ~3.6 ppm (CH₂ between two C=O).

#### Advanced Traps & Exceptions

| Trap | What students assume | Correct behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Reversibility of Fischer esterification | Reaction "goes to completion" | K ≈ 1–4; Le Chatelier — remove H₂O or use excess ROH |
| Saponification kinetics | First-order in ester | With [NaOH] >> [ester], pseudo-first-order: k = (1/t)·ln([A]₀/[A]) |
| Amide reactivity | "Amide hydrolyses faster than ester" | Amide is **least reactive** under neutral/acidic conditions; only forced hydrolysis (H₃O⁺, heat, 6 M HCl) or enzymatic/strong-base hydrolysis |
| Enolate regiochemistry | "NaOEt gives only the thermodynamic enolate" | NaOEt gives **thermodynamic**; **LDA at −78 °C** gives the **kinetic (less-substituted)** enolate cleanly |
| Crossed Claisen | Equimolar mix gives one product | Use the **non-enolisable partner in excess**; otherwise expect a statistical mixture |

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics

- **Aldol condensation** uses the same enolate chemistry but attacks an aldehyde/ketone C=O, not an ester C=O → β-hydroxy carbonyl, dehydrating to α,β-unsaturated carbonyl.
- **Michael addition** delivers the enolate to an α,β-unsaturated acceptor (1,4-addition).
- **Acetoacetic ester synthesis** ties Claisen products to **decarboxylation** and **malonic ester synthesis** (also a Claisen-type disconnection).
- **IR spectroscopy** of carboxylic acids: broad O–H (2500–3300 cm⁻¹) overlapping C–H stretches — easily mistaken for an alcohol.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. **Dieckmann product:** Diethyl suberate, EtOOC–(CH₂)₆–COOEt (0.05 mol), + NaOEt (0.05 mol), EtOH, reflux → draw the cyclic β-ketoester and predict its ¹H NMR (methine CH, δ ≈ 3.4 ppm; ring –CH₂– groups, δ 1.6–2.5 ppm).
2. **Crossed Claisen selectivity:** Ethyl acetate (1.0 mol) + ethyl benzoate (1.0 mol) + NaOEt (1.0 mol). Predict the major product, the by-products, and propose a one-line change that would give a single β-ketoester in >80% yield.
