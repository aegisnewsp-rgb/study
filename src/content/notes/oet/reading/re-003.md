# Part C: Inference, Attitude and Gist

The official OET Test Handbook 2026 sets out a Reading sub-test of 60 minutes across three parts, with 42 questions and the note that "Part A takes 15 minutes. Parts B and C take 45 minutes." It also states that Reading is the same for every candidate regardless of profession, and that one mark is awarded per correct answer with the score out of 42 converted to the 0 to 500 scale.

Part C is where inference lives, and it is the part candidates find hardest despite containing no specialist vocabulary — the difficulty is entirely about calibration, not language.

## The calibration problem

A plausible answer is not a supported answer. Part C items are built so that one option is what you would say if you were inferring from general knowledge, one is what you would say if you were guessing from the tone, and one is what the text actually supports.

The discipline is to be able to point at the sentence that licenses your answer. If you cannot, you are choosing between distractors, however confident you feel.

Consider a text that notes a hospital trust introduced an electronic referral system and that referral times fell. An option saying the trust believed the new system would reduce waiting lists is not supported — nothing in the text reports anyone's belief, only the outcome. An option saying referrals completed faster after the system was introduced is supported, and it says no more than the text says.

## Attitude is read from evidence, not impression

Part C often asks what the writer thinks or how a described person feels. Read it from what they choose to emphasise and what they decline to say.

Three tells. Vocabulary: a writer who uses loaded or qualifying language is signalling something, and a writer who uses flat factual language and then concedes a limitation in the same sentence is signalling scepticism. Concession structure: a paragraph that states a strong case and then ends with "although", "Nevertheless", "it remains unclear whether" is not a strong case, and the concession is usually what the question wants. Detail selection: what a writer chooses not to mention is informative, but only when the omission is conspicuous — an article about a treatment failure that never mentions risk is making a point by omission.

Beware the pitfall of inferring stance from tone alone. A writer can be neutral in tone and highly sceptical in content.

## Gist questions are about proportion

Gist items ask for the main idea, which is not the same as the title-like topic and not the first paragraph. The main idea is the claim a reasonable reader would write down afterwards.

Test a candidate by paraphrase: if it needs the text's vocabulary to be recognisable, it is probably too narrow. If it would be true of half the articles in the field, it is too broad. Main ideas are claims about what the text establishes, not subjects it mentions.

## Common mistakes

Choosing the most medically plausible option. Plausibility is exactly what the distractors trade in, and a clinical prior has no authority over the text.

Reading the whole part before attempting items. Part C sits inside the 45 minutes shared with Part B, and reading order should be set by the questions.

Inferring a writer's opinion from tone. Attitude is carried by what is argued and how, not by how warmly it is written.

Mistaking a stated limitation for an implied criticism. "The trial was too small to detect a difference" is a limitation; calling it a failure is an interpretation the text may not support.

Answering a "main idea" item with a subheading. The main idea is the claim the whole text supports; subheadings are sections of it.

Skipping an item because two options look defensible. In Part C the ambiguity usually resolves on close reading of one word, so reading the stem again is faster than guessing.
