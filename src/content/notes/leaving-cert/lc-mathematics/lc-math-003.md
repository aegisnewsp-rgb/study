# Number — Leaving Certificate (Established) Mathematics Notes

Number is one of the five strands of the Leaving Certificate Mathematics syllabus, sitting at weight 3 in the catalogue — lower than Algebra and Functions but still substantive because it carries the financial mathematics that appears across every Ordinary-level paper and a large portion of the Higher-level Paper 1 short questions. The strand covers arithmetic, percentages, indices, surds, complex numbers, financial mathematics (compound interest, APR, depreciation, amortisation) and counting principles including permutations and combinations. At Higher level, complex numbers add a substantial block of procedural content that does not appear at Ordinary; financial mathematics at Higher extends into amortisation schedules and the present-value chain.

What makes Number a high-yield revision strand is that the procedures are reliable and the marks are cheap. A correctly set-up compound interest calculation, a correctly applied permutation, or a correctly simplified surd each takes 30 to 60 seconds to execute and yields between two and four marks. Candidates who over-invest revision time in Functions and Algebra frequently under-prepare Number, leaving three or four cheap marks per paper uncollected. The strategic implication is clear: Number is a foundation-tier strand in difficulty terms but a high-tier strand in mark-recuperation terms.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Strand weight & spread**: Weight 3/5 in the catalogue. Marks are spread across both papers at Ordinary and Higher level. Short questions on percentages and indices appear early on Paper 1; longer financial mathematics items typically appear mid-paper; permutations/combinations and surds appear as standalone short questions.
- **Levels that include each sub-topic**: Arithmetic, percentages, indices, surds and basic counting appear at all three levels. Compound interest, depreciation, APR, amortisation and permutations/combinations are assessed at Ordinary and Higher. Complex numbers are Higher level only.
- **First move on any Number question**: Identify the *type* of question before reaching for the formula sheet. Percentage change vs compound interest vs APR vs amortisation each use a different formula; using the wrong one is the most common error.
- **Percentage change**: New = Original × (1 ± rate/100). A 12% increase followed by a 12% decrease does *not* return to the original — the second operation is applied to a different base.
- **Compound interest**: A = P(1 ± r/n)^(nt). Identify P, r, n, t before substituting. The difference between APR and AER is the compounding frequency — the examiner's marks depend on choosing the right one.
- **Surds**: Simplify by extracting perfect squares (√72 = 6√2). Rationalise the denominator by multiplying top and bottom by the conjugate. Add and subtract surds by collecting like terms.
- **Indices**: a^m × a^n = a^(m+n); a^m / a^n = a^(m−n); (a^m)^n = a^(mn); a^0 = 1 (for a ≠ 0); a^(-n) = 1/a^n. Each is a one-line rule that candidates occasionally invert.
- **Permutations vs combinations**: If *order matters*, use nPr. If *order does not matter*, use nCr. The Leaving Certificate's exam question almost always states "in how many ways can..." — if the chosen arrangement is distinguishable from a rearrangement, the answer is nPr.
- **Complex numbers (Higher)**: i² = −1. Add, subtract and multiply complex numbers using the standard rules of algebra with i² replaced by −1. Divide by multiplying by the conjugate.

#### Examiner traps

- Quoting the compound interest formula with the rate not converted from a percentage (using 5 instead of 0.05) — the formula's exponent is sensitive to the form of r.
- Confusing *present value* with *future value*. "What is the lump sum I would need to invest today" is present value; "what will my investment be worth in 10 years" is future value.
- Forgetting that an amortisation schedule pays off the loan at the *end* of the schedule — the final payment may be smaller than the others if the schedule rounds to a whole currency amount.
- Adding surds that cannot be added: √2 + √3 cannot be simplified into a single surd. Candidates who write √5 lose a mark.
- Using nCr where nPr was intended: "in how many ways can a committee of three be chosen from twelve" is combinations; "in how many ways can a chair, secretary and treasurer be chosen" is permutations.
- Multiplying out a complex number product and forgetting to replace i² with −1 — the standardisation mark depends on this substitution.

#### 30-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Can you write the compound interest formula, the APR/AER distinction and an amortisation line from memory?
- [ ] Can you simplify, add, subtract and rationalise surds in under 60 seconds each?
- [ ] Can you decide between nPr and nCr for any "in how many ways" question before reaching for the calculator?
- [ ] Can you add, multiply and divide complex numbers, replacing i² with −1 at every step?

### 🟡 Standard — Structured Study (1d–1mo)

#### Financial mathematics across the two papers

Financial mathematics is the largest sub-topic in the Number strand at both Ordinary and Higher levels. The four operations are: simple interest, compound interest, depreciation and amortisation. Each is tested through a word problem that begins "Maria invested €X at r% per annum compounded annually..." or "A loan of €Y is to be repaid over n years at r% per annum..." The procedural skill is to identify which operation is being described, write the formula, substitute and solve. The interpretive skill is to identify what the question is *asking* — present value, future value, monthly repayment, total interest paid.

Compound interest at Ordinary level typically runs over a small number of years with annual compounding. At Higher level the exam extends the scenario with monthly compounding (n = 12), continuous compounding (using e^(rt) — confirm the live Higher-level specification on examinations.ie for whether this remains in the syllabus) and chained compound growth where the rate changes mid-period. Candidates who can write A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt) from memory and identify P, r, n, t correctly before substituting are well-prepared for the financial mathematics items at both levels.

#### Indices and surds in exam context

Indices and surds appear as short questions worth between two and four marks each. An indices question at Ordinary level typically tests one or two of the index laws (multiplying, dividing, raising to a power) with a small number of operations. At Higher level the indices question may chain three or four operations together, ending with a numerical value that the candidate must evaluate.

Surds at Ordinary level require only the basic operations: simplifying √72 to 6√2; rationalising 1/(√3 − 1) by multiplying by (√3 + 1)/(√3 + 1); adding and subtracting like surds. At Higher level, surds appear in the context of geometry (the length of a chord, the distance between two points) and in the quadratic formula (where roots frequently involve surds). Candidates who can rationalise denominators reliably recover a mark that many candidates forfeit.

#### Counting principles and probability bridges

Permutations and combinations form the bridge between Number and Probability. The exam rarely tests them as standalone questions; instead, a probability question begins "five cards are drawn from a deck of 52... what is the probability of..." and the candidate must use nCr to count the favourable outcomes and the total outcomes before dividing. The procedural skill is to identify whether order matters in the *selection* (almost always no, hence nCr) or in the *arrangement* (yes for seating, no for selection).

#### Complex numbers at Higher level

Complex numbers at Higher level are introduced as an extension of the number system, with i = √(−1) and i² = −1 as the defining relation. The exam tests four operations: addition, subtraction, multiplication and division. Addition and subtraction are component-wise. Multiplication uses the distributive law, replacing i² with −1 wherever it appears. Division uses the conjugate: (a + bi)/(c + di) = (a + bi)(c − di) / (c² + d²).

A typical Higher-level question presents two complex numbers z₁ and z₂ and asks the candidate to compute z₁ + z₂, z₁ × z₂, z₁/z₂, and the modulus of one of the results. The procedural skill is reliable; the trick is in spotting which modulus formula applies — |a + bi| = √(a² + b²). Candidates who confuse the modulus formula with the Pythagorean formula in coordinate geometry lose the mark.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Full Mastery (1mo+)

#### Financial mathematics at the highest level

At the highest level, financial mathematics chains two or more operations: a depreciation schedule followed by a compound-interest calculation, an amortisation schedule with a balloon payment at the end, or a present-value calculation chained to a future-value calculation. Each part tests a separate sub-skill, and the marks are awarded per part. Candidates who skip the first part to attempt the second lose the lead-in marks.

The depreciation question at Higher level typically tests reducing-balance depreciation, where the value at the end of year n is V = P(1 − r)^n, and straight-line depreciation, where the value decreases by a fixed amount each year. The examiner distinguishes between the two by the language: "the value decreases by r% each year" implies reducing-balance; "the value decreases by €X each year" implies straight-line. Mixing the two formulas is the most common Higher-level financial mathematics error.

#### Complex numbers and polynomial roots (Higher)

The hardest complex number questions at Higher level connect to polynomial roots: "show that 1 + i is a root of x² − 2x + 2 and find the other root." The procedural skill is to substitute 1 + i into the polynomial, use i² = −1 to simplify, and confirm the result is zero. The other root follows from the sum-of-roots or product-of-roots relation. Candidates who attempt to factor directly without first verifying the given root lose the lead-in mark.

A second Higher-level extension uses complex numbers in the context of geometry on the Argand diagram. A complex number z = a + bi is plotted as the point (a, b); the modulus |z| is the distance from the origin; the argument is the angle from the positive real axis. The exam tests locus questions: "find the locus of z given |z − (1 + i)| = 2" describes a circle centred at (1, 1) with radius 2. Candidates who can plot complex numbers and recognise geometric loci are well-prepared for this item.

#### Common mistakes catalogue

1. **Compound interest rate form**: using r = 5 instead of r = 0.05 in the formula. The exponent doubles the effect of any error — a 1% rate error produces a 1% × t error in the exponent, which compounds to a substantial numerical error.
2. **Amortisation final payment**: assuming the final payment is the same as all others. The schedule typically rounds to a whole currency amount, leaving a residual that must be paid off in the final instalment.
3. **Surds addition**: combining √2 + √3 into √5 — these are unlike terms and cannot be added.
4. **nCr vs nPr**: choosing nPr when the question describes a committee (order does not matter). The candidate should ask: "if the same three people were rearranged, would that produce a different committee?"
5. **Complex number arithmetic sign errors**: when multiplying (a + bi)(c + di), the cross terms must be combined correctly. The imaginary part is ad + bc, not ad − bc. A sign slip here cascades through the rest of the calculation.

#### Long-run mastery schedule

A four-week pre-mock plan for Number at Higher level:

- **Week 1**: Drill the four financial mathematics formulas until they can be written from memory in under 30 seconds. Identify P, r, n, t in five different word problems per day.
- **Week 2**: Drill surds and indices with timed exercises. Set a 10-minute timer for ten questions; aim for ten correct answers. The skill is automatic, not thought-through.
- **Week 3**: Work every past paper's complex number question. Plot the numbers on the Argand diagram; check the locus against the modulus or argument.
- **Week 4**: Mock-exam week. Solve three past papers under timed conditions; mark against the marking scheme; write the "errors I keep making" list; re-drill those errors specifically.
