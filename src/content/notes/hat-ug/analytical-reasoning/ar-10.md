# Puzzles

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Puzzles** in the HAT-UG Analytical Reasoning section are logic problems that ask you to fix the **order, position, or rank** of objects, people, days, or attributes using a set of written clues. The five question families you must recognise are **linear arrangement**, **circular/seating arrangement**, **scheduling (day-based)**, **comparison/ranking**, and **direction sense**. Always draw a diagram — a line, a circle, or a 2×N/3×N grid — before marking options; this single habit eliminates 70% of wrong answers. Two high-yield rules: apply the **transitive property** (if A > B and B > C, then A > C), and start with the **most constrained clue** (the one mentioning the most entities, or the one with "not"/"neither-nor"). HAT-UG typically gives 2–3 puzzle sets, each followed by 4–5 MCQs carrying roughly 4% of the total score.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Question Families
A puzzle is a small **constraint-satisfaction** problem. The examiner hands you 5–8 clues and asks for the final arrangement, a missing position, or a "which of the following must be true" inference. The five families differ only in geometry:

| Family | Working Model | Typical Clue Words |
|---|---|---|
| Linear | A horizontal line with N slots | left of, right of, extreme end, adjacent to |
| Circular | Persons around a round table | clockwise, facing centre, immediate neighbour |
| Scheduling | Days, months, time slots | Monday, first week, only one gap |
| Ranking | Vertical order or numeric rank | taller than, third from top |
| Direction sense | A grid with N-S-E-W axes | starts, turns, faces |

#### Types of Clues
- **Direct clues** fix a position outright ("A sits at the extreme left"). Place these first.
- **Negative clues** ("D is not between A and C") eliminate slots; treat them as restrictions.
- **Comparative clues** ("P is taller than Q") feed into the transitive chain.
- **Conditional clues** ("If R is at position 2, then S is at position 4") demand a case split when the condition may or may not hold.

#### Solving Procedure
1. Read every clue once to identify the entities and the geometry.
2. Lock the **most constrained clue** (the one referencing the maximum entities or a definite position).
3. Use the transitive rule to propagate: if X→Y and Y→Z, infer X→Z.
4. Mark negative clues with a cross on the diagram to forbid slots.
5. Re-check the arrangement against every clue before finalising.

#### Standard HAT-UG Pattern
Each puzzle set has a short passage (4–6 sentences) followed by 4–5 MCQs. The first question usually asks for the full arrangement; later questions test "must be true" / "cannot be true" / "could be true" distinctions, which require checking alternatives, not just the one solution.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Traps
The most expensive mistakes in HAT-UG puzzles are linguistic, not logical.

- **"Between" ≠ "Next to".** "B sits between A and C" forces A and C to flank B with one slot gap; it does **not** make A and C adjacent to B. A common trap option places A or C directly beside B.
- **"Immediate left" is direction-sensitive in circular sets.** "Immediate left of A" depends on whether persons face the centre or face outward. Always re-read the stem's facing direction.
- **Multiple-valid-arrangement traps.** When clues are sufficient to fix a *partial* order but not every slot, "must be true" is false even though the arrangement feels determined. Verify by trying a counter-example.
- **Variable vs fixed count.** A puzzle with 7 persons and 7 days is full; a puzzle with 6 persons and 7 days has one empty slot, which changes "extreme" answers.

#### Worked Micro-Example
Six friends — A, B, C, D, E, F — sit in a row. Clues: (i) A is at the extreme left; (ii) B sits immediately to the right of A; (iii) D is not at either end; (iv) F is immediately to the left of C; (v) E sits between B and D.
Place A at slot 1, B at slot 2. By (v), E lies between B and D, so E is at slot 3 and D at slot 4 (D cannot be at an end, so this fits). By (iv), F is left of C; the only free adjacent pair is slots 5–6, giving F at 5 and C at 6. Final order: **A, B, E, D, F, C**.

#### Common Mistakes
- Skipping the diagram and reasoning verbally — works for 3 entities, fails for 6+.
- Forgetting to **revisit** earlier deductions after a later clue forces a change.
- Treating a "could be true" question as "must be true".

#### Practice Prompts
1. Seven employees P–V stand in a line. P stands third from the left end. Q is at an extreme end. R stands exactly between P and S. M is to the immediate right of T, who is at the right extreme. U is neither at any extreme nor adjacent to P. Find the position of V.
2. Eight persons sit around a circular table facing the centre. A sits opposite D. B sits two places to the right of A. C is immediate neighbour of D. E sits between B and F. G and H occupy the remaining seats. How many persons sit between H and C, moving clockwise from H?

#### Exam Strategy
In the HAT-UG paper, attempt puzzle sets **after** finishing coding-decoding and number-series items; puzzles consume disproportionate time. Allocate ~90 seconds per MCQ inside a set, and if the first question resists in 3 minutes, mark the set for review and move on — partial-attempt guessing on a tightly constrained set costs more than skipping.
