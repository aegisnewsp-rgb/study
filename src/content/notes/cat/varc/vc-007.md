# Vocabulary

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

CAT VARC has **no standalone vocabulary section**. Vocabulary is tested only *indirectly*, through Reading Comprehension passages, para-jumbles, summary questions, and odd-one-out items where word meaning drives the answer.

| Term | Working definition | CAT angle |
|---|---|---|
| **Denotation** | Literal dictionary meaning | Rarely the answer on tone questions |
| **Connotation** | Implied, associative, or emotional sense | Frequently the answer on inference items |
| **Polysemy** | One word, multiple related meanings | Disambiguated by passage context |
| **Register** | Formal, academic, journalistic, colloquial tone | Signals the writer's stance |
| **Collocation** | Words that naturally pair (e.g., *strong tea*, not *powerful tea*) | Surfaces in para-jumble fluency checks |
| **Idiom** | Fixed phrase whose meaning ≠ sum of its parts (e.g., *kick the bucket*) | Tested in VA fill-in-the-blanks |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **D-C-P-R-C-I** — **D**enotation, **C**onnotation, **P**olysemy, **R**egister, **C**ollocation, **I**dioms. CAT never asks "what does the word mean?" — it asks "what does the word mean *here*?"

Inference is the test: pick the meaning that fits the author's tone, not the meaning that appears first in a dictionary.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

CAT VARC rewards readers who treat vocabulary as **contextual reasoning**, not list memorisation. The IIMCAT.ac.in pattern confirms VARC contains 24 questions split across RC and VA, and every one of them is mediated by how precisely you read word meaning in context.

#### Core Mechanisms You Must Master

**1. Denotation vs Connotation.** The dictionary gives you *fast*; the passage gives you *speedy*. CAT inference questions hinge on connotation — the mood, attitude, or evaluative charge a word carries. *Slim* (denotative: thin) and *gaunt* (connotative: thin in a wasted way) are not interchangeable.

**2. Polysemy and Disambiguation.** Many editorials use the same word in two senses within five lines (e.g., *bank* as financial institution and river bank). The right reading is the one that keeps the passage internally consistent.

**3. Collocational Fit.** Para-jumble and odd-sentence-out questions punish sentences whose transitions clash with normal word pairing (*heavy rain* fits, *strong rain* jars for British editorial register).

**4. Register Shifts.** Academic prose uses *utilise, demonstrate, considerable*; journalism uses *use, show, large*. A shift often marks an author's stance change or a contrasting viewpoint — the trigger for many RC inference items.

**5. Etymology as a Decoder.** Latin roots (*-dict* = say, *-cred* = believe), Greek roots (*-logy* = study), and French borrowings (*culprit, liaison*) appear in CAT editorials. A 60-second root review before the exam unlocks 5–10 unknown words.

**6. Idioms and Phrasal Verbs.** *Red herring*, *white elephant*, *move the goalposts*, *carry the day* — their meaning is non-compositional. CAT VA fill-in-the-blank sets have historically included 1–2 idioms per shift.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Pair | Denotation | Connotation | CAT-tested distinction |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Economical / Cheap* | Cost-saving | Frugal vs low-quality | Tone: *cheap* is pejorative |
| *Assert / Claim* | State | Confident vs disputable | RC author stance |
| *Slim / Gaunt* | Thin | Attractive vs unhealthy | Inference from health RC |
| *Propaganda / Information* | Communication | Biased vs neutral | Editorial tone judgment |
| *Resigned / Surrendered* | Gave up | Graceful vs defeated | Mood of a memoir passage |

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** A CAT RC passage argues that governments' *ostensible* commitment to fiscal discipline has not translated into actual deficit reduction. A question asks: *"The author's use of 'ostensible' most nearly means…"* Options: (A) Genuine, (B) Apparent, (C) Questionable, (D) Public.

#### Solution:
1. Decode the root: Latin *ostendere* = to show. *Ostensible* = shown to the public, professed.
2. Read the clause: commitment has *not* translated into reduction → the commitment is professed but not real.
3. Match meaning: *apparent / professed but not actual* fits exactly.
4. Eliminate: (A) **Genuine** contradicts "not translated"; (C) **Questionable** overstates the author's judgement; (D) **Public** is incomplete — public commitments can still be genuine.
5. **Answer: (B) Apparent.**

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students who pick (D) *Public* confuse etymology with meaning. *Ostensible* is not merely "public" — it specifically means *shown/professed on the surface*, which is why the author uses it to flag a gap between appearance and reality.

#### Standard Error Types in VARC

1. **Dictionary-first trap** — picking the first dictionary synonym over the context-fit meaning.
2. **Literal idiom trap** — reading *kick the bucket* as physical kicking.
3. **Register-clash trap** — forcing a colloquial sentence into an academic para-jumble.
4. **Root-blindness trap** — skipping etymology and guessing from the first two letters.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

Vocabulary mastery for CAT is a **comprehension multiplier**, not a separate skill. IIMBx's published CAT pattern notes emphasise that VARC rewards *reading between the lines*, and every "between the lines" judgment is a vocabulary judgment.

#### Advanced Word-Classes That Surface in CAT Editorials

| Word-class | Example | Why CAT uses it |
|---|---|---|
| **Hedge words** | *seemingly, ostensibly, purportedly* | Signal author skepticism |
| **Booster words** | *clearly, undeniably, patently* | Signal author certainty |
| **Evaluative adjectives** | *egregious, paltry, hefty, modest* | Convey tone without stating opinion |
| **Nominalisations** | *implementation, commodification, neoliberalisation* | Distant, abstract register of op-eds |
| **Foreign loanwords** | *zeitgeist, schadenfreude, fait accompli, bête noire* | Literary and economic RC passages |

#### Edge Cases and Boundary Conditions

- **Same word, opposite charge across genres.** *Radical* in a political RC = extreme; in a mathematics RC = relating to roots. Always re-anchor to the passage's domain.
- **False friends across registers.** *Sympathetic* in medicine = showing similar symptoms; in an editorial = showing support. Mixing registers breaks inference chains.
- **Polysemy chains.** A single passage may use *culture* to mean (a) arts, (b) cultivation, (c) organisational norms. Track the referent across paragraphs or your inference collapses.
- **Collocation cascades.** *Strong* collocates with *tea, argument, candidate* but not with *rain* (standard British) or *car* (use *powerful*). Para-jumble transition sentences often hinge on a single adjective-noun pair.

#### Connection to Adjacent VARC Topics

- **Reading Comprehension:** vocabulary is the silent substrate of every inference, tone, and assumption question.
- **Para-jumbles:** collocational fluency determines whether a transition sentence lands naturally in position B or D.
- **Odd-Sentence-Out:** the odd sentence is usually the one whose register, collocations, or evaluative charge differ from the others.
- **Paragraph Summaries:** picking the right summary depends on tracking evaluative adjectives (*modest gains* vs *meagre gains*) across the original.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. Take three consecutive CAT-editorial paragraphs. Circle every hedge, booster, and evaluative adjective. Without re-reading the prose, write one sentence explaining the author's stance from the circled words alone.
2. Build a 50-word personal list of foreign loanwords and polysemous words from the last month of *The Economist* or *The Hindu* op-eds. Test yourself on connotation, not denotation.

> 📌 **Exam Strategy:** Allocate ~2 minutes per RC passage for a fast *register scan* — note the writer's tone words in the margin. When an inference question appears, return to your margin notes. This single habit lifts vocabulary accuracy on tone items from ~60% to ~80% across a 24-question VARC section.
