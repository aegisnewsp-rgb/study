# Pakistan Affairs: Historical Foundations and the Creation of Pakistan

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

The Pakistan Movement grew from the **Two-Nation Theory** — the idea that Hindus and Muslims are separate nations by faith, history, and culture, therefore requiring a separate Muslim homeland. The **All-India Muslim League** (founded 30 December 1906 at Dhaka) became the political vehicle for this demand. **Sir Syed Ahmad Khan** seeded the intellectual groundwork through the Aligarh Movement (1875), **Allama Iqbal** articulated a Muslim state's vision in his Allahabad Address (December 1930), and **Quaid-i-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah** converted that vision into a constitutional demand through the **Lahore Resolution (23 March 1940)**.

- **Pakistan emerged on 14 August 1947** under the **Indian Independence Act 1947**, with boundaries drawn by the **Radcliffe Award**.
- The **Cabinet Mission Plan (1946)** was the last attempt at a united India; its collapse and **Direct Action Day (16 August 1946)** precipitated partition.
- **KPPSC weightage:** ~1–2 marks, usually MCQs on dates (1906, 1930, 1940, 1947), personalities, or the wording of the Lahore Resolution.

| Landmark | Date | Significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Muslim League founded | 30 Dec 1906 | Dhaka; political platform for Muslim demands |
| Allahabad Address | Dec 1930 | Iqbal's vision of a Muslim state |
| Lahore Resolution | 23 Mar 1940 | Demands independent Muslim states |
| Independence | 14 Aug 1947 | Pakistan becomes a sovereign Dominion |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Ideological Roots: The Two-Nation Theory

The Two-Nation Theory is the philosophical bedrock of the Pakistan Movement. It holds that Hindus and Muslims are not merely two religious communities but two distinct nations with divergent historical trajectories, cultural traditions, and social structures. The theory did not emerge from a single mind; it evolved through Sir Syed Ahmad Khan's warnings after 1857, the Urdu-Hindi controversy, and the Hindu–Muslim riots of the 1920s. Iqbal gave it philosophical depth, and Jinnah translated it into a political programme at the League's Allahabad session (1930) and the Lahore session (1940).

#### The Muslim League and Electoral Politics

Separate electorates were first conceded by the **Minto-Morley Reforms 1909**, giving Muslims reserved seats and a distinct political identity. The **Lucknow Pact 1916** briefly aligned the League with the Congress, but the pact broke down over issues such as the Khilafat Movement (1920–22) and the Nehru Report (1928). Jinnah's **Fourteen Points (1929)** failed to win Congress acceptance, pushing the League toward separatism.

#### The Road to Partition

The **Congress Ministries (1937–39)** deepened Muslim anxieties through what the Pirpur and Shareef Reports documented as anti-Muslim policies in UP, Bihar, and elsewhere. World War II forced the British to seek an exit: the Cripps Mission (1942) failed, as did the Gandhi–Jinnah talks (1944). The **Cabinet Mission Plan (1946)** proposed a weak central federation with grouped provinces; the League accepted it initially but withdrew, prompting **Direct Action Day (16 August 1946)** — the Great Calcutta Killings triggered the chain of communal violence that made partition inevitable. The **Indian Independence Act (18 July 1947)** and the **Radcliffe Award** finally formalised the creation of Pakistan on 14 August 1947.

- **Distinct feature of the movement:** unlike India's largely secular Congress-led nationalism, Pakistan's demand rested on a religious-cultural identity combined with economic and political grievances (Muslim under-representation in civil services, industry, and education).
- **KPPSC exam tip:** examiners commonly test the *plural* ("independent states") wording of the Lahore Resolution and the correct chronology of the Cripps (1942), Cabinet Mission (1946), and Mountbatten Plan (1947).
- **Common trap:** confusing the Muslim League (1906, Dhaka) with the All-India Muslim Conference (1928, Delhi), which briefly rivaled the League on Muslim representation.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Misinterpretations

Several standard textbook claims need careful handling. The Lahore Resolution's original text used the phrase "independent states" in the plural, not "one state" — the singular interpretation was refined by the League's later proceedings, especially Jinnah's 1940–46 speeches and the Muslim League's 1946 demand for "Pakistan" as a single constitutional unit. Iqbal's 1930 Address envisaged a Muslim state within an Indian framework, possibly federated with Hindu-majority provinces; Jinnah and the post-1940 League radicalised this into full sovereignty.

#### Comparative Missions and Constitutional Offers

Three British constitutional initiatives shaped the endgame. Each must be distinguished:

| Initiative | Year | Key Proposal | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cripps Mission | 1942 | Dominion status after war; Indian constituent assembly | Rejected by both Congress and League |
| Cabinet Mission | 1946 | Weak centre; grouped provinces (Group A, B, C) | League accepted then withdrew; led to Direct Action Day |
| Mountbatten Plan / 3rd June Plan | 1947 | Partition of Punjab and Bengal; early transfer of power | Accepted; preceded the Indian Independence Act |

#### Economic and Administrative Grievances

The Muslim economic case rested on the under-representation visible in the Simon Commission-era data: Muslims formed roughly one-quarter of British India's population but held fewer than 10% of senior ICS posts and a small share of industrial ownership, especially in jute (Bengal) and cotton (Punjab). Sir Muhammad Iqbal and Chaudhry Rahmat Ali both emphasised that without a sovereign homeland, Muslim-majority regions would remain politically dominated by Hindu-majority provinces in any federal India.

#### Common Mistakes and Exam Strategy

1. Confusing **Cripps Mission (1942)** with **Cabinet Mission (1946)** — dates are the easiest giveaway; examiners love this MCQ.
2. Attributing Pakistan's *creation* to 1940 (Lahore Resolution) instead of 1947 — 1940 is the *demand*, 1947 is the *achievement*.
3. Misstating Iqbal's address year as 1929 or 1940 — it is **December 1930**, at the League's Allahabad session.
4. Calling the Radcliffe Award the "legal basis" of independence — it merely demarcated the boundary; the **Indian Independence Act 1947** is the legal basis.
5. Treating Direct Action Day as the *cause* of partition; it was an *escalation* following the Cabinet Mission's collapse.

> **Exam strategy:** allocate 30–45 seconds per MCQ in the Pakistan Affairs block. Memorise four anchor dates (1906, 1930, 1940, 1947) and four anchor names (Sir Syed, Iqbal, Jinnah, Rahmat Ali) — they unlock ~70% of the factual questions on this topic.

#### Practice Prompts

1. *"Critically examine the role of the Two-Nation Theory in the Pakistan Movement."* — Frame your answer around Sir Syed → Iqbal → Jinnah, and explicitly address whether the theory was religious, cultural, or political in nature.
2. *"Why did the Cabinet Mission Plan fail?"* — Discuss the Congress's preference for a strong centre, the League's strategic withdrawal, the breakdown of coalition talks with Nehru, and the Jinnah–Mountbatten correspondence leading to Direct Action Day.
