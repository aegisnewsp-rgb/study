# Error Spotting

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before CUET English Section IA.

**Top 6 Error Spotting Rules Tested in CUET UG**
1. **Subject-Verb Agreement (Proximity vs. Precedence)**:
   - When subjects are joined by *either...or*, *neither...nor*, or *not only...but also*, the verb agrees with the **closest subject**:
     - *Neither the captain nor the players **were** [plural] ready.*
     - *Neither the players nor the captain **was** [singular] ready.*
   - When joined by *as well as*, *along with*, *together with*, *in addition to*, or *accompanied by*, the verb agrees with the **first subject**:
     - *The teacher, along with all her students, **was** [singular] honored.*
2. **Indefinite Pronouns are Singular**:
   - Words such as *each*, *every*, *everyone*, *everybody*, *anyone*, *anybody*, *no one*, *nobody*, *neither*, and *either* take a **singular verb** and **singular pronoun**:
     - Incorrect: *Each of the participants must bring their own equipment.*
     - Correct: *Each of the participants must bring **his or her** own equipment.*
3. **Redundancy and Pleonasm Traps**:
   - *Despite* never takes *of* (*Despite the rain*, NOT *Despite of the rain*).
   - *Comprise* never takes *of* in active voice (*The committee comprises ten members*, NOT *comprises of*).
   - Never say *return back*, *revert back*, *repeat again*, or *cope up with* (the correct idiom is *cope with*).
4. **Conditional Sentence Tense Harmony**:
   - Zero Conditional: *If + Present, Present* (*If you heat ice, it melts*).
   - First Conditional: *If + Present, Will + Verb* (*If it rains, we will stay inside*).
   - Second Conditional: *If + Past Subjunctive (Were), Would + Verb* (*If I were you, I would accept*).
   - Third Conditional: *If + Past Perfect (Had + V3), Would Have + V3* (*If she had studied, she would have passed*).
5. **Dangling and Misplaced Modifiers**:
   - An introductory participial phrase must modify the subject immediately following the comma:
     - Incorrect: *Walking through the botanical garden, the roses smelled heavenly.* (Roses weren't walking).
     - Correct: *Walking through the botanical garden, **she** noticed the heavenly scent of roses.*
6. ⚡ **CUET Time Shortcut**: Read the sentence in full without looking at division slashes first. Natural cadence often pinpoints jarring dissonance immediately before formal grammatical parsing.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Detailed taxonomy of grammatical error categories, sentence patterns, and structural parallelism.

#### Grammatical Error Classification Matrix

| Error Domain | Underlying Grammatical Rule | Incorrect Construction Example | Corrected Construction | High-Yield Exam Tip |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Correlative Conjunctions** | Balanced correlatives must link grammatically identical syntactic elements | *She not only visited Paris but also London.* | *She visited **not only Paris but also London**.* | Both elements following *not only* and *but also* must be parallel nouns. |
| **Inversion after Negative Adverbs** | When sentences begin with *seldom*, *rarely*, *scarcely*, *hardly*, or *no sooner*, the auxiliary verb precedes the subject | *Scarcely he had left the room when the phone rang.* | *Scarcely **had he left** the room **when** the phone rang.* | *Scarcely/Hardly* pairs with *when*; *No sooner* pairs with *than*. |
| **Subjunctive Mood** | After verbs of demand, request, or proposal (*insist, recommend, suggest, mandate*), the dependent clause uses the bare infinitive | *The professor insisted that every student submits the essay on Monday.* | *The professor insisted that every student **submit** the essay on Monday.* | Drop the third-person singular "-s"; use the bare root form regardless of subject. |
| **Comparative Degrees** | Comparative forms like *superior, inferior, senior, junior, prior, anterior, prefer* take the preposition *to*, not *than* | *This laptop model is superior than the older edition.* | *This laptop model is superior **to** the older edition.* | Never combine comparative affixes (*more superior* is redundant and incorrect). |
| **Uncountable Nouns** | Nouns such as *furniture, luggage, advice, information, scenery, machinery, poetry* have no plural form and take singular verbs | *The furnitures in the gallery were imported from Italy.* | *The **furniture** in the gallery **was** imported from Italy.* | To quantify, use counters: *a piece of advice*, *three articles of luggage*. |
| **Relative Pronouns (Who vs. Whom)** | *Who* acts as grammatical subject; *Whom* acts as grammatical object | *The candidate whom won the debate received a standing ovation.* | *The candidate **who** won the debate received a standing ovation.* | Replace with *he/she* (who) vs. *him/her* (whom) to verify function quickly. |

#### Structural Parallelism in Complex Sentences

Parallelism requires that elements in a series, comparison, or correlative construction share the same grammatical form.

```
Non-Parallel (Faulty):
She enjoys [swimming (Gerund)], [to hike (Infinitive)], and [books (Noun)].
   │
   ▼
Corrected Parallel Form:
She enjoys [swimming], [hiking], and [reading] (All Gerunds).
```

- **Comparisons with "That of" and "Those of"**:
  - Faulty: *The climate of Bangalore is cooler than Delhi.* (Compares climate to a whole city).
  - Correct: *The climate of Bangalore is cooler than **that of** Delhi.* (Compares singular climate to climate).
  - Plural: *The streets of Tokyo are cleaner than **those of** New York.*

#### Advanced Grammatical Traps for 99+ Percentile Aspirants

1. **The Split Infinitive and Adverb Placement**:
   - Traditional formal grammar prefers placing adverbs before or after the infinitive phrase (*to consider carefully* vs. *to carefully consider*), though modern usage permits split infinitives unless ambiguity results.
2. **Possessive Case with Gerunds**:
   - A noun or pronoun immediately preceding a gerund (verbal noun ending in *-ing*) must take the possessive form:
     - Incorrect: *I objected to him smoking in the conference room.*
     - Correct: *I objected to **his** smoking in the conference room.* (The objection is to the act of smoking, not the person).
3. **Hypothetical Subjunctive vs. Indicative Conditional**:
   - In contrary-to-fact hypothetical statements, *were* is used for all persons:
     - *If he **were** the governor, he would enact immediate reforms.* (Hypothetical, unfulfilled present).
   - In simple factual uncertainty regarding the past, standard indicative past tense applies:
     - *If he **was** present at the meeting, he made no public comment.*

#### CUET UG Practice Questions with Exhaustive Explanations

**Q1**: *In the following question, a sentence is divided into four parts: (A), (B), (C), and (D). Find out which part of the sentence contains a grammatical error. If there is no error, choose (E).*
> (A) Neither the managing director / (B) nor the branch managers / (C) was willing to take responsibility / (D) for the financial discrepancy.
- **Answer**: **(C)**.
- **Explanation**: In a *neither...nor* construction, the verb must agree with the subject closest to it. Here, *the branch managers* is plural and sits immediately adjacent to the verb. Therefore, the singular *was willing* must be corrected to the plural **were willing**.

**Q2**: *Find the part containing an error:*
> (A) One of the main reasons / (B) for his catastrophic downfall / (C) were his inability / (D) to delegate administrative tasks.
- **Answer**: **(C)**.
- **Explanation**: The subject of the sentence is the singular phrase *One of the main reasons*, not the plural noun *reasons* inside the prepositional phrase. The verb must therefore be singular: **was his inability**.

**Q3**: *Find the part containing an error:*
> (A) No sooner had the bell rung / (B) when all the enthusiastic children / (C) rushed out of the classrooms / (D) toward the playground.
- **Answer**: **(B)**.
- **Explanation**: The adverbial phrase *No sooner* is always followed by the correlative conjunction **than**, never *when*. The sentence should read: *No sooner had the bell rung **than** all the enthusiastic children...* (Note: *Hardly* and *Scarcely* take *when*).

**Q4**: *Find the part containing an error:*
> (A) She is one of those rare authors / (B) who has successfully maintained / (C) critical acclaim alongside / (D) commercial popularity.
- **Answer**: **(B)**.
- **Explanation**: The antecedent of the relative pronoun *who* is the plural noun phrase *those rare authors*, not *one*. Consequently, the relative clause requires a plural verb: **who have successfully maintained**.

**Q5**: *Find the part containing an error:*
> (A) Supposing if you / (B) are selected for the scholarship, / (C) will you accept the offer / (D) to study abroad?
- **Answer**: **(A)**.
- **Explanation**: The construction *Supposing if* is a pleonasm (redundancy). Either *Supposing* or *If* should be used individually to introduce the conditional clause, never both concurrently: **Supposing you are selected...** or **If you are selected...**.

#### Tactical 4-Step Checklist for Error Spotting
When approaching any 4-part error spotting question under timed conditions:
1. **Identify the Core Predicate**: Locate the primary finite verb and identify its exact subject. Check number and person.
2. **Inspect Modifiers & Connectors**: Look for introductory participles, correlatives (*neither/nor*, *not only/but also*), and comparative adjectives.
3. **Verify Prepositional Collocations**: Check whether dependent prepositions (*accustomed to*, *comply with*, *abstain from*) are appropriately paired.
4. **Check Pronoun Consistency**: Verify that every pronoun has an unambiguous antecedent and agrees in number, case, and gender.
