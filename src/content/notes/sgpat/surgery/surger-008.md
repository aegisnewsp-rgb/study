# Crystalloids vs. Colloids

> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your Saudi GP Board Surgery exam.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

Pick the fluid by where the water needs to stay.

- **Crystalloids** — electrolyte/small-solute solutions (e.g., NaCl, Ringer's lactate, Plasma-Lyte, dextrose). Low molecular weight (<~30 kDa), so they cross capillary walls freely and split between intravascular and interstitial compartments.
- **Colloids** — large osmotically active molecules (>~30 kDa; albumin 4–5%, HES, gelatin, dextran) that are largely retained inside vessels and pull water in by oncotic pressure.
- **Isotonic crystalloid volume expansion rule**: of each 1 L infused, only ~200–300 mL stays intravascular at 1 h. Colloid retention is closer to 700–1000 mL per litre.
- **First-line for most resuscitation**: isotonic balanced crystalloid (Ringer's lactate or Plasma-Lyte 148). Reserve 5% albumin for SBP, paracentesis >5 L, hepatorenal syndrome, severe burns.
- **Avoid HES** in sepsis and critical illness — renal injury, coagulopathy, increased mortality (EMA 2018 PRAC suspension).

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook — "Big stays, small leaks" (Memory Hook):** Colloids are **BIG** molecules (albumin 66 kDa, HES up to ~1000 kDa) so they **STAY** in the vessel; crystalloids are **SMALL** solutes that **LEAK** into the interstitium. Hence the "1 L in ≈ 250 mL intravascular" rule for isotonic crystalloids.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Volume-of-distribution physiology

The capillary wall behaves as a sieve. Tonicity governs which compartment a fluid shifts into; oncotic pressure (π) governs how much plasma volume is held back. Two equations anchor the topic for the Saudi GP Board Surgery MCQs:

| Equation | Variables and units | Clinical use |
|---|---|---|
| Plasma volume expansion ≈ infused volume × (1 − leak fraction) | mL retained ≈ mL infused × dimensionless fraction | Crystalloid ~0.2–0.3; colloid ~0.7–1.0 |
| Starling: Jv = Lp·S·[(Pc − Pi) − σ(πc − πi)] | Jv mL·min⁻¹; Lp mL·min⁻¹·mmHg⁻¹·cm⁻²; S cm²; P, π mmHg; σ 0–1 | Explains why σ drops in sepsis and why colloids then leak |
| Holliday–Segar 4-2-1 | 4 mL·kg⁻¹·h⁻¹ for first 10 kg + 2 mL·kg⁻¹·h⁻¹ for next 10 kg + 1 mL·kg⁻¹·h⁻¹ above 20 kg | Maintenance fluids (mL per hour) |
| Landis–Pappenheimer: π ≈ 2.1c + 0.16c² + 0.009c³ | c = plasma total protein (g/dL); π in mmHg | Oncotic pressure from albumin + globulin |

#### Comparison matrix: crystalloid vs. colloid

| Feature | Crystalloid (e.g., RL, NS, Plasma-Lyte) | Colloid (albumin, HES, gelatin, dextran) |
|---|---|---|
| Solute size | <~30 kDa, freely filtered | >~30 kDa (albumin 66; HES up to ~1000) |
| Intravascular retention at 1 h | ~20–30% | ~70–100% (intact endothelium) |
| Volume needed to replace 1 L blood loss | 3–4 L | ~1 L |
| Effect on oncotic pressure | Negligible | Raises π directly |
| Major complications | Hyperchloraemic acidosis (NS), interstitial oedema | AKI/coagulopathy (HES), anaphylactoid (gelatin), cost |
| Cost per litre | Low | 20–100× crystalloid |
| First-line status | Yes — most guidelines and SCFHS-aligned practice | Specific indications only |
| Examples in Saudi formularies | NS 0.9%, Ringer's lactate, Plasma-Lyte 148, Dextrose 5% | Human albumin 4–5% and 20%, HES 6% 130/0.4, gelatin 4%, dextran 70 |

#### Standard principles for fluid choice

1. Match fluid to the compartment: intravascular deficit → colloid or isotonic crystalloid; total body water deficit → hypotonic (e.g., D5W or 0.45% saline only when appropriate).
2. Prefer balanced solutions (RL, Plasma-Lyte) over 0.9% saline once volume exceeds ~2 L to avoid hyperchloraemic acidosis and reduced renal cortical perfusion.
3. Capillary leak in sepsis drops σ toward 0 — colloids leak too, negating their oncotic advantage and worsening interstitial oedema.
4. HES is contraindicated in septic, critically ill, or coagulopathic patients (PRAC 2018 EMA suspension still referenced in current SCFHS materials).
5. Albumin 4–5% has a defined role list: spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, large-volume paracentesis >5 L, hepatorenal syndrome, hypoalbuminaemic burns.
6. Maintenance fluids use Holliday–Segar on lean (not actual) body weight to avoid overload in obesity.

#### Worked scenario — septic post-op patient, chloride-loaded

A 62-year-old, 70 kg man post- colectomy receives 4 L of 0.9% saline over 6 h for hypotension. ABG: pH 7.29, HCO₃⁻ 18 mmol/L, Cl⁻ 119 mmol/L, Na⁺ 140 mmol/L, lactate 1.4 mmol/L. Calculate expected intravascular expansion, identify the disturbance, and recommend the next litre.

- **Step 1 — intravascular expansion from 4 L NS**: 4 × 0.25 = 1.0 L retained intravascular; the other 3 L shifts to interstitium.
- **Step 2 — identify disturbance**: non-anion-gap (hyperchloraemic) metabolic acidosis. Anion gap = 140 − (119 + 18) = 3 mmol/L (low/normal). Cause: NS Cl⁻ 154 mmol/L vs plasma 100–110 mmol/L.
- **Step 3 — next litre**: switch to Ringer's lactate (Cl⁻ 109, lactate 28 mmol/L) or Plasma-Lyte 148 (Cl⁻ 98 mmol/L). Lactate is metabolised to bicarbonate by the liver, helping correct acidosis.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap**: Students often attribute the low pH to "lactic acidosis" because lactate is in the fluid name. The actual lactate here is 1.4 — normal. The cause is the chloride load, not lactate.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Edge cases and advanced traps

| Scenario | What changes | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Septic shock with capillary leak | σ → 0; Starling force collapses | Colloid leaks → interstitial oedema, worse oxygen diffusion |
| Major burns >24 h old | Massive capillary leak then partial restoration | Crystalloid (Parkland) in first 24 h; add albumin 4–5% after 12–24 h if π < 15 mmHg |
| Traumatic brain injury | SAFE subgroup: albumin 4% raised mortality vs saline | Use isotonic crystalloid, avoid albumin 4% |
| Hepatorenal syndrome type 1 | Splanchnic vasodilation + low oncotic pressure | Albumin 20% plus terlipressin |
| Paediatric maintenance | Higher body-water turnover | 4-2-1 rule, D5% ½NS or D5% NS after first month |
| Obstetric haemorrhage | Coagulopathy risk | Balanced crystalloid 1:1 with blood until products arrive; avoid HES |

#### Micro-example: when the Starling equation actually decides

A 25-year-old polytrauma patient (80 kg) receives 1.5 L of HES 6% 130/0.4 within the first hour. σ has fallen to ~0.4 because of systemic inflammation.

- Effective intravascular retention = 1.5 L × 0.7 × σ ≈ 0.42 L; the remaining ~1.08 L distributes interstitially.
- Compare with 1.5 L RL: intravascular ≈ 1.5 × 0.25 = 0.375 L.
- The colloid advantage collapses once σ < 0.5 — a fact that explains why large HES volumes fail to reduce total fluid requirement in the CRISTAL reanalysis for septic subgroups.
- Renal exposure to HES also climbs; in the CHEST trial (N = 7000), HES increased renal-replacement therapy without 28-day mortality benefit.

#### Common mistakes to flag in the OSCE

1. Dosing HES by ideal rather than actual weight in obese patients — same renal risk per kg, but exposure is higher.
2. Choosing albumin 5% for "low albumin" alone — hypoalbuminaemia is a marker, not an indication, unless the patient is in SBP or post-paracentesis.
3. Adding 20% albumin to a non-volume-depleted patient — oncotic load without hypovolaemia pulls water intravascularly and risks hypervolaemia, pulmonary oedema.
4. Mixing colloids and crystalloids in the same liter calculation — they obey different retention coefficients; sum them separately.
5. Using D5W for resuscitation — hypotonic in vivo; free water shifts intracellularly, not intravascularly.
