# Exponentials and Logarithms

Paper 1 is 20 multiple-choice questions in 75 minutes with no calculator allowed, and the MM5 family in the official TMUA content specification is "Exponentials and logarithms". It is one of the smallest families on the list and one of the highest-yield, because a handful of ideas generate a large fraction of the questions you will meet.

The family is really two ideas wearing one label. Exponential growth is the phenomenon; logarithms are the tool that lets you answer questions about it when the unknown is in the exponent.

## Exponential growth is proportional, not additive

Something grows exponentially when its rate of change is proportional to its current value, not to a fixed constant. That single distinction explains most of the item traps.

If a population doubles every 20 minutes, the amount added in the next 20 minutes depends on how large the population was at the start of that period, so the increments double too. An item that offers "adds 1,000 a day forever" as the alternative is describing linear growth, and it is wrong for the same reason that interest is not a flat fee.

Write the model as $y = a e^{kx}$ or $y = a(1 + r)^t$ and be deliberate about which. The continuous form is natural for growth and decay described by a rate per unit time; the discrete form is natural for anything that happens in whole steps, like monthly compounding or a population measured each generation. Both are defensible; mixing them is not.

## Taking logs to release the exponent

Most difficulty in this family is algebraic, not conceptual. Given $2^{3x} = 5$, there is no algebraic route to $x$ — you take logs of both sides, which turns $3x \log 2 = \log 5$ and gives you $x$. That single move unlocks almost every item where the unknown sits in an exponent.

The log laws are what make it work. $x \log a + y \log b = \log(a^x b^y)$, so any product becomes a sum and any quotient becomes a difference. The most useful consequence is the change-of-base identity $\log_a b = \dfrac{\log b}{\log a}$, which turns any base into 10 or into $e$ — and without a calculator you will want natural logs for most of this paper anyway, because $e^1 = 2.718$ and $e^2 \approx 7.39$ are worth knowing rather than computing.

## Half-life and doubling time are the same problem

Read the question as one of two kinds. Given a rate constant, find the time to double or halve; or given a doubling time, find the rate constant. Both become trivial once you take logs, and both are why the item exists.

For halving, set $y = a/2$ and solve. For doubling, set $y = 2a$. For a compound-interest question where you need the time, take logs of $(1 + r)^t = 2$ to get $t \log(1 + r) = \log 2$. Note that the doubling time depends on $r$ and on the compounding frequency in a way that surprises people — quarterly compounding reaches the same total faster than annual compounding at the same nominal rate.

## Solving when the unknown is on both sides

$3^x + 2 \cdot 3^{x+1} = 45$ looks impossible and is not. Factor the left side into $3^x(1 + 2\cdot 3)$, which is $9 \cdot 3^x$, so $3^x = 5$ and you are done. The habit to build is to look for a common exponential factor before you reach for logs — reaching for logs first will make you wait a long time for an answer that needs none.

The same trick handles $2^{x+3} = 8 \cdot 2^{x-1}$: divide through, $2^4 = 8$, and the equation is an identity, which is itself sometimes the answer.

## Common mistakes

Adding growth rates instead of compounding them. 10 per cent then 10 per cent is 21 per cent, not 20.

Forgetting that $e^{kx}$ is not $e^k x$. The $x$ belongs in the exponent and nowhere else.

Using $\log x$ when you mean $\ln x$. They differ by a factor of $\log_{10} e \approx 0.434$, so an answer built on the wrong one is off by a factor of more than two. TMUA works in radians and natural logarithms by default.

Losing solutions when you take logs. Logarithms are only defined for positive arguments, so a step that silently assumes $x > 0$ needs a check afterwards.

Mixing continuous and discrete models in one question. Decide which one the question is using before you start, from whether the compounding happens in whole periods.

Dividing by an exponential and losing the exponent shift. $\dfrac{e^{x+4}}{e^{x-1}} = e^5$, and the answer is a constant — a result that should make you check your work, because an $x$-free answer usually means the terms really were like for like.
