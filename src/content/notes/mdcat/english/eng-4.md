# Idioms and Phrases

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

An **idiom** is a fixed expression whose meaning cannot be derived from its individual words (e.g. *kick the bucket* = to die). A **phrase** is a meaningful word-group whose sense is closer to literal, though still a single unit. In MDCAT English, idioms are tested as **multiple-choice meaning questions**: a sentence shows an underlined idiom, and you pick the option closest in meaning.

- **Core skill:** read the surrounding context (subject, verb tense, adjective cues) before choosing a meaning.
- **Substitution trick:** mentally replace the idiom with a single synonym; if the sentence still reads logically, your answer works.
- **Watch for:** negative idioms disguised as positive (e.g. *a blessing in disguise* is *good*, despite the word "blessing").

| Idiom | Closest meaning |
| --- | --- |
| A piece of cake | Very easy |
| Spill the beans | Reveal a secret |
| Hit the nail on the head | State something exactly right |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Definition and Scope

In MDCAT English, *idioms and phrases* refers specifically to **non-compositional fixed expressions** — word groups whose overall meaning diverges from the sum of their parts. A *phrase* in the same question bank usually retains more literal colour (e.g. *in conclusion*, *on the other hand*). The Pakistani MDCAT syllabus, published by PMC and aligned with UGC framing, treats idioms as a discrete vocabulary sub-section worth roughly 3% of paper marks.

#### How MDCAT Frames the Questions

Each item presents a short sentence with the idiom underlined, followed by four options. Only one option carries a meaning that fits the sentence's context. Two distractors are common: one is a **literal restatement** (e.g. "a piece of cake" → "a slice of cake"), and the other is a **near-synonym that does not fit the grammar or register** of the sentence.

#### Strategy

- Read the full sentence, not just the idiom, to determine connotation.
- Eliminate the literal-comprehension option first.
- Confirm by substitution: swap in the proposed meaning and check coherence.

| Trap type | Example trap | Correct response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Literal reading | "Bite the bullet" → bite a piece of metal | Accept pain bravely |
| Wrong register | "Cut to the chase" → argue slowly | Get to the point |
| False synonym | "Once in a blue moon" → rarely (correct) | Not "never" |

- Memorise **150–200 high-frequency idioms** grouped under themes: body parts (*cold feet*, *lump in throat*), colours (*green with envy*), animals (*let the cat out of the bag*).
- Practise **context-based selection**, not memorised lists alone, because MDCAT items vary the surrounding sentence every year.
- Note **negative vs positive polarity** before marking an answer.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Idiom Families

MDCAT occasionally mixes idioms with **proverbs** (e.g. *a stitch in time saves nine*) and **phrasal verbs** (e.g. *give up*, *run into*). A proverb states a general truth with moral weight; a phrasal verb keeps a transparent verb-particle logic. Idioms sit between them: fixed, figurative, but not proverb-like. Recognising the family prevents misclassification.

#### Common Mistakes Candidates Lose Marks On

1. Picking the literal option because the idiom "looks obvious" — e.g. reading *spill the beans* as scattering vegetables.
2. Confusing *spill the beans* (reveal a secret) with *let the cat out of the bag* (also reveal a secret). Both work, but only one matches the in-text syntax.
3. Ignoring **tense agreement**: *hit the books* means "study intensely," but if the sentence uses past tense and the option uses a present-tense synonym, both still fit; the test is meaning, not grammar.
4. Choosing a meaning with the wrong connotation — e.g. treating *a blessing in disguise* as misfortune because of the word "disguise."

#### Adjacent Topics to Revise

- **One-word substitutions** (often overlap with idiom meanings, e.g. *a piece of cake* ↔ *effortless*).
- **Prepositions** and **phrasal verbs**, since phrasal verbs test similar pattern-recognition.

#### Practice Prompts

1. Sentence: *"He bit the bullet and apologised first."* Which meaning fits? — Answer: *Accepted an unpleasant situation bravely.*
2. Sentence: *"Her remarks were a blessing in disguise for the team."* Choose the closest option. — Answer: *An apparently negative event with hidden benefit.*
