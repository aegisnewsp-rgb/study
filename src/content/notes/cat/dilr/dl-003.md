# Data Interpretation Graphs

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Scan a graph in under 60 seconds by anchoring on three things: the y-axis units, the legend order, and the steepest segment.

**Data Interpretation – Graphs** means extracting numbers from line, bar, pie, stacked and combination visuals and turning them into percentage, ratio, average and growth answers. CAT tests the arithmetic, not the chart-reading.

- **Line Graph**: plots one series across an ordered axis (usually time). Read by tracing vertical lines down to the x-axis labels.
- **Bar Chart (Simple, Stacked, Clustered)**: bars compare categories. Stacked bars sum sub-parts; clustered bars compare groups side by side.
- **Pie Chart**: each slice's angle is (Category / Total) × 360°.
- **Histogram / Frequency Polygon**: continuous class intervals on the x-axis, frequency on the y-axis.
- **Cumulative Frequency Curve (Ogive)**: y values keep adding up — never read a single bar height in isolation.

Memory Hook — **"YALS": Y-axis → Averages → Legend → Steepest segment.** Run YALS in that order every time a chart opens, and you will catch mis-scaled axes, misread bars, and hidden crossovers before the timer punishes you.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> The hardest trap in DI is not the arithmetic; it is believing the picture the chart draws.

#### Reading the Picture Before the Numbers

Most wrong answers in CAT DI come from a misread, not a miscalculation. Check four things in order: (1) the y-axis starts at zero or has a break, (2) the x-axis is ordered time or unordered categories, (3) the legend maps colour to series, and (4) the question asks for the value at a tick or a value between ticks. Between-tick reads use linear interpolation; do not eyeball them.

#### Mechanics of the Common Computations

Every DI-graph question reduces to one of five operations: percentage change, ratio comparison, weighted average, CAGR/growth rate, or share-of-total. The table below gives the formula, the variable definitions, and the unit. Remember that all five are dimensionless or carry a single % sign — units like rupees or units sold live in the raw read, not the answer itself.

| Computation | Formula | Variables & Units |
|---|---|---|
| Percentage Change | ((New − Old) / Old) × 100 | New, Old — same unit as the axis (₹, units, tonnes) |
| Average Growth over n years | ((Final / Initial)^(1/n) − 1) × 100 | Final, Initial — axis unit; n — years |
| Weighted Average | Σ(wᵢ × xᵢ) / Σ(wᵢ) | xᵢ — value, wᵢ — weight, output shares xᵢ's unit |
| Pie Slice Angle | (Part / Whole) × 360° | Part, Whole — same unit; output in degrees |
| Index Number | (Current / Base) × 100 | Current, Base — axis unit; output is index points |

#### Translating Between Chart Types

A single CAT set often hides the same data in two forms: a line graph for one series, a stacked bar for another, plus a pie for shares. The fastest move is to convert each chart into a row in a mental matrix (year on the left, series across the top), then do all arithmetic on the matrix. Skipping this step is the single biggest time-loss in DI-graph sets.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Confusion | Correct Reading |
|---|---|
| Percentage point vs % change | A move from 20% to 25% is **5 percentage points** but a **25% relative change**. |
| Ogive vs Bar | Ogive y-values are **running totals**; reading a single point gives cumulative, not class-wise, frequency. |
| Stacked bar total vs segment | The whole bar height is the **sum** of all stacks; each segment height is its share only. |
| CAGR vs arithmetic mean | CAGR compounds; arithmetic mean of yearly %s ignores compounding. Use CAGR for multi-year growth. |
| Data Sufficiency "either alone" | Each statement must **independently** yield the answer; otherwise the answer is option (3). |

#### A Worked CAT-Style Set

Consider three products A, B, C with revenues (₹ crore) for 2019–2023. A: 200, 240, 288, 346, 415. B: 150, 165, 182, 200, 220. C: 100, 130, 156, 195, 234. Question: which product has the highest CAGR over 2019→2023?

Step 1 — pick the formula. CAGR = ((V_f / V_i)^(1/4) − 1) × 100, where V_f is the 2023 value (axis unit ₹ crore) and V_i is the 2019 value (same unit). The exponent 1/4 = 0.25 reflects four compounding periods (years).

Step 2 — compute ratios.
- A: 415 / 200 = 2.075 → 2.075^0.25 ≈ 1.200 → CAGR ≈ 20.0% per year.
- B: 220 / 150 ≈ 1.4667 → 1.4667^0.25 ≈ 1.1006 → CAGR ≈ 10.06% per year.
- C: 234 / 100 = 2.34 → 2.34^0.25 ≈ 1.236 → CAGR ≈ 23.6% per year.

Step 3 — rank. C > A > B. Product C wins despite a smaller absolute rupee gain (₹134 cr vs A's ₹215 cr), because the base was lower.

Examiner Trap — students compare absolute increases (₹215 cr for A vs ₹134 cr for C) and pick A. CAGR rewards percentage growth, not rupee growth; a small base compounded at a high rate beats a large base compounded at a low rate over the same window.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Beyond raw extraction, the CAT reward sits in how fast you fold a chart into a working equation.

#### Weight Shifts and Sub-Category Drill-Downs

Stacked bars and treemaps carry one extra layer: each bar is a total, but the question may ask about a sub-segment whose weight changes year on year. The trap is to average the percentages across years — that gives a misleading share. Compute segment-value / total-value for each year first, then compare.

| Edge case | What goes wrong | Right move |
|---|---|---|
| Broken y-axis from 0 to 50, then 50+ | Differences look 10× larger than reality | Rescale mentally before estimating |
| Index numbers with shifting base year | Comparing indices on different bases | Convert each to a common base first |
| Heatmap with dense shading | Hard to read individual cells | Read row-wise, ignoring exact shade |
| Combination chart with dual y-axes | Reading the wrong scale for each series | Tag each series to its axis before plotting |
| Approximation under timer | Rounding 16.67% to 16% compounds | Memorise: 1/6 ≈ 16.67%, 1/7 ≈ 14.29%, 1/8 = 12.5%, 1/9 ≈ 11.11% |

#### Boundary Conditions and Asymptotic Behaviour

Two mathematical limits matter for DI graphs. First, when the initial value V_i approaches zero, CAGR explodes toward infinity even for tiny absolute changes — so whenever the chart starts near zero, check whether CAGR is even the right metric. Second, when n grows large, the geometric-mean growth rate converges to the arithmetic mean only when each year's growth is identically small (under ~5%); for volatile yearly swings, arithmetic-mean % is meaningless.

#### Connection to Adjacent Topics

DI-Graphs feeds directly into **Caselet DI** (text-as-data + chart), **Data Sufficiency** (logical sufficiency rather than computation), and **Logical Reasoning puzzles with tables** (the seating/arrangement grid is structurally identical to a bar-chart matrix). Mastering the matrix-from-chart translation transfers cleanly to all three.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. A multi-line graph shows revenue and headcount for five companies across five years. The total headcount of the smallest company in 2023 is what percentage of the total revenue of all five in 2023? Convert to a matrix, then do the computation in one pass.
2. A pie chart with five slices for 2023 sits next to a stacked bar showing 2019–2023 for each slice. A Data Sufficiency question asks: was 2023 the peak year for Slice S? Evaluate each statement for independent sufficiency before reading the chart.
