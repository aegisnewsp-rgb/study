# CA24: Auditing and Assurance

CA24 is one of the six Intermediate Level papers of the KASNEB CPA qualification. KASNEB states that examinations are held three times each year — April, August and December — and that CPA papers run for three hours, with morning sessions beginning at 9:00 a.m. and afternoon sessions at 2:00 p.m.

Auditing is the paper where structure beats volume. Almost every mark in it comes from knowing what a step is for and what would make it fail, rather than from memorising standard wording.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> The paper has one spine — risk is identified, assessed, responded to and concluded on — and the six assertions hang off it. Learn the assertions cold; they are a lookup table, not a definition to memorise.

| Assertion | The question behind it | A scenario that puts it at risk |
|---|---|---|
| Existence | Does the asset exist at all? | Stock counted in excess of what is held |
| Completeness | Is everything that should be recorded, recorded? | A liability the entity never books |
| Valuation | Is it carried at the right amount? | An estimate built on stale inputs |
| Rights and obligations | Can the entity dispose of it as it wishes? | An asset pledged to someone else |
| Cut-off | Does it belong to this period? | A transaction posted late at the year end |
| Presentation and disclosure | Is it properly described? | A disclosure omitted from the notes |

**What this paper rewards instead of volume.** A mark here is earned by explaining why a procedure would or would not discharge an assertion, not by reproducing the wording of a standard. That is why the same student who can recite a definition struggles when a scenario turns the usual facts around. KASNEB sits CPA papers three times a year — April, August and December — for three hours each, with morning sessions from 9:00 a.m. and afternoon sessions from 2:00 p.m., so there is room in the paper for the scenario items that test that reasoning rather than recall.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

> The working picture — what each core concept decides, and the order the concepts have to appear in.

| Concept | The distinction that carries the marks | Where the item lives |
|---|---|---|
| Sufficient against appropriate evidence | Quantity against quality | Choosing how much, then choosing what |
| Reliability against relevance | External and directly obtained evidence ranks higher, but relevance still decides whether it proves the assertion | Picking between two procedures on offer |
| Materiality | A quantitative benchmark with a qualitative override | A small item that changes a loss, a covenant or a related party |
| Controls | Preventive or detective, manual or automated, and never effective when one person both benefits from and performs the error | Whether reliance reduces substantive work |
| Opinion type | Modified because a misstatement is material, qualified or disclaimed because pervasiveness decides which | The wording of the report itself |

**Answer in the order the audit runs.** A scenario item is usually marked against a chain, not a list. Name the assertion, let it set the risk level, let the level dictate the response, and the procedure follows from what you have already written down. Candidates who reverse that order can name every relevant fact and still lose the mark, because nothing on the page then shows the examiner that the facts were connected rather than recalled.

### The audit is a risk-driven argument

The chain that runs through the whole paper is: identify risk, assess risk, respond to risk, conclude. If a question gives you a scenario, that chain is the answer.

Risk assessment is at the assertion level, and you should know the assertions cold because they map onto the procedures. Existence — does the asset exist? Completeness — is everything recorded? Valuation — is it at the right amount? Rights and obligations — does the entity own it and owe it? Cut-off — is it in the right period? Presentation and disclosure — is it properly described?

An item that gives you "the client did not count inventory at year end" and asks which assertion is at risk is testing this mapping directly. Excessive inventory means existence; an unrecorded liability means completeness.

### Evidence, materiality and sampling

Audit evidence must be sufficient and appropriate. Sufficient is a quantity judgement — more when the risk is higher. Appropriate is a quality judgement — the evidence must be relevant and reliable, and reliability depends on its source and form.

External evidence is more reliable than internal, and evidence obtained directly by the auditor is more reliable than evidence obtained orally or through the client. But relevance still governs: a document that is highly reliable and proves nothing about the assertion is worth very little.

Materiality has both a quantitative benchmark and a qualitative dimension. An item below materiality can still be material if it changes a loss, affects a covenant, or concerns a related party. Items that are qualitatively material are the ones auditors miss most.

### Internal controls

A control is effective only if it prevents or detects misstatement promptly and at a cost reasonable relative to the benefit. Controls can be preventive or detective, manual or automated, and a control operated by the same person who benefits from the error is rarely a control at all.

Where controls are strong, the auditor may rely on them and reduce substantive testing. Where they are weak, substantive work rises and control testing is largely wasted.

### The auditor's report

Unmodified versus modified is the core distinction, and it turns on materiality and pervasiveness. A misstatement is material but not pervasive and you can qualify; a misstatement is both material and pervasive and you disclaim.

Going concern is separate from the opinion and requires its own treatment. Scope limitation caused by the client versus by the circumstances produces different conclusions, and that distinction is examined repeatedly because candidates flatten it.

The report also has to be independent of the entity — management prepares the statements, the auditor opines on them — and this separation is examined in its own right, particularly on ethical questions such as fee dependence and non-audit services.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (3mo+)

> What separates a candidate who can define every concept on this paper from one who can keep several of them in play at the same time.

#### Reliability ranks evidence; it does not excuse it
External evidence outranks internal, and evidence the auditor obtains directly outranks evidence relayed through the client — but that ranking answers a question about trustworthiness, not about whether the item helps. A highly reliable document bearing on no assertion under test is worth close to nothing, which is why a procedure chosen purely for the strength of its source can still be the wrong procedure. The exam tests the two judgements separately, and a candidate applying the reliability hierarchy mechanically tends to defend the wrong answer convincingly.

#### Qualitative materiality is where most marks are lost
An item below the quantitative benchmark can still be material because it changes a loss, trips a covenant or involves a related party. None of those tests is available from the size of the figure, so a candidate working only from a threshold clears the numerically significant items and quietly misses the ones that decide the opinion. Auditors are said to miss qualitatively material items for a structural reason rather than a careless one: the threshold is what the eye goes to first.

#### Independence is examined in its own right
Management owns the statements and the auditor's job is to opine on somebody else's work, and the report has to hold that separation independently of the opinion it expresses. Fee dependence and non-audit services are weighed as threats to that independence rather than as an afterthought to the technical work, which is why an item can turn on ethics while the surrounding scenario is entirely about evidence. Candidates who treat independence as a separate chapter rather than a condition of every conclusion lose the recurring cluster of marks sitting on it.

| Decision in the report | What turns it | The trap |
|---|---|---|
| Unmodified against modified | Whether a misstatement is material at all | Assuming a qualified opinion means the figures are false |
| Qualification against disclaimer | Material but not pervasive against material and pervasive | Deciding on size alone and skipping pervasiveness |
| Scope limitation | Caused by the client, or by the circumstances themselves | Flattening the two into one conclusion |
| Going concern | Requires its own treatment, separate from the opinion | Folding it into the modified-opinion question |

### Common mistakes

Conflating materiality with significance. An item can be numerically small and qualitatively decisive.

Asserting that stronger controls always reduce substantive testing. They reduce it only if the controls are operating effectively and are relevant to the assertion.

Assuming a qualified opinion means the accounts are false. It means the auditor cannot obtain sufficient appropriate evidence, or disagrees with a material figure.

Missing the pervasiveness test when deciding between qualification and disclaimer.

Ignoring ethical independence. Fees and non-audit services are assessed as threats, and a candidate who never considers them loses a recurring cluster of marks.
