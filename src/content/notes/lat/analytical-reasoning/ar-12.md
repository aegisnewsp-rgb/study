# Puzzles and Ranking

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Puzzles and Ranking in LAT Analytical Reasoning tests your ability to order persons, objects, or events using logic-grid constraints — linear rows, circular tables, floors, ranks, and dates — and then extract absolute or relative positions from those constraints. LAT past papers (HEC, 2018–2023) consistently carry 4–6 such questions per 20-MCQ section, making this the single highest-scoring cluster inside the 4%-weighted Analytical Reasoning block.

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Linear Arrangement | Persons/items placed in a straight row with fixed left–right order |
| Circular Arrangement | Persons sit around a table; facing centre or facing outside flips left/right |
| Ranking Comparison | Order of merit (taller, older, rank 1st, rank kth) by relative height or merit |
| Conditional Clue | If–then statement that activates only when the condition triggers |
| Constraint Matrix | Rows = persons, columns = attributes; ticks/crosses encode possibilities |

Key formulas to memorise: position-from-end equals `N + 1 − position-from-top`; persons between A and B in a row equals `|(posA − posB)| − 1`; opposite seats in a circle occur at clockwise distance `n/2`.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"FIX → MAP → CHAIN"** — Fix one variable to break symmetry, MAP attributes on a grid, then CHAIN transitive links (A>B, B>C ⇒ A>C). Works for every LAT arrangement puzzle.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Mechanics of Ordering Puzzles

Every puzzle reduces to a finite set of variables (persons, professions, floors, days) bound by three clue types: **definite** (A sits at position 3), **comparative** (A is taller than B), and **conditional** (if A sits at an end, then B sits next to C). Solve by listing all clues, classifying them, and converting each into a tick, cross, or position marker on a constraint matrix.

The **transitive property** drives ranking chains: if P > Q and Q > R, then P > R. Stopping at two-link chains is the single most common reason students lose 1–2 marks per LAT paper. For circular arrangements, always fix one person first (anchor technique) because rotating the entire circle by any of the n positions produces the same arrangement — distinct seatings equal (n−1)!.

#### Ranking Formulas and Position Mathematics

| Quantity | Formula | Variables & Units |
|----------|---------|------------------|
| Position from opposite end | `x = N + 1 − y` | x, y = positions (unitless ranks); N = total persons (count) |
| Persons between two in a row | `|posA − posB| − 1` | pos = absolute position from same end (unitless) |
| Clockwise distance to opposite seat | `n / 2` | n = number of seats (must be even for opposites) |
| Distinct circular seatings | `(n − 1)!` | n = number of persons (dimensionless permutation count) |
| Rank-sum invariant | `x + y = N + 1` | x = rank from top; y = rank from bottom; N = group size |

The rank-sum invariant is the workhorse for ranking-only questions: if A is 12th from the top and 28th from the bottom in a class, then N = 12 + 28 − 1 = 39 students — no other calculation needed.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix: Easily Confused Setups

| Setup Type | Direction Reference | Common Confusing Element |
|-----------|---------------------|--------------------------|
| Linear row, facing north | Left = west end, right = east end | "Immediate left" means the neighbour physically on the left |
| Circle, facing centre | Left = clockwise neighbour's left | "A's immediate left" = the person on A's left hand = clockwise neighbour |
| Circle, facing outside | Left = anti-clockwise neighbour | Direction reverses compared to centre-facing |
| Building floors (bottom to top) | Floor 1 = ground, floor n = top | "Above B" means higher floor number |
| Day-date scheduling | Week cycle = 7, month cycles ≠ 30 | Use the actual month length, not a flat 30 |

#### Step-by-Step Solving Protocol

1. List every variable (5–7 persons, 4–5 attributes typical for LAT).
2. Tag each clue as definite / comparative / conditional.
3. Place definite clues on the grid first — they anchor the puzzle.
4. Apply transitive chains for any ranking link that shares a variable.
5. Eliminate possibilities using negative clues ("none of A, B, C…").
6. Verify the final arrangement against **every** clue, including unused conditionals.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** Six persons — A, B, C, D, E, F — sit in a row facing north. B sits immediately to the right of A. C sits at the far left end. D sits somewhere between A and F. E is not adjacent to A. How many persons sit between B and F?

#### Solution:
- Place C at position 1 (definite clue).
- Place A at some position p ≥ 2, with B at p + 1 (A and B occupy two consecutive positions, B immediately right of A).
- D sits "between A and F" ⇒ F is on the opposite side of A from D, and one of A, D, F occupies the middle slot of a sub-block.
- Trying A at position 2 → B at 3, then D and F must occupy 4, 5 or 5, 6 with D between A and F. D = 4, F = 5 satisfies "D between A and F" (D lies between positions of A and F on the row).
- E occupies position 6 (only remaining spot); check E-not-adjacent-to-A: A is at 2, E at 6 — not adjacent ✓.
- Final row: C(1), A(2), B(3), D(4), F(5), E(6).
- Persons between B(3) and F(5) = |3 − 5| − 1 = **1 person (D)**.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students frequently read "D sits between A and F" as D being physically between the two people, then count D as one of the between-slots — the correct between-count **excludes both endpoints** (A and F themselves), so the answer is positions strictly between them.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Boundary Conditions

The rank-sum formula `x + y = N + 1` silently assumes the ranker counts the same person twice — once from each end. If a question states "A is 7th from the top **and** 7th from the bottom", N = 13 and A occupies the exact middle; many students miscompute N = 14 because they add without subtracting 1. Another boundary: the formula breaks when the total N is odd and the person occupies the geometric midpoint, because no unique between-pair exists at that point.

In circular arrangements with `n = 6`, two persons are opposite only if their clockwise gap is exactly 3 seats; with `n = 7`, no opposite pairs exist at all — a subtle trap when examiners switch from a 6-person to a 7-person circle mid-problem. Day-date puzzles require the actual month length (28, 29, 30, or 31 days), so always write the calendar before applying modular arithmetic.

#### Advanced Traps and Exceptions

1. **Double negatives** like "none of A, B, C is a doctor" eliminate three cells in one stroke — students often miss the third.
2. **Conditional clauses** ("if A sits at an end") must be evaluated only after the antecedent is forced; treating them as universal statements wastes grid cells.
3. **Facing-direction flip**: in a centre-facing circle, the person on your immediate left sits to your **clockwise** side, not counter-clockwise — reversing this is the most frequent reason for a wrong final arrangement.
4. **Cross-variable mapping**: when two sets (e.g., 5 persons and 5 cities) must be matched one-to-one, build a separate cross-table; do not interleave columns on a single grid.
5. **Hybrid blood-relation + ranking**: statements like "the son of A ranks higher than B" require decoding the family tree first, then placing ranked persons — solve the relation half before touching the rank chain.

#### Strategy for the LAT Paper

Within the 20-MCQ Analytical Reasoning section, allocate ~45 seconds to ranking/comparison MCQs and ~90 seconds to full seating puzzles; skip and return if a conditional branch stalls you. Most LAT top-scorers clear the 50% sectional cutoff by nailing the 4–6 puzzle/ranking questions rather than chasing obscure logic games — train the constraint-matrix reflex through timed practice on HEC past papers (2018, 2019, 2021, 2023 are publicly downloadable).

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. Eight persons form a circle facing the centre. P sits second to the left of Q. R sits opposite P. S is not adjacent to Q. T sits between Q and R. How many distinct seating arrangements satisfy all clues?
2. In a class of 47 students, Karim ranks 19th from the top. What is his rank from the bottom, and how many students rank between him and the exact middle student?
