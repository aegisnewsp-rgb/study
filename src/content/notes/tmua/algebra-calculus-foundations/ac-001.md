# MM1 Algebra and Functions

MM1 is the widest family in Section 1 Part 1 of the official content specification — seven numbered strands under one heading — and it is the one every other family leans on. The strands are: index laws for rational exponents, surds including rationalising the denominator, quadratic functions with the discriminant and completing the square, simultaneous equations solved by substitution, linear and quadratic inequalities, polynomial manipulation with the Factor and Remainder Theorems, and a qualitative treatment of functions as mappings.

There is no formula booklet on this paper, so every one of those results has to arrive from memory. And 75 minutes for 20 questions means algebra you can do without hesitating is time you buy for the families that are harder to recall.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> MM1 never asks you to admire an algebraic technique. It asks you to pick the cheapest one and finish inside the time.

| The specification strand | The move that settles it | The thing that catches people |
|---|---|---|
| MM1.1 indices, all rational exponents | $a^{m/n} = (\sqrt[n]{a})^m$, so take the root first | Reading $27^{2/3}$ as $27^2 \div 3$ |
| MM1.2 surds, rationalising | Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate | Forgetting $(\sqrt2+\sqrt3)(\sqrt2-\sqrt3) = -1$, not $+1$ |
| MM1.3 quadratics | Compute the discriminant before choosing a method | Two algebraic roots, and neither is in the domain |
| MM1.4 simultaneous equations | Substitute the linear relation into the quadratic one | Substituting correctly and then reporting the wrong pair |
| MM1.5 inequalities | Flip the sign only when you multiply or divide by a negative | Leaving the sign alone, or flipping it when you should not |
| MM1.6 polynomials | Test $p(k)$ first; only divide if it is a factor | Long division when a one-line theorem settles it |
| MM1.7 functions | Write the domain down before you evaluate anything | Reading $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ as two branches |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Rational indices: take the root first

$27^{2/3}$ is ambiguous only if you read it left to right. Rewrite it as $(\sqrt[3]{27})^2 = 3^2 = 9$. Negative exponents invert, so $\left(\tfrac{8}{27}\right)^{-1/3} = \left(\sqrt[3]{\tfrac{27}{8}}\right) = \tfrac{3}{2}$. The index laws — add when multiplying, subtract when dividing, multiply when raising to a power — hold for rational exponents exactly as they do for integers, but only once the base is positive. That is why a negative base with a fractional index is either undefined or depends on the root, and why the specification restricts this strand to the case you can evaluate safely.

#### Surds: the conjugate is the whole trick

To rationalise $\dfrac{2+\sqrt3}{2-\sqrt3}$, multiply top and bottom by the conjugate $2+\sqrt3$:

$$\frac{(2+\sqrt3)^2}{(2-\sqrt3)(2+\sqrt3)} = \frac{4 + 4\sqrt3 + 3}{4-3} = 7 + 4\sqrt3$$

The denominator collapses because $(2-\sqrt3)(2+\sqrt3) = 4 - 3 = 1$. Whenever the denominator is a sum or difference of two surds, the conjugate is the object that cancels the root. Watch the sign: with $\sqrt2$ and $\sqrt3$ you get $\sqrt6$, not $-\sqrt6$, because $(\sqrt2+\sqrt3)(\sqrt2-\sqrt3) = 2 - 3$.

#### Quadratics: discriminant first, then choose

For $ax^2+bx+c = 0$, $D = b^2 - 4ac$. $D > 0$ means two distinct real roots, $D = 0$ one repeated root, $D < 0$ no real roots. Computing $D$ is one subtraction and saves you from completing the square only to discover the answer is complex.

Completing the square is the method that also gives you the vertex, which MM8.4 asks for constantly. Take $x^2 - 6x + 7 = 0$. Half of $-6$ is $-3$, so add $9$ to both sides and add $9$ inside:

$$(x-3)^2 = 2 \implies x = 3 \pm \sqrt2$$

Note that $D = 36 - 28 = 8$ and $2\sqrt{D}/(2a) = 2\sqrt8/2 = 2\sqrt2$, so $x = 3 \pm \sqrt2$ agrees. The form $x^2 + px + q = 0$ becomes $(x + \tfrac{p}{2})^2 = \tfrac{p^2}{4} - q$, which is worth writing out once and reusing.

#### Simultaneous equations: substitute the linear one

The specification names the case explicitly — one linear and one quadratic. Take $y = x+1$ and $x^2 + y^2 = 25$:

$$x^2 + (x+1)^2 = 25 \implies 2x^2 + 2x + 1 = 25 \implies x^2 + x - 12 = 0 \implies (x+4)(x-3) = 0$$

So $x = -4$ or $x = 3$, and because $y$ was defined by the linear relation, $y = -3$ or $y = 4$. The solutions are the points $(-4,-3)$ and $(3,4)$, and both sit on the circle $x^2+y^2=25$ because $16+9 = 25$ and $9+16=25$. Always write the pairs back into the original equations: it costs four seconds and catches an arithmetic slip immediately.

#### Inequalities: the sign is the whole question

Solve $x^2 - 5x + 6 < 0$. Factorise to $(x-2)(x-3) < 0$. The upward-opening parabola $y = x^2-5x+6$ has $D = 25 - 24 = 1 > 0$ and roots at 2 and 3, and it is negative between them. So $2 < x < 3$. For linear inequalities the rule is simpler but more frequently broken: multiplying or dividing by a negative reverses the direction, so $3 - 2x > 7$ gives $-2x > 4$ and then $x < -2$, not $x > -2$.

#### Polynomials: test, then divide

**Factor Theorem.** $(x-k)$ is a factor of $p(x)$ if and only if $p(k) = 0$. For $p(x) = x^2 - 5x + 6$, $p(2) = 4 - 10 + 6 = 0$, so $(x-2)$ is a factor, and dividing gives $x - 3$.

**Remainder Theorem.** Dividing $p(x)$ by $(x-k)$ leaves remainder $p(k)$. For $p(x) = x^3 - 2x + 5$ and $k=2$: $p(2) = 8 - 4 + 5 = 9$. Confirm it — $(x-2)(x^2+2x+2) + 9$ expands to exactly $x^3 - 2x + 5$.

**Division by a quadratic.** For $x^3 + 3x^2 - 4x - 12$ divided by $(x+3)$: first test $p(-3) = -27 + 27 + 12 - 12 = 0$, so $x+3$ is a factor. Then $x^2$ gives $x^3+3x^2$ with remainder $-4x-12$, and $-4$ finishes it. Quotient $x^2 - 4$, remainder $0$.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (3mo+)

#### A function is a mapping, and that changes the algebra

MM1.7 asks for a qualitative understanding: a function is many-to-one, or sometimes one-to-one, and you should be comfortable with the properties of common ones. This sounds soft until you notice it explains an error that costs marks repeatedly. $f(x) = x^2$ is many-to-one over the reals — $f(2) = f(-2) = 4$ — so the horizontal-line test on its graph crosses twice. Restrict the domain to $x \ge 0$ and the same formula becomes one-to-one.

By contrast $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ is one-to-one, and the specification singles it out for a reason: $\sqrt{x}$ always means the positive square root. $\sqrt{9} = 3$. The $\pm$ arrives only when you invert it, because the equation $x^2 = 9$ has the two solutions $x = 3$ and $x = -3$. Conflating the two is one of the most reliable ways to lose a mark, and it explains an entire class of error in MM3 and MM4.

#### The domain is part of the answer

Writing the domain before you evaluate prevents both the surd trap and the logarithm trap. For $f(x) = \sqrt{5-x}$ the radicand must be non-negative, so $5-x \ge 0$ and the domain is $x \le 5$; $f(8)$ does not exist. For $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x-3}$ you need $x \ne 3$. MM8.1 asks you to sketch square roots and the modulus function, and both are defined by their domains rather than by their formulas.

#### Where the routine runs out

Three situations break the standard methods. An equation where you divide by an expression in $x$ can lose its own solutions at the point of division — if you divide $x^3 - 6x^2 + 5x$ by $x$, the root $x = 0$ disappears and you must add it back. Simultaneous equations can produce more candidate pairs than the geometry allows, and the domain check from the paragraph above removes them. And a quadratic inequality has three possible shapes on the number line, decided entirely by the sign of $a$ and the value of the discriminant; candidates who always draw the upward parabola get two of the three cases wrong.

#### Common mistakes

Forgetting to reverse the inequality when dividing by a negative number.

Reading $\sqrt{9}$ as $\pm3$, or writing $x = \pm\sqrt9$ when the question gave $x = \sqrt9$.

Losing $x=0$ (or any value where you divided) when solving by division.

Treating the Remainder Theorem as giving you the quotient.

Multiplying out $(2-\sqrt3)$ by $(2+\sqrt3)$ and getting $+3$ instead of $-3$ in the denominator.

Reporting only one root of a quadratic without checking whether the second is admissible.

Assuming a function is one-to-one because its formula looks tidy — $x^2$ is the standing counterexample.
