# Simple Chemical Changes

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **simple chemical change** is a process where one or more new substances (with new chemical properties) form from the original substances, and the change is in most keys **irreversible** by simple physical means.

- Atoms in the **reactants** rearrange to form **products** with different properties.
- Five reliable **evidences** of a chemical change: colour change, gas evolution (effervescence), precipitate formation, temperature change, and a new smell.
- **Mass is conserved** (Lavoisier's Law): mass of reactants = mass of products.
- Everyday examples: **rusting** of iron, **burning** of paper, **cooking** raw egg, **neutralisation** of acid with base.

| Evidence | What you observe |
| --- | --- |
| Colour change | Iron turns reddish-brown when it rusts |
| Gas evolution | Bubbles escape when vinegar meets baking soda |
| Temperature change | Burning wood feels hot; photosynthesis absorbs heat |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Chemical vs Physical Change

A **physical change** only alters the state, size, or shape of a substance — the original substance can in most keys be recovered. Melting ice, dissolving sugar in water, and tearing paper are physical changes. A **chemical change** produces a new substance with new properties, and most cannot be reversed easily by physical means.

#### Evidences of a Chemical Change

When a new substance forms, at least one of the following is in most keys noticed:

- Colour change (e.g., cut apple turning brown)
- Gas evolved with or without effervescence (e.g., HCl on zinc gives hydrogen)
- Precipitate formed when two solutions mix (e.g., KI + Pb(NO₃)₂ → yellow PbI₂)
- Temperature change — gets hot (exothermic) or cold (endothermic)
- Change in smell or taste that was not present before

#### Common Types at NCEE Level

- **Combustion (burning):** C + O₂ → CO₂; 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (bright white light).
- **Rusting (slow oxidation):** Fe + O₂ + H₂O → hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust).
- **Neutralisation:** NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O (acid + base → salt + water).
- **Synthesis:** Fe + S → FeS (heat the mixture to form iron(II) sulphide).

#### Conservation of Mass

Lavoisier showed that mass is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. If 4 g of iron reacts fully with 2 g of sulphur, exactly 6 g of iron(II) sulphide is formed.

| Change | Reversible? | New substance? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Melting ice | Yes (freeze it back) | No |
| Dissolving salt in water | Yes (evaporate) | No |
| Burning magnesium | No | Yes (MgO) |
| Rusting iron nail | No | Yes (iron oxide) |

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Fast vs Slow Chemical Changes

Some reactions release energy quickly as heat and light (a match burning, magnesium ribbon); others release (or absorb) energy slowly over hours or days (rusting of iron, ripening of bananas, souring of milk). The chemical principles are the same — only the **rate** differs.

#### Easy Traps in NCEE Questions

1. A substance changing colour does **not** always mean a chemical change; coloured paper tearing is still physical.
2. Rusting needs **both air (oxygen) and water** — dry iron or oil-coated iron hardly rusts.
3. When a candle burns, the wax seems to "disappear", but the mass is conserved in the gases (CO₂ and water vapour) released into the air.
4. Dissolving sugar or salt in water is **physical**, not chemical, because you can recover the solute by evaporation.

#### Worked Micro-Example

A student burns 2.4 g of magnesium ribbon in air and gets 4.0 g of white ash (MgO). The oxygen that joined the magnesium came from the air and had a mass of 4.0 − 2.4 = 1.6 g, confirming Lavoisier's conservation of mass: 2.4 g Mg + 1.6 g O₂ = 4.0 g MgO.

#### Practice Prompts

1. State three observations that prove a chemical change has occurred, and give one everyday example of each.
2. A nail weighing 7.0 g is left outdoors and later weighs 7.4 g. Using conservation of mass, explain where the extra mass came from.

| Reaction type | General pattern | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Combustion | Fuel + O₂ → oxide + heat | CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O |
| Neutralisation | Acid + Base → Salt + Water | H₂SO₄ + 2KOH → K₂SO₄ + 2H₂O |
| Rusting | Fe + O₂ + H₂O → Fe₂O₃·xH₂O | Iron gate left in rain |
