# Statement and Assumptions

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

An **assumption** is an unstated premise that the speaker treats as true for a statement to hold. If the assumption is false, the statement falls apart. In LAT Analytical Reasoning, the task is to pick the assumption that is **necessarily** taken for granted — not the one that is merely possible or desirable.

- A **statement** is a given fact, opinion, or proposal.
- An **assumption** is the hidden belief that makes the statement reasonable.
- A **conclusion** is drawn *from* an assumption; the assumption supports the conclusion.
- A **course of action** prescribes what *should be done* — never an assumption.

**Trigger words** that signal an assumption: *should, must, ought to, let us, assuming that, if…then.* Whenever these appear, expect an unstated value judgment underneath. Apply the **negation test**: if assuming the opposite makes the statement invalid or absurd, the original is a necessary assumption.

#### High-yield LAT pointers:
- Expect 1–3 MCQs per LAT paper, in most keys with 3–5 options.
- Eliminate options that restate the statement, propose an action, or only *can* be true.
- Choose the option that *must* be true for the statement to make sense.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Defining the components

A **statement** is the surface claim — "The college should ban phones in classrooms." The **assumption** is the unstated belief without which the ban makes no sense — for example, that phones harm learning. The **conclusion** is a derived judgement ("Phones reduce grades"). The **course of action** is the practical step ("Ban phones").

The relationship is directional: assumption → statement → conclusion. The assumption sits *behind* the statement; the conclusion sits *in front* of it. LAT questions almost always test whether you can locate that hidden step.

#### The negation test (mechanism)

To verify a candidate assumption, deny it. If the statement becomes illogical, contradictory, or absurd, the candidate is a **necessary assumption**. If the statement still survives, the candidate is merely a *possible* or *irrelevant* belief. This single test eliminates 60–70% of wrong options on a typical LAT paper.

#### Distinguishing assumption from neighbours

| Concept | What it does | LAT trap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assumption | Hidden premise that must be true | In many papers misread as conclusion |
| Conclusion | Deduction drawn from assumptions | Students pick this instead of the premise |
| Course of action | Prescribes what should be done | Looks like assumption but is prescriptive |
| Inference | A reasoned deduction | Confused with assumption when both feel "hidden" |

#### Common LAT patterns

- "Let us…" → assumption about the audience's willingness to act.
- "The government should…" → assumption that the action will solve a stated problem.
- "If X happens, then Y" → assumption that the link between X and Y is valid.

> **Scoring tip:** When two options both *can* be true, choose the one the speaker is *compelled* to believe — not the one a critic can add.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge cases that trap toppers

1. **Suppressor assumptions** — facts whose removal would *strengthen* the argument but whose presence is still presupposed. LAT rarely tests these, but HEC has included them in past papers.
2. **Cultural presuppositions** — statements like "Parents should guide their children" assume parental authority is accepted. Students miss these because they read them as obvious.
3. **Value-loaded triggers** — words like *should* smuggle in a moral or policy belief. Strip the trigger word and the assumption surfaces.

#### Worked micro-example

**Statement:** "The university should introduce a dress code to improve academic focus."
**Candidate assumption:** Students' clothing currently distracts from study.
**Negation test:** If clothing *does not* distract students, then the dress code cannot logically improve focus. The statement collapses. Therefore, the candidate is a **necessary assumption**.
A wrong option can read: "Students will follow the dress code." This is a *course of action* outcome, not an assumption.

#### Common mistakes in the LAT exam hall

| Mistake | Why it loses marks |
| --- | --- |
| Choosing a "should/ought" option | That is a course of action, not a premise |
| Picking a restated statement | The assumption must be unstated, not paraphrased |
| Selecting a "possible" belief | Assumptions must be *necessary*, not probable |
| Ignoring the negation test | Forces guesswork instead of elimination |

#### Connection to adjacent topics

Statement-Assumption sits beside **Statement-Conclusion**, **Statement-Argument**, and **Course of Action**. The same stem can appear across all four — only the question verb changes. Mastering the negation test transfers directly to **Statement-Assumption** and **Weakest Argument** questions.

#### Practice prompts

1. A municipal notice reads: "Citizens should segregate wet waste to reduce landfill load." Identify the assumption and apply the negation test.
2. "Let us ban junk food in school canteens to fight obesity." Which assumption is *necessarily* taken for granted, and which option is actually a course of action?

> **Time strategy:** Spend under 90 seconds per Statement-Assumption MCQ. Run the negation test on the most plausible option; if it survives, lock it in.
