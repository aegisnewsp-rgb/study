# Topic 7

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Fundamental Rights (Part III, Articles 12–35)** are six enforceable guarantees: Equality (14–18), Freedom (19–22), Against Exploitation (23–24), Religion (25–28), Cultural & Educational (29–30), and Constitutional Remedies (32). **Directive Principles (Part IV, Articles 36–51)** are non-justiciable policy directions borrowed from the **Irish Constitution**; **Fundamental Duties (Article 51A)** are 11 non-justiciable duties added by the **42nd Amendment (1976)** based on the **Swaran Singh Committee**.

**Article 32** makes writ jurisdiction a Fundamental Right itself; Dr. **B. R. Ambedkar** called it the *heart and soul* of the Constitution. **Article 21** ("Right to Life and Personal Liberty") has been judicially expanded into the broadest right — from the **Maneka Gandhi (1978)** ruling onward.

| Feature | FRs (Part III) | DPSPs (Part IV) | Duties (51A) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Justiciable? | Yes | No | No |
| Source | USA | Ireland | USSR (inspired) |
| Added by | Original | Original | 42nd Amendment, 1976 |
| Enforced by | Courts (Arts. 32/226) | Legislature | Moral obligation |

**High-yield pointers:** Writs = Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto. Right to Education is **Article 21A**, added by the **86th Amendment (2002)**. **Minerva Mills (1980)** — FRs cannot be destroyed by DPSPs.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Six Fundamental Rights in Detail

**Right to Equality (Art. 14–18)** establishes *equality before law* (Art. 14), bans discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth (Art. 15), opens public employment equally (Art. 16), abolishes untouchability (Art. 17), and ends titles except military/academic (Art. 18). **Right to Freedom (Art. 19–22)** lists six freedoms — speech, assembly, association, movement, residence, profession — and protects against arbitrary conviction (Art. 20) and arbitrary arrest (Art. 22). **Article 21** guarantees *life and personal liberty*, and through **Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)** now requires fairness, reasonableness, and non-arbitrariness in State action.

**Right against Exploitation (Art. 23–24)** forbids traffic in human beings and forced labour; no child under 14 may work in factories or mines. **Right to Freedom of Religion (Art. 25–28)** is *secular* — every person has freedom of conscience, religious denominations manage their own affairs, and State-funded institutions cannot give religious instruction.

#### Constitutional Remedies and the Five Writs

**Article 32** gives the **Supreme Court** writ power; **Article 226** gives the same to **High Courts**, plus territorial reach over persons residing outside the State. Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right; Article 226 is merely a constitutional right.

| Writ | Latin meaning | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Habeas Corpus | "to have the body" | Release illegal detention |
| Mandamus | "we command" | Compel public authority to act |
| Prohibition | "to forbid" | Stop inferior court from proceeding |
| Certiorari | "to be certified" | Transfer/quash inferior court order |
| Quo Warranto | "by what authority" | Challenge unlawful office-holder |

#### Directive Principles vs Fundamental Duties

**DPSPs (Art. 36–51)** cover welfare goals like a Uniform Civil Code (Art. 44), separation of judiciary (Art. 50), and free legal aid (Art. 39A). **Fundamental Duties (Art. 51A)** carry 11 obligations — to abide by the Constitution, promote common brotherhood, protect sovereignty, and (added by the **86th Amendment, 2002**) provide education opportunities to children aged 6–14. Both are *non-justiciable*.

#### Exam Patterns

TNPSC Group 1 typically frames this topic as **match-the-following** (writs to meanings), **assertion–reason** (FR vs DPSP conflict), and **150-word notes** (Article 21 expansion, Article 32 vs 226).

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Emergency Mechanics

During a **National Emergency**, Article 358 automatically suspends the six freedoms under **Article 19** as against the State, while **Article 359** suspends enforcement of other Fundamental Rights (except Arts. 20 and 21) via presidential order. The **44th Amendment (1978)** plugged this loophole: even during Emergency, **Article 20** (protection against conviction for offences) and **Article 21** (life and liberty) cannot be suspended. This safeguard emerged directly from the excesses of the 1975–77 Emergency.

#### Harmonious Construction Doctrine

Where Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles collide (e.g., Art. 14's equality vs Art. 39(b)'s wealth-distribution mandate), courts apply the **Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)** rule: *Indian Express* newspapers *harmony and balance* doctrine. **Article 39(b)** and **(c)** were the textual hooks; the verdict struck down parts of the **42nd Amendment** that gave Parliament untrammelled power to amend any part of the Constitution, reaffirming the *basic structure* doctrine of **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)**.

#### Common Mistakes to Avoid

> **Trap:** Candidates count Fundamental Duties as **10** — there are **11** under Article 51A(a) to (k). The 11th duty, 51A(k), on parent/guardian education duty, was inserted by the **86th Amendment, 2002**, not the original 1976 list.

> **Trap:** Confusing the source of the **Right to Education**. **Article 21A** is the constitutional provision; the operational **Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009** is the statute that implements it for ages 6–14.

#### Practice Prompts

1. *In 150 words, distinguish Article 32 from Article 226 jurisdiction, citing at least two points of difference and explaining why Dr. Ambedkar termed Article 32 the "heart and soul" of the Constitution.*
2. *Discuss how the Supreme Court has used Article 21 to expand the scope of personal liberty since Maneka Gandhi (1978). Cite two subsequent judgments.*
