# Measurements and Units

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Measurement** is the quantitative comparison of a physical quantity with a standard unit. The **International System of Units (SI)** defines seven **base quantities**: length in **metres (m)**, mass in **kilograms (kg)**, time in **seconds (s)**, electric current in **ampere (A)**, temperature in **kelvin (K)**, amount of substance in **mole (mol)**, and luminous intensity in **candela (cd)**. All other units (e.g. **newton N = kg·m·s⁻²**, **joule J = kg·m²·s⁻²**, **watt W = J·s⁻¹**) are **derived units** built from these seven. Density is the key derived quantity for WASSCE Paper I: **ρ = m / V** (kg·m⁻³). Standard prefixes you must recognise are **kilo (k, 10³)**, **centi (c, 10⁻²)**, **milli (m, 10⁻³)**, and **micro (µ, 10⁻⁶)**. **Accuracy** = closeness to the true value; **precision** = closeness of repeated readings to one another. The two laboratory instruments tested most often are the **vernier caliper** (reading = main scale + vernier coincidence × 0.01 mm) and the **micrometer screw gauge** (reading = main scale + thimble × 0.01 mm).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Fundamental vs. Derived Quantities
A **fundamental (base) quantity** is independent of other quantities and is defined by a standard (metre, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole, candela). A **derived quantity** is obtained by multiplying, dividing, or raising base quantities to powers. Examples frequently tested: **area A = L² (m²)**, **volume V = L³ (m³)**, **velocity v = d/t (m·s⁻¹)**, **density ρ = m/V (kg·m⁻³)**, **force F = ma (kg·m·s⁻² = N)**, **pressure P = F/A (N·m⁻² = Pa)**, and **kinetic energy KE = ½mv² (kg·m²·s⁻² = J)**.

#### Prefixes and Scientific Notation
Converting between units uses powers of ten. Memorise: **tera (T, 10¹²)**, **giga (G, 10⁹)**, **mega (M, 10⁶)**, **kilo (k, 10³)**, **centi (c, 10⁻²)**, **milli (m, 10⁻³)**, **micro (µ, 10⁻⁶)**, **nano (n, 10⁻⁹)**, **pico (p, 10⁻¹²)**. Example: 5.2 mm = 5.2 × 10⁻³ m.

#### Errors in Measurement
**Systematic errors** shift every reading in the same direction (zero error, miscalibrated instrument, parallax) and cannot be removed by averaging. **Random errors** produce scatter around the true value and are reduced by taking the **mean** of several readings. A **zero error** on a vernier or micrometer must be subtracted from every reading.

#### Significant Figures
Record **all certain digits plus one estimated digit**. The final result of a calculation must be quoted to the **least precise measurement** used. Example: 2.5 × 3.42 = 8.55, but because 2.5 has two significant figures the answer is reported as **8.6**.

#### Reading Instruments
**Vernier caliper:** MSR (last visible mm before the vernier zero) + (vernier coincidence × 0.01 mm); zero-error correction applied. **Micrometer screw gauge:** main-scale mm + (thimble division × 0.01 mm); corrected similarly.

#### WASSCE Pattern
Paper I Objective usually tests 1–2 items on units, prefixes, and instrument-reading conversions. Paper II Essay/Structured asks students to read a vernier or micrometer diagram and quote a value to the correct precision.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Dimensional Analysis
Every physical equation must be **dimensionally homogeneous**: the left-hand side and right-hand side must reduce to the same combination of M, L, T (and I, Θ, N, J when relevant). Use this to (1) check equations, (2) derive relationships, and (3) convert units. Example: the period of a simple pendulum is assumed T = k·L^x·g^y. Equating dimensions [T] = [L]^x [L·T⁻²]^y gives x + y = 0 and y = −1/2, hence x = 1/2 and **T = 2π√(L/g)**.

#### Accuracy, Precision, and Sensitivity
A measuring instrument is **accurate** when readings cluster around the true value, **precise** when repeated readings cluster tightly together, and **sensitive** when it detects small changes. A rifle with tightly-grouped shots off-centre is precise but not accurate. The **least count** of an instrument (smallest measurable change) defines its resolution.

#### Worked Example — Micrometer Reading
A screw gauge has a thimble reading of 23 and the thimble coincides with the 0.47 mm mark. The zero error is +0.03 mm.
- Main scale (sleeve) = 4.5 mm
- Thimble = 0.47 mm
- Uncorrected = 4.97 mm
- **Corrected length = 4.97 − 0.03 = 4.94 mm**

#### Common Mistakes
- Confusing **mass (kg, beam balance)** with **weight (N, spring balance)**; weight W = mg depends on local gravity.
- Reading the vernier main scale at the vernier zero mark rather than the **last visible mark before it**.
- Forgetting the **zero-error correction** on calipers and micrometers.
- Quoting answers to more significant figures than the data supports.
- Treating a **systematic error** as something averaging can fix — it cannot; it must be eliminated at the source.

#### Practice Prompts
1. A metal block of mass 0.045 kg displaces 5.0 cm³ of water. Calculate its density in kg·m⁻³ and state the answer to the correct number of significant figures.
2. The vernier coincidence of a caliper is 6, the main scale reads 3.4 cm, and the zero error is −0.02 cm. Find the corrected measurement in mm.
