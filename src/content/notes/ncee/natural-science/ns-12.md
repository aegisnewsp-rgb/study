# Safety in the Home and School

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Safety** in the home and school means the knowledge, attitudes and habits that prevent accidents and injuries in places where we live, learn and play. A **hazard** is anything that can cause harm (fire, wet floor, exposed wire, sharp knife, chemical); an **accident** is an unplanned event that results from a hazard. **First aid** is the immediate, temporary care given to an injured or sick person before a doctor arrives. Memorise four core responses: cool a **burn** under running water for at least 10 minutes, clean a **cut** with clean water and bandage it, switch off the power before touching a victim of **electric shock**, and lay a fainted person flat with fresh air. NCEE commonly tests the *match-the-hazard* idea (water on oil/petrol fire = wrong; sand or extinguisher = right) and the *look-left-look-right* pedestrian rule. Three high-yield pointers: keep chemicals out of children's reach, never overload sockets, do not run on staircases.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Meaning of Safety, Hazards and Accidents
**Safety** is the state of being protected from danger or harm, achieved through correct knowledge, careful attitudes and safe habits. A **hazard** is a potential source of danger (e.g. a frayed wire, a slippery kitchen floor, an unlabelled chemical bottle), while an **accident** is an unexpected event that causes injury, damage or death. Common *home hazards* include fire outbreaks, electric shock from exposed wires, sharp objects (knives, broken glass), poisonous chemicals left within children's reach, gas leaks from poorly maintained cookers, and choking on small objects such as beads or peanuts. *School hazards* include broken desks and chairs, overcrowded corridors during dismissal, laboratory chemicals and broken glassware, sharp tools in the art/craft room, and poorly maintained playground equipment.

#### Causes of Accidents
Most accidents in the home and school are preventable. They are usually caused by **carelessness** (rushing on stairs, playing with matches), **ignorance** of safety rules (touching sockets with wet hands), **poor housekeeping** (spills not wiped, toys left on stairways), **faulty equipment** (damaged cords, cracked gas cylinders), and **failure to obey rules** (running in corridors, ignoring laboratory instructions).

#### First Aid: Definition and Basic Responses
**First aid** is the immediate and temporary care given to a victim of accident or sudden illness before the arrival of a qualified medical doctor or before the victim is taken to hospital. It is not a replacement for medical treatment — it only buys time and prevents the condition from worsening. Standard responses to remember:

| Emergency | Correct First Aid Action |
|---|---|
| **Cuts and wounds** | Wash with clean water or antiseptic, apply pressure with clean cloth, bandage; seek help if bleeding is heavy. |
| **Burns** | Hold under cool running water for 10–15 minutes; cover with clean, non-stick dressing; never apply ice, butter or oil. |
| **Electric shock** | Switch off the mains first; do not touch the victim with bare hands; use a dry wooden stick to separate them; call for help. |
| **Fainting** | Lay the person flat on their back, loosen tight clothing, allow fresh air, do not crowd the person. |
| **Choking** | Encourage coughing; for small children, give firm back blows between the shoulder blades. |

#### Fire Prevention and Fire-Fighting
Prevent fires by switching off electrical appliances after use, keeping matches and lighters away from children, storing petrol and kerosene in sealed containers away from flames, and never overloading a single socket with many plugs. When fire breaks out, the method of extinguishing depends on what is burning: **water** for wood, paper or cloth fires; **sand** or a **fire extinguisher (CO₂ or dry powder)** for oil, petrol or electrical fires; a **fire blanket or thick cloth** for clothing-on-fire; and **smothering the lid** for a small cooking-gas flame. NCEE questions often present the wrong method (e.g. throwing water on a petrol fire) and ask the student to identify the error.

#### Electrical, Road and School Safety
Electrical safety rules: never insert wet hands or metal objects into sockets, replace damaged cords immediately, keep water away from switches, and switch off appliances before unplugging. Road safety: walk on the side of the road *facing oncoming traffic* so you can see approaching vehicles, use pedestrian crossings (zebra crossings), look **right–left–right** before crossing, and never play on the road. School safety: do not run on staircases or corridors, avoid pushing in queues, handle laboratory chemicals only with a teacher's permission, and report any broken furniture or equipment to the class teacher.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Personal Hygiene as a Form of Personal Safety
While NCEE often frames safety as protection from accidents, **personal hygiene** is a closely related preventive practice that keeps the body free from disease-causing organisms. Key practices: wash hands with soap and clean water before eating, after using the toilet and after handling refuse; bathe daily; brush teeth at least twice a day; keep finger nails short and clean (long nails trap dirt and may scratch the skin, opening a route for infection); cover the mouth and nose with a handkerchief when coughing or sneezing; and dispose of refuse in covered bins to keep flies and rodents away from the home.

#### Storage of Chemicals, Medicines and Sharp Objects
One of the highest-frequency NCEE traps involves how domestic substances are stored. Medicines, pesticides, kerosene, petrol, detergents and insecticides must be **labelled clearly**, kept in their original containers, and stored **on high shelves or locked cabinets out of the reach of children**. Never transfer a chemical into a soft-drink bottle — children may drink it thinking it is a beverage. Knives, razor blades, scissors and needles belong in safe drawers, never on floors or bed edges.

#### Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
1. Applying **ice, butter, toothpaste or palm oil** to a burn — these trap heat and worsen the injury. Correct action is cool running water only.
2. Touching a person who is still in contact with an electric source — the rescuer becomes the next victim. Always cut the power first.
3. Using **water on a petrol, kerosene or electrical fire** — water spreads the fuel and conducts electricity, making the fire worse. Use sand or an extinguisher instead.
4. Believing that a faint person needs a hot drink immediately — actually, lay them flat and let them breathe first; giving food or drink to an unconscious person may cause choking.
5. Walking *with* traffic instead of facing it — children often think walking in the same direction as cars is safer, but facing traffic lets you see danger approaching and step aside.

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics
Safety links directly to **Family Health and Disease Prevention** (clean surroundings reduce infection), **Drugs and Medicines** (proper storage prevents accidental poisoning), **The Human Body** (understanding why cool water helps a burn ties to skin structure), and **Environmental Health** (refuse disposal prevents breeding of mosquitoes and rats).

#### Practice Prompts
1. A pupil's clothing catches fire in the laboratory. List, in the correct order, three actions you would take before the teacher arrives.
2. A younger brother drinks kerosene stored in an empty soft-drink bottle. Describe the first four steps of first aid you would give at home before rushing him to hospital.
