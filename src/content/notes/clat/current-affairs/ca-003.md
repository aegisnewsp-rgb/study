# Legal News

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Legal News in CLAT covers judicial pronouncements, ordinances, constitutional amendments, and statutory changes reported during the 12-month reference window before the exam. It forms roughly 5% of the paper inside the 25% Current Affairs & GK section. The highest-yield items are Supreme Court and High Court landmark judgments, Central Government ordinances under Article 123, and the three new criminal codes — **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS)**, **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS)** and **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA)** — which replaced the IPC, CrPC and Indian Evidence Act from 1 July 2024.

| Must-know | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| New criminal codes | BNS / BNSS / BSA enforced from 1 July 2024 |
| Ordinance power | Article 123 (Centre), Article 213 (State) |
| Writ jurisdiction | Article 32 (SC), Article 226 (HC) |
| Complete justice | Article 142 of the Constitution |
| Top reading list | The Hindu / Indian Express + LiveLaw, Bar & Bench |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### What examiners test
CLAT frames Legal News items as factual MCQs, comprehension passages, or principle-application sets. Each item is drawn from a reported development — a judgment, an ordinance, a bill receiving Presidential assent, or a constitutional bench ruling. Candidates must identify the court, the bench, the statutory provision interpreted, and the relief granted. The political slug of a news item (for example, "SC slams government") carries no marks; the legal issue does.

#### Mapping news to subject heads
Every reported item should be filed under a legal head. Criminal, constitutional, commercial, environmental, family, labour, tax, cyber and intellectual property rights (IPR) are the recurring categories. A single day's edition of *The Hindu* or *Indian Express* will surface items under three or four heads.

#### The new criminal codes
The IPC, CrPC and Indian Evidence Act stood repealed from 1 July 2024. Old IPC sections no longer answer a question framed in present tense — Section 302 IPC is now Section 302 BNS, the erstwhile Section 376 is Section 63 BNS, and Section 420 maps to Section 318 BNS. Procedurally, the BNSS and BSA govern investigation and evidence.

| Concept | Key point |
| --- | --- |
| Ratio decidendi | Binding legal reasoning of the court |
| Obiter dicta | Non-binding judicial observation |
| PIL | Public Interest Litigation under Article 32 / 226 |
| PMLA | Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002 |
| RTI Act | Right to Information Act, 2005 |

- Read at least one national daily and one law outlet (LiveLaw, Bar & Bench, Supreme Court Observer) daily.
- Note the bench strength, judge names, citation and date for every judgment you log.
- Distinguish between an ordinance, a bill passed by Parliament, and a Law Commission report.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Distinguishing binding law from commentary
A Supreme Court judgment contains *ratio decidendi* (the binding rule on the facts) and *obiter dicta* (persuasive observations). Examiners reward candidates who quote the ratio and cite the correct article or section. Treating obiter as the "law declared" is a recurring trap in principle-application sets.

#### Common mistakes examiners exploit
1. Quoting IPC sections in a post-July 2024 fact pattern instead of the BNS equivalents.
2. Citing a High Court division bench ruling as a Supreme Court judgment.
3. Treating a Law Commission recommendation or Standing Committee report as enacted law.
4. Reporting only the headline ("Court slams Centre") and skipping the statutory provision interpreted.
5. Citing judgments outside the 12-month reference window, which the Consortium disqualifies.

#### Worked example
A passage states: "The Union promulgated an Ordinance in December 2024 to amend the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023. The Ordinance lapsed in April 2025 because Parliament did not pass it within six weeks of reassembly."
The correct response identifies Article 123 of the Constitution, the six-week rule under the proviso, and the fact that an ordinance ceases to operate on lapse — it does not become void retrospectively.

#### Exam strategy
Legal News carries roughly 1–2 questions in the 50-question Current Affairs section. Spend under 90 seconds per item, eliminate by court level, and prefer the answer naming the specific statute or article over a generic option.
