# Arithmetic, Indices and Surds — Leaving Certificate (Established) Mathematics Notes

Sections 3.1, 3.4 and 3.5 cover the mechanical core: fractions, standard form, indices and surds. No calculator-relevant concept appears here that you do not already use constantly in every other strand. The marks are lost almost entirely to sign errors and to leaving answers in a form the marking scheme does not accept, and both are cheap to fix with a fixed routine.

---

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Indices

**Negative indices** move the term to the denominator: *a*⁻ⁿ = 1/*a*ⁿ.

**Zero index** gives 1 for any non-zero base.

**Fractional indices** are roots: *a*^(1/ⁿ) is the *n*th root of *a*.

**Multiplying** adds the indices; **dividing** subtracts them; **power of a power** multiplies them. These three, applied consistently, are the whole law.

The exam's use is inverse working: simplifying an expression so a variable sits in the numerator, and then finding an unknown index from an equation. In that second direction the reasoning is the reverse of the first, and candidates who try to apply the laws forwards get tangled. Work out what operation the expression represents, then solve for the index.

Two expressions that look alike and are not:

- *a*² *a*³ = *a*⁵, but (*a*²)³ = *a*⁶, and *a*² + *a*³ does not combine at all.
- √*x* means *x*^(1/2); √*x* + √*x* = 2√*x*, while √(*x* + *x*) is a completely different expression. Root signs that extend over only part of a term are the single most expensive misreading in this topic.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Standard form

Any number written as *a* × 10ⁿ with 1 ≤ *a* < 10.

**Addition and subtraction** require the powers of ten to match first — convert, then add or subtract, then return to standard form. Candidates who add 2.4 × 10⁵ and 3.1 × 10³ directly are adding quantities of different orders of magnitude, and the answer is wrong by two orders of magnitude.

**Multiplication and division** work directly: multiply or divide the coefficients, add or subtract the powers.

Estimation and order-of-magnitude questions are assessed. Saying "the answer is about 10⁷" from the coefficients alone is a legitimate and often-credited step.


#### Surds

A surd is an irrational root that cannot be simplified — √2, √3, √5, √7 and combinations of them. The arithmetic laws apply exactly as for fractions, which means surds never cancel to a rational number unless they are the *same* surd.

**Simplifying.** Take any perfect squares out of the root: √72 = √(36 × 2) = 6√2. √50 = √(25 × 2) = 5√2. √12 = 2√3. Two unlike surds never combine — √2 + √8 = √2 + 2√2 = 3√2 because √8 reduces to 2√2, but √2 + √3 stays as it is.

**Adding and subtracting.** Only like surds. Rewrite everything in reduced form first, then collect. This is worth a full extra line of working and prevents nearly every surd error.

**Multiplying and dividing.** Treat exactly as fractions: √*a* × √*b* = √(*ab*), and √*a* ÷ √*b* = √(*a*/*b*). Always reduce the result, because √8 is not an acceptable final answer.

**Rationalising the denominator.** The exam's regular request. Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate, which has the opposite sign in the middle term:

√2 / (5 − √2)  × (5 + √2)/(5 + √2)  =  (5√2 + 2) / (25 − 2)  =  (5√2 + 2) / 23

Two things earn the marks: writing the multiplication by the conjugate as an explicit step, and reducing the final answer. Leaving the surd in the denominator is the failure the question exists to prevent.

**Rationalising a binomial denominator with two surds.** Same procedure with *a* + √*b* and *a* − √*b*: their product is *a*² − *b*, which is rational. Then reduce any surds remaining in the numerator.


#### Fractions

The routine that keeps fraction errors out:

1. Simplify every surd in the question to reduced form before you start.
2. Convert everything to a common structure — a common index base for powers, a common number of terms for sums.
3. Do the operation.
4. Reduce and rationalise before you stop.
5. Estimate the answer. If the sign or magnitude is impossible, you have made an error before you write it.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Fractions and surds combined

The higher-value items mix them. Solving for *x* in an equation with surds, or evaluating a function at a surd argument, both require the same discipline: reduce, operate, rationalise, check.

The check is genuinely worth doing. A surd answer that comes out negative where the expression is manifestly positive has an error, and the estimate takes a second.

## Self-check

1. Simplify √75. *(5√3. √75 = √(25 × 3). Leaving √75 is not a simplified answer and forfeits the final mark.)*
2. Simplify √8 + √18. *(*4√2 + 3√2 = 7√2. Both roots must be reduced before combining; combining in their given form is the error.)*
3. Rationalise the denominator of 1/(√3 − 1). *(*(√3 + 1)/(√3 + 1) = (√3 + 1)/(3 − 1) = (√3 + 1)/2. The conjugate multiplication must be shown, not just the result.)*
4. Add 2.4 × 10⁵ and 3.1 × 10³ in standard form. *(2.71 × 10⁵. Convert the second to 0.031 × 10⁵ before adding — adding the coefficients directly gives 5.5 × 10⁵, which is out by a factor of 200.)*
5. What is the difference between *a*³ + *a*⁴ and *a*³ *a*⁴? *(Nothing in particular in common — unlike terms with different indices cannot be combined by adding indices, because they are not like terms. The product gives *a*⁷.)*
