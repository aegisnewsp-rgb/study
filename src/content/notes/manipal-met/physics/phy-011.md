### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

**Coulomb's law and superposition.** Force between two point charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ separated by distance $r$: $F = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \dfrac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2}$ with $k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \approx 9 \times 10^9 \text{ N·m}^2/\text{C}^2$. Force is along the line joining the charges, repulsive for like signs, attractive for unlike. Superposition: the net force on a test charge is the vector sum of forces from each source charge — never add magnitudes.

**Electric field.** $\vec{E} = \dfrac{\vec{F}}{q_0}$ for a positive test charge $q_0$. Field of a point charge: $E = \dfrac{k|q|}{r^2}$. Field lines start on positive charges, end on negative charges, never cross, and density reflects field magnitude.

**Dipole basics.** Dipole moment $\vec{p} = q \cdot \vec{d}$ from negative to positive charge. Field on the axial line: $E_{\text{axial}} = \dfrac{2kp}{r^3}$. Field on the equatorial line: $E_{\text{eq}} = \dfrac{kp}{r^3}$. Torque in a uniform field: $\vec{\tau} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E}$, maximum when $\vec{p} \perp \vec{E}$. Potential energy: $U = -\vec{p}\cdot\vec{E}$.

**Gauss's law.** $\Phi = \oint \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A} = \dfrac{q_{\text{enclosed}}}{\varepsilon_0}$. Three MET-favourite applications:
- Infinite line charge (linear density $\lambda$): $E = \dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$.
- Infinite plane sheet (surface density $\sigma$): $E = \dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$.
- Thin spherical shell (radius $R$, charge $Q$): $E = 0$ inside ($r < R$), $E = \dfrac{kQ}{r^2}$ outside ($r > R$).

**Potential.** $V = \dfrac{kq}{r}$ for a point charge, $V_{\text{dipole}} = \dfrac{kp\cos\theta}{r^2}$ on the axis. Work done moving charge $q$ through potential difference $\Delta V$: $W = q\Delta V$. Equipotential surfaces are always perpendicular to $\vec{E}$.

**Capacitors.** Parallel-plate capacitance with dielectric: $C = \dfrac{\kappa \varepsilon_0 A}{d}$. Series: $\dfrac{1}{C_s} = \sum \dfrac{1}{C_i}$. Parallel: $C_p = \sum C_i$. Energy stored: $U = \dfrac{1}{2}CV^2 = \dfrac{Q^2}{2C} = \dfrac{1}{2}QV$.

### 🟡 Standard — Exam Prep (3d-3w)

**Worked Example 1 — Three charges at a triangle.**
Three point charges $+2\,\mu\text{C}$, $-3\,\mu\text{C}$, and $+5\,\mu\text{C}$ sit at the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side $10\,\text{cm}$. Find the net force on the $-3\,\mu\text{C}$ charge.
Use $F = kq_1q_2/r^2$ from each of the other two charges, vector-decompose along and perpendicular to one side, then sum. With $r = 0.1\,\text{m}$, the magnitude from $+2\,\mu\text{C}$ is $F_1 = (9 \times 10^9)(2 \times 10^{-6})(3 \times 10^{-6})/(0.1)^2 = 5.4\,\text{N}$, attractive (toward $+2\,\mu\text{C}$). Magnitude from $+5\,\mu\text{C}$: $F_2 = 13.5\,\text{N}$, attractive (toward $+5\,\mu\text{C}$). The angle between them is $60^\circ$, so $F_{\text{net}} = \sqrt{F_1^2 + F_2^2 + 2F_1F_2\cos 60^\circ} = \sqrt{5.4^2 + 13.5^2 + 5.4 \cdot 13.5} \approx 17.0\,\text{N}$, directed along the bisector of the two attractive forces.

**Worked Example 2 — Spherical shell and point charge.** A thin spherical shell of radius $R = 0.5\,\text{m}$ carries total charge $Q = +4\,\mu\text{C}$ uniformly on its surface. A point charge $q = +1\,\mu\text{C}$ sits at the centre.
By Gauss's law, the field inside the shell is zero, so the force on $q$ at the centre is zero. The potential at the centre is the sum of contributions from every shell element, all at distance $R$: $V_{\text{center}} = \dfrac{kQ}{R} = \dfrac{(9 \times 10^9)(4 \times 10^{-6})}{0.5} = 7.2 \times 10^4\,\text{V}$. The potential energy of the system is $U = qV_{\text{center}} = (1 \times 10^{-6})(7.2 \times 10^4) = 7.2 \times 10^{-2}\,\text{J}$.

**Worked Example 3 — Capacitor network.** Three capacitors, $C_1 = 6\,\mu\text{F}$, $C_2 = 3\,\mu\text{F}$, $C_3 = 2\,\mu\text{F}$, are connected with $C_2$ and $C_3$ in parallel, and that combination in series with $C_1$. Find the equivalent capacitance and the charge on $C_1$ when connected to a $12\,\text{V}$ battery.
$C_{23} = C_2 + C_3 = 5\,\mu\text{F}$. Series: $C_{\text{eq}} = \dfrac{C_1 \cdot C_{23}}{C_1 + C_{23}} = \dfrac{6 \cdot 5}{6 + 5} = \dfrac{30}{11} \approx 2.73\,\mu\text{F}$. Total charge from the battery $Q = C_{\text{eq}} V = 2.73 \times 12 \approx 32.7\,\mu\text{C}$. Since $C_1$ is in series, the charge on $C_1$ equals $Q = 32.7\,\mu\text{C}$. Voltage across $C_1$: $V_1 = Q/C_1 \approx 5.45\,\text{V}$. Voltage across the parallel combination: $12 - 5.45 = 6.55\,\text{V}$. Charge on $C_2$: $6.55 \times 3 \approx 19.6\,\mu\text{C}$; on $C_3$: $6.55 \times 2 \approx 13.1\,\mu\text{C}$. Both sum to $32.7\,\mu\text{C}$.

**Practice Set.**
1. Two charges $+4\,\mu\text{C}$ and $-1\,\mu\text{C}$ are placed $30\,\text{cm}$ apart. Find the point on the line joining them where the net field is zero. (Answer: $1.71\,\text{m}$ from the smaller charge on its far side — use $E_1 = E_2$, solve $x/(x+d) = \sqrt{q_1/q_2}$.)
2. A parallel-plate capacitor with plate area $200\,\text{cm}^2$ and separation $2\,\text{mm}$ is filled with a dielectric of $\kappa = 4$. Find $C$ and the energy stored at $50\,\text{V}$. ($C = 3.54 \times 10^{-10}\,\text{F}$, $U = 4.43 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{J}$.)
3. Use Gauss's law to find the field at $r = 0.3\,\text{m}$ from an infinite line charge of linear density $\lambda = 6\,\mu\text{C/m}$. ($E = 3.6 \times 10^5\,\text{N/C}$ outward.)

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (1mo+)

**Why superposition holds.** Coulomb's law is linear in each charge, so the field due to a continuous distribution is the integral of point-charge contributions: $\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \int \dfrac{\rho(\vec{r'})(\vec{r} - \vec{r'})}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r'}|^3} d\tau'$. Linearity of integration then guarantees the superposition principle for fields and forces. This is why Gauss's law plus symmetry solves three canonical geometries in seconds — and why problems that break that symmetry (off-axis points, finite cylinders) require direct integration.

**Boundary conditions at a conductor.** Inside a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium, $\vec{E} = 0$ and charge resides on the surface. Just outside, the field is perpendicular to the surface with magnitude $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$. The potential is constant throughout the conductor's volume. For a cavity inside a conductor with no charge inside, the field in the cavity is zero regardless of external fields — this is the electrostatic shielding effect used in Faraday cages.

**Dielectric physics.** Inserting a dielectric of constant $\kappa$ between capacitor plates reduces the field inside to $E_0/\kappa$ because bound surface charges on the dielectric partially cancel the free charge on the plates. The capacitance scales by $\kappa$. Breakdown field sets a hard limit: typical air breakdown is $\sim 3 \times 10^6\,\text{V/m}$; going beyond it produces a spark that resets the charge distribution.

**Energy density of the field.** A capacitor's stored energy can be rewritten as $U = \dfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2 \cdot Ad$ for the parallel-plate geometry, which equals energy density $u = \dfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2$ integrated over the field volume. The same expression $u = \dfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon E^2$ generalises to any electric field configuration — energy is genuinely stored in the field, not just "in the plates".

**Connection to current electricity.** A discharging capacitor through a resistor follows $Q(t) = Q_0 e^{-t/RC}$. The time constant $\tau = RC$ determines how fast the field collapses; this is the bridge topic to Current Electricity (phy-012), where $RC$ circuits reappear.

## Common traps

1. **Confusing $E$ inside and outside a spherical shell.** Inside a uniformly charged thin shell ($r < R$), $E = 0$ exactly — the most-tested fact in MET-style Gauss questions. Outside ($r > R$), use $E = kQ/r^2$ as if the entire charge were a point charge at the centre.
2. **Adding scalar magnitudes for force or field on multiple charges.** Always vector-add. Two equal charges at the vertices of an equilateral triangle with the test charge at the third vertex produce a net force whose magnitude is $\sqrt{3}$ times one side force, not $2$ times.
3. **Mixing $C$ formulas.** Parallel: $C_p = C_1 + C_2 + \dots$ Series: $\dfrac{1}{C_s} = \dfrac{1}{C_1} + \dfrac{1}{C_2} + \dots$. In series, charge is the same on each capacitor but voltages add; in parallel, voltage is the same but charges add. A common slip is using the wrong formula because the diagram was misread.
4. **Forgetting that potential is a scalar with sign.** $V = kq/r$ keeps the sign of $q$. The potential at the midpoint of a dipole on the equatorial line is zero (the two contributions cancel), but the field there is not zero. Candidates confuse the two.
5. **Ignoring the dielectric constant when stated.** A question that says "filled with a dielectric of $\kappa$" expects every capacitance value multiplied by $\kappa$. Forgetting this underestimates $C$ by exactly the factor $\kappa$.
