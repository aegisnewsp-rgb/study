# Hydrocarbons

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Memory Hook anchor for one-day revision: classify by **saturation + hybridisation**, not by name alone.

A hydrocarbon contains only carbon and hydrogen. Three families cover nearly every NEET MCQ on this chapter:

- **Alkanes** — CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, sp³, σ-bonds only (e.g., ethane C₂H₆).
- **Alkenes** — CₙH₂ₙ, sp², one C=C (one σ + one π), electrophilic addition.
- **Alkynes** — CₙH₂ₙ₋₂, sp, one C≡C (one σ + two π), acidic terminal H.

Arenes (benzene C₆H₆) sit apart: resonance-stabilised, substitution not addition.

| Family | General formula | Hybridisation | Characteristic bond |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkane | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ | sp³ | σ only |
| Alkene | CₙH₂ₙ (n ≥ 2) | sp² | σ + π (C=C) |
| Alkyne | CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ (n ≥ 2) | sp | σ + 2π (C≡C) |
| Arene (benzene) | CₙHₙ (n = 6 for C₆H₆) | sp² | delocalised π sextet |

> 💡 **Memory Hook:** "**S**aturated = **S**igma only = **S**pongy (alkane)" → the more π-bonds you add, the more reactive the molecule becomes toward electrophiles.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Working knowledge for 2-day to 2-month preparation: mechanisms, nomenclature logic, and the distinguishing tests examiners recycle.

#### Nomenclature that decides options

NEET questions pivot on IUPAC root names: meth- (1C), eth- (2C), prop- (3C), but- (4C), pent- (5C), hex- (6C). Suffix changes with bond type: **-ane** (single), **-ene** (one C=C), **-yne** (one C≡C). The locant (number) is chosen to give the lowest set to the multiple bond; for the same lowest locant, the lowest goes to the principal characteristic group.

Examples:
- CH₂=CH–CH₂–CH₃ → but-1-ene (locant 1 beats 2).
- CH₃–CH=CH–CH₃ → but-2-ene; the molecule shows **cis-trans isomerism** because each doubly-bonded carbon carries two different groups.
- HC≡C–CH₂–CH₃ → but-1-yne (terminal H, acidic).

#### Reactivity pattern: σ vs π chemistry

π-bonds are weaker (≈ 251 kJ mol⁻¹) than σ-bonds (≈ 347 kJ mol⁻¹), so alkenes and alkynes undergo **electrophilic addition**. Alkanes lack π-electrons and instead undergo **free-radical halogenation** (UV-initiated: initiation → propagation → termination).

#### Markovnikov addition across HX

> 📌 **Formula Check:** for R–CH=CH–R′ + H–X, H attaches to the carbon already carrying **more hydrogens**, X attaches to the more substituted carbon because it forms the **more stable carbocation** (3° > 2° > 1° > methyl).

Peroxide (R–O–O–R′) reverses this to **anti-Markovnikov** (Kharasch effect) for HBr only, via a radical chain that installs Br on the less substituted carbon.

#### Comparison matrix: which reagent tells what

| Reagent | Alkane | Alkene | Alkyne (terminal) | Benzene |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Br₂ in CCl₄ (dark) | no reaction | decolourises fast | decolourises | **no reaction** |
| Dilute KMnO₄ (Baeyer's) | no reaction | decolourises (→ diol) | decolourises (warm: → COOH cleavage) | **no reaction** under mild conditions |
| Ammoniacal AgNO₃ | – | – | **white ppt** of silver acetylide | – |
| Ammoniacal Cu₂Cl₂ | – | – | **red ppt** of copper acetylide | – |

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** cycloalkanes also fit CₙH₂ₙ but they are **saturated** — they do NOT decolourise bromine water. Selecting an alkene just from the formula, without checking for C=C, costs the mark.

#### Worked problem in prose

**Q.** Compound A has molecular formula C₄H₆. It decolourises bromine in CCl₄, reacts with ammoniacal AgNO₃ to give a white precipitate, and on catalytic hydrogenation absorbs two equivalents of H₂ to give n-butane. Identify A and write the equations.

**Working.** C₄H₆ fits CₙH₂ₙ₋₂, so A is either an alkyne or a diene. A white ppt with AgNO₃ narrows this to a **terminal alkyne** (R–C≡C–H). Two equivalents of H₂ giving n-butane means the C≡C plus any C=C are fully reduced; with C₄H₆ and only one triple bond, both H₂ molecules saturate that one C≡C. The terminal alkyne C₄H₆ is therefore **but-1-yne, HC≡C–CH₂–CH₃**.

Equations:
1. HC≡C–CH₂–CH₃ + 2 H₂ (Pd/BaSO₄ then Pt, or Ni, Δ) → CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃.
2. HC≡C–CH₂–CH₃ + Ag(NH₃)₂⁺ → Ag–C≡C–CH₂–CH₃ (white) + NH₃ + NH₄⁺.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** students often write Ag₂O or just "Ag⁺" — the test requires the **ammine complex**, not bare silver ion.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Edge cases, quantitative resonance numbers, and the traps that surface only when two chapters collide on the paper.

#### Resonance energy and why benzene refuses addition

Benzene's experimental heat of hydrogenation is ≈ 208 kJ mol⁻¹; hydrogenating three hypothetical cyclohexatriene "double bonds" separately would release ≈ 3 × 119.7 ≈ 359 kJ mol⁻¹. The shortfall of **≈ 150.5 kJ mol⁻¹ (≈ 36 kcal mol⁻¹)** is the **resonance energy**, the thermodynamic reason benzene undergoes **substitution** (which preserves the sextet) rather than addition (which would destroy it). Hückel's rule generalises this: planar, fully conjugated rings with **(4n + 2) π-electrons** (n = 0, 1, 2, …) are aromatic.

#### Acidity ladder: why sp-H is removable

| Bond / species | Approximate pKₐ |
|---|---|
| Alkane C–H (sp³) | ≈ 50 |
| Alkene C–H (sp²) | ≈ 36 |
| Terminal alkyne C–H (sp, 50% s-character) | ≈ 25 |
| Water O–H | 15.7 |

The 50% s-character of an sp hybrid pulls electron density closer to carbon, making the terminal alkynyl hydrogen removable by NaNH₂. **Only terminal alkynes** form acetylides with Ag⁺/Cu⁺; internal alkynes (R–C≡C–R′) cannot.

#### Conformations worth memorising

Ethane's **staggered** conformation is more stable than **eclipsed** by ≈ 12 kJ mol⁻¹; the rotational barrier (hydrogen-hydrogen eclipsing strain) reappears as the same 12 kJ mol⁻¹ torsional barrier. In butane, looking along C2–C3, the **anti** conformer (methyl groups 180° apart) is the global minimum; **gauche** sits ≈ 3.8 kJ mol⁻¹ higher, and the **eclipsed** (methyl–methyl) conformation is the maximum.

#### Advanced traps and exceptions

1. **Geometric isomerism requires two different groups on EACH doubly-bonded carbon.** 2-butene has cis/trans; 1-butene does not. 2-methylbut-2-ene has none either (one carbon carries two identical methyls).
2. **Peroxide effect applies to HBr only.** HCl and HI show no clean reversal because the H–Cl bond is too strong and the H–I step is endothermic, so the radical chain breaks.
3. **Lindlar's catalyst (Pd/CaCO₃, poisoned) gives cis-alkene from alkyne**; **Na/NH₃(l)** gives the **trans** alkene via a radical anion mechanism. Confusing these switches the geometry.
4. **Markovnikov's rule reverses when the alkene bears an electron-withdrawing group** (e.g., CF₃–CH=CH₂ + HX: H goes to the carbon bearing CF₃ because that carbon stabilises the negative charge developing on it).
5. **Heat of combustion falls ≈ 658.6 kJ mol⁻¹ per added CH₂** — useful for comparing isomeric alkanes by stability, but isomeric alkenes/alkynes do **not** follow the same simple increment.
6. **Benzene's bromination needs a Lewis-acid catalyst (anhydrous FeBr₃ or AlBr₃)**; without it, the ring is too unreactive and the test tube stays yellow.
7. **Friedel–Crafts alkylation fails on strongly deactivated rings** (e.g., nitrobenzene) and also rearranges carbocations — that is why direct Friedel–Crafts on n-propyl chloride gives mostly isopropyl product.

#### Two practice prompts

1. A hydrocarbon C₅H₈ shows cis-trans isomerism but does NOT give a precipitate with ammoniacal AgNO₃. Identify the molecule and justify why the silver test is silent even though the formula fits an alkyne.
2. Benzene (3.90 g, M = 78 g mol⁻¹) is hydrogenated in a lab at 298 K using 1 atm H₂. Estimate the enthalpy released in kJ using a resonance energy of 150.5 kJ mol⁻¹; then state why this number matters when predicting whether benzene will undergo addition versus substitution at 298 K.
