# Absorption Covers the Rate and Extent of Drug

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your INI CET exam.

**Pharmacokinetics** is the quantitative study of drug movement through the body, organised around the **ADME** framework — **A**bsorption, **D**istribution, **M**etabolism, and **E**xcretion. The five parameters you must keep on one finger are: **Vd**, **CL**, **t½**, **AUC**, and **F**. Each is calculated from plasma concentration–time data and feeds directly into rational dosing.

- **Vd (L)** = Amount in body ÷ Plasma concentration
- **CL (mL/min)** = Dose ÷ AUC; **t½ (h)** = 0.693 × Vd ÷ CL
- **Loading dose** = Vd × Target Cp ÷ F; **Maintenance dose** = CL × Target Cp × τ ÷ F
- Steady-state concentration is reached after **4–5 half-lives**
- **Bioequivalence** limit: 90% CI of Cmax and AUC must lie within **80–125%**

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard depth for systematic Pharmacology revision.

#### ADME — the umbrella framework
Absorption covers the rate and extent of drug entry, governed by route, formulation, lipophilicity, and the **first-pass (presystemic) metabolism** in the gut wall and liver. Distribution is captured numerically by **Vd**, an apparent volume that reflects protein binding and tissue partitioning. Metabolism splits into **Phase I** (CYP450-mediated oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis) and **Phase II** (glucuronidation, sulfation, acetylation). Excretion is mainly renal — combining **GFR**, tubular secretion, and reabsorption — with biliary and faecal routes contributing for non-renal drugs.

#### First-order vs zero-order kinetics
In **first-order kinetics**, the elimination rate is proportional to plasma concentration, so **t½ stays constant** regardless of dose. Most drugs at therapeutic doses obey this rule. In **zero-order kinetics**, elimination enzymes are saturated, so a fixed amount is removed per unit time; **t½ rises with dose**. Classic examples are ethanol, phenytoin at therapeutic concentrations, and aspirin at toxic doses.

#### Worked derivation
Starting from CL = Dose ÷ AUC and t½ = 0.693 × Vd ÷ CL, a doubling of Vd (oedema) increases t½ without changing CL — useful clinically when interpreting prolonged drug action.

| Parameter | Formula (first-order) | Units | Clinical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Vd | Dose ÷ Cp | L | Loading-dose calculation |
| CL | Dose ÷ AUC | mL/min or L/h | Maintenance-dose calculation |
| t½ | 0.693 × Vd ÷ CL | h | Time to steady state |
| AUC | F × Dose ÷ CL | mg·h/L | Extent of exposure |
| F | AUCoral ÷ AUCiv | fraction 0–1 | Bioequivalence |

- High-yield point: **Css = F × Dose ÷ (CL × τ)** at steady state.
- TDM drugs: aminoglycosides, vancomycin, phenytoin, digoxin, lithium.
- Hepatic/renal dysfunction lowers CL and lengthens t½ — both must be measured.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Mastery depth covering edge cases and exam traps.

#### Edge cases and pitfalls
A Vd of 15 L/kg for **chloroquine** or 8 L/kg for **digoxin** is not a physiological compartment — it is a mathematical construct reflecting extensive tissue sequestration. Misreading it as a real volume costs marks. In **obesity**, lipophilic drugs (e.g. diazepam) show increased Vd; hydrophilic ones (e.g. aminoglycosides) require dosing on lean body weight. Lengthening of t½ may stem from **decreased CL** (renal/hepatic failure) or **increased Vd** (oedema) — identical t½ values can mask opposite mechanisms, so report both parameters in viva answers.

#### Bioequivalence and clinical implications
Two products are bioequivalent if the **90% CI** for both **Cmax** (rate) and **AUC** (extent) lies within **80–125%** under fasting, single-dose, crossover conditions. Generic approval in India uses this rule. Confusing AUC with Cmax is a classic MCQ trap: equal AUC with different Cmax is **not** bioequivalent.

| Drug class | TDM target | Toxic plasma level (approx.) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gentamicin | Peak 5–10 µg/mL | >12 µg/mL |
| Vancomycin | Trough 10–20 µg/mL | >40 µg/mL |
| Phenytoin | 10–20 µg/mL | >20 µg/mL |
| Digoxin | 0.5–2 ng/mL | >2.5 ng/mL |
| Lithium | 0.6–1.2 mEq/L | >1.5 mEq/L |

#### Exam strategy and practice
INI CET frequently sets a 1–2 mark numerical on loading or maintenance dose using a given Cp and τ — write the formula first, substitute, then check units (Vd in L, Cp in mg/L yields mg). Expect case-based questions where enzyme induction (rifampicin, phenytoin, carbamazepine, St John’s wort) lowers AUC of co-administered drugs (warfarin, OCP, ciclosporin), while inhibitors (ketoconazole, erythromycin, grapefruit juice) raise it. Practice prompt 1: compute loading and maintenance doses for digoxin (Vd 7 L/kg, CL 2 mL/min/kg, target Cp 1 ng/mL, τ 24 h, F 0.7) in a 70 kg adult. Practice prompt 2: a phenytoin dose is increased by 30 % — predict the plasma concentration change assuming zero-order kinetics at this level.
