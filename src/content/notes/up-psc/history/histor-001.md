# Ancient Indian History: From Prehistory to the Classical Gupta Age

## UPPSC PCS Examination Blueprint and Historical Scope

In the Uttar Pradesh Public Service Commission (UPPSC PCS) Combined State / Upper Subordinate Services Examination, Ancient Indian History forms an essential foundational pillar of General Studies Paper 1 in both the Preliminary and Main stages. In the Preliminary Examination, Ancient History regularly contributes 6 to 9 questions covering archaeological excavations, Vedic literature, religious reform movements, and imperial administrations. In the Main Examination (GS Paper 1), analytical questions focus on Mauryan civic administration, Ashoka's Dhamma policy, ancient art traditions (Gandhara, Mathura, and Amaravati), and the social transition toward early medieval feudalism.

In our review of UPPSC PCS past papers over the last ten years, questions concentrate on four primary thematic clusters:
1. **Harappan Urbanism and Archaeological Sites**: Specific findings, geographical river basins, and archaeological discoverers of Indus Valley Civilization sites (such as Lothal, Kalibangan, Dholavira, and Rakhigarhi).
2. **Vedic Transition and Institutional Evolution**: Social, political, and economic differences between the Early Vedic (Rigvedic pastoral) and Later Vedic (sedentary agricultural and iron-using) epochs.
3. **The 6th Century BCE Heterodox Transformation**: The political rise of the Magadhan Empire and the philosophical doctrines of Buddhism and Jainism, alongside the four great Buddhist Councils.
4. **Imperial Administration and Epigraphy**: The administrative machinery outlined in Kautilya's *Arthashastra*, Ashokan inscriptions (Major Rock Edicts and Pillar Edicts), and Gupta classical developments recorded in the *Prayag Prashasti*.

## Prehistory, Harappa, and the Vedic Transition

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Systematic analysis of urban bronze age culture, Vedic socioeconomic shifts, and religious movements.

When studying Ancient India for UPPSC, candidates must appreciate the technological and ecological factors that catalyzed transitions between historical periods.

#### 1. The Indus Valley Civilization (c. 2600–1900 BCE)
The Mature Harappan phase represented the first urbanization of the Indian subcontinent:
- **Urban Planning and Sanitation**: Cities were constructed on an orderly grid system with streets intersecting at right angles. The standard building material was kiln-fired brick adhering to the strict ratio of 4:2:1 (length:breadth:thickness). Houses featured private bathrooms, interior courtyards, and vertical wastewater pipes discharging into covered brick municipal drains equipped with inspection sumps.
- **Economic Integration**: Standardized weights based on the binary (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64) and decimal systems were manufactured from chert. Maritime commerce linked coastal sites (Lothal, Sutkagen Dor) with the Persian Gulf (Dilmun / modern Bahrain) and Mesopotamia (where Harappan merchants were referred to as *Meluhha*).
- **Religious Symbols**: Archaeological evidence indicates reverence for a proto-Shiva figure (the Pashupati seal featuring an ithyphallic deity surrounded by an elephant, tiger, rhinoceros, buffalo, and two deer), Mother Goddess figurines, sacred pipal trees, and conical stone phallic objects (*lingams*).

#### 2. Comparative Analysis of Early Vedic and Later Vedic Periods

```
Vedic Sociopolitical Transformation:
├── Early Vedic (c. 1500–1000 BCE) ── Pastoral seminomadic ── Tribal Rajan ── Cattle raids (Gavishti) ── Open Varna
└── Later Vedic (c. 1000–600 BCE) ── Sedentary agriculture ── Territorial Janapada ── Iron ploughshares ── Rigid Varna
```

| Societal & Economic Dimension | Early Vedic (Rigvedic) Era | Later Vedic (PGW / Iron) Era |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Economic Basis** | Pastoralism; cattle (*Gau*) formed principal wealth | Sedentary agriculture; iron tools (*Shyama Ayas*) expanded cultivation |
| **Political Organization** | Tribal chiefdom (*Rajan*) checked by *Sabha* and *Samiti* assemblies | Hereditary territorial monarchs (*Janapadas*); standing imperial courts |
| **Royal Consecration Rituals** | Simple tribal installation; no standing military forces | Elaborate legitimizing sacrifices: *Ashvamedha*, *Rajasuya*, *Vajapeya* |
| **Social Stratification** | Flexible occupational division; no rigid hereditary caste | Rigid hereditary fourfold *Varna* hierarchy; emergence of *Gotra* system |
| **Status of Women** | Attended assemblies (*Sabha*, *Vidatha*); composed hymns (Gargi, Lopamudra) | Disenfranchised from political assemblies; child marriage begins |
| **Religious Focus** | Naturalistic polytheism: Indra (250 hymns), Agni (200 hymns), Varuna | Sacrificial sacerdotalism; prominence of Prajapati (Creator), Rudra, and Vishnu |

## The Imperial Epoch: Mauryas, Post-Mauryas, and the Guptas

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> In-depth statecraft, administrative structures, trade networks, and cultural renaissance.

In our analysis of UPPSC Mains GS Paper 1 questions, examiners emphasize the comparative mechanics of imperial governance: comparing the highly centralized bureaucratic control of the Mauryas with the decentralized, land-grant-driven administration of the Guptas.

#### 1. The Mauryan Administrative Machine (c. 321–185 BCE)
Founded by Chandragupta Maurya with the strategic counsel of Chanakya (Kautilya), the Mauryan state established an unprecedented apparatus of fiscal and civic centralization, described in the *Arthashastra* and the Greek ambassador Megasthenes' *Indica*:
- **The Central Executive Hierarchy**: The king was assisted by the *Mantriparishad* and top executive officers called **Tirthas** (18 in total, including the *Samaharta* or Chief Revenue Collector, and *Sannidhata* or Chief Treasurer). Beneath them served 27 **Adhyakshas** (superintendents of state monopolies like mines, customs, and agriculture).
- **Ashoka's Policy of Dhamma**: Following the catastrophic bloodshed of the Kalinga War in 261 BCE (documented in **Major Rock Edict XIII**), Ashoka renounced aggressive warfare (*Bherighosha*) in favor of cultural and moral conquest (*Dhammaghosha*). His *Dhamma* was not a state religion but an ethical civic code emphasizing filial piety, non-injury to living beings (*Ahimsa*), religious tolerance, and courteous treatment of servants and prisoners. To supervise this policy, he created the dedicated office of **Dhamma Mahamattas**.

#### 2. The Post-Mauryan Synthesis (c. 200 BCE–300 CE)
The collapse of the Mauryan Empire led to vigorous regional political consolidation and international trade:
- **The Satavahanas (Deccan)**: Ruled from Pratishthana (modern Paithan). Prominent rulers included Gautamiputra Satakarni (who called himself *Eka Brahmana* and destroyer of the Sakas). The Satavahanas were the **first Indian dynasty to issue royal land grants with tax exemptions** to Buddhist monks and Brahmins, laying the structural groundwork for Indian feudalism.
- **The Kushans (Northern India)**: Reaching their height under **Kanishka I** (who inaugurated the **Saka Era in 78 CE**). They controlled the lucrative Silk Route traversing Central Asia and minted high-purity gold coins (*Dinaras*). The period witnessed the flowering of the **Gandhara School of Art** (Greco-Roman stylistic realism featuring Buddha in drapery with wavy hair) and the **Mathura School of Art** (indigenous red sandstone sculptures with robust, smiling iconography).

#### 3. The Classical Gupta Empire (c. 320–550 CE)
Often characterized as the "Golden Age" of classical Sanskrit literature and scientific advancement:
- **Samudragupta (c. 335–375 CE)**: Dubbed the "Napoleon of India" by Vincent Smith. His military conquests are immortalized in the **Allahabad Pillar Inscription** (*Prayag Prashasti*), composed in elegant Sanskrit *Champu* style by his court poet **Harishena**. His campaigns followed four distinct policies: violent annexation (*Prasabhoddharana*) of Northern Aryavarta kings; capture and liberation (*Grahana-Mokshanugraha*) of twelve Dakshinapatha (Deccan) rulers; subservience of border forest states (*Atavika*); and tributary diplomatic submission (*Daivaputra-Shahanushahi*) from frontier tribes.
- **Chandragupta II Vikramaditya (c. 375–415 CE)**: Annihilated the Western Kshatrapas (Sakas) of Malwa and Gujarat, issuing commemorative silver coins. His court was adorned by the **Navaratnas** (Nine Gems), including poet Kalidasa, astronomer Varahamihira, physician Dhanvantari, and lexicographer Amarasimha. Chinese Buddhist pilgrim **Faxian (Fa-Hien)** visited India during his reign, noting peace, vegetarianism, and the flourishing of Buddhism and Brahmanism.

## High-Yield UPPSC PCS Previous Year Practice Stems

#### Practice Question 1 (UPPSC Prelims 2020)
**Question:** Which among the following Harappan sites provides the earliest archaeological evidence of a ploughed field?
- (A) Harappa
- (B) Kalibangan
- (C) Lothal
- (D) Banawali
*Answer:* **(B)**. Kalibangan (in Hanumangarh district, Rajasthan) revealed furrow marks arranged in intersecting grids dating to the Early Harappan phase.

#### Practice Question 2 (UPPSC Prelims 2022)
**Question:** The famous *Prayag Prashasti* inscription in Allahabad was composed by which of the following court poets?
- (A) Banabhatta
- (B) Kalidasa
- (C) Harishena
- (D) Ravikirti
*Answer:* **(C)**. Harishena, the court poet and *Mahadandanayaka* of Samudragupta, inscribed the 33-line panegyric on an Ashokan sandstone pillar at Kausambi (later shifted to Allahabad).

#### Practice Question 3 (UPPSC Prelims 2023)
**Question:** In the context of ancient Indian history, the first royal land grants (*Brahmadeya*) with fiscal and administrative exemptions were issued by which dynasty?
- (A) Mauryas
- (B) Sungas
- (C) Satavahanas
- (D) Guptas
*Answer:* **(C)**. Inscriptional evidence from the Nanaghat inscription confirms that the Satavahanas initiated tax-exempt land grants to Buddhist monks and Brahmins.
