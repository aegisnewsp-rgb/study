# Elasticity

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.
Elasticity is the **percentage-response ratio** of one economic variable to another. For demand it is calculated as the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price — written PED = (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q), a dimensionless number because the units cancel out. The benchmark is **unity**: PED > 1 means elastic, PED < 1 means inelastic, PED = 1 means unit elastic. Two prelim questions are repeated almost every year: classify a good as a necessity, luxury, or inferior using **YED** (income elasticity), and identify substitutes vs complements using the **sign of XED** (positive = substitute, negative = complement). Also remember the **Total Revenue Test**: when PED > 1, a price cut raises total revenue, and vice versa.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Point vs Arc Formulas
At a single point the elasticity is **PED = (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q)**. Between two price-quantity observations use the average (mid-point) form so that moving from P1 to P2 gives the same value as moving back from P2 to P1:
**Arc PED = [(Q2−Q1)/((Q2+Q1)/2)] / [(P2−P1)/((P2+P1)/2)]**.
RBI Phase-II descriptive questions often require the arc version when two numerical data points are given.

#### Determinants — What Drives the Number
Five factors set the size of PED:

1. **Substitutes available** — more close substitutes, larger |PED|. Salt has few substitutes → inelastic; one brand of soap → highly elastic.
2. **Share of budget** — a 10% rise in the price of a car matters more than 10% on a matchbox.
3. **Necessity vs luxury** — insulin is inelastic, restaurant meals are elastic.
4. **Time horizon** — long-run elasticity exceeds short-run because consumers adjust habits.
5. **Market definition** — narrow (Coke) → elastic; broad (soft drinks) → less elastic.

PES follows its own list: time to produce, stored inventory, spare capacity, factor mobility, and perishability. Agricultural PES is inelastic in the short run because acreage cannot change between sowing seasons.

#### Total Revenue (TR) Test
TR = P × Q. Differentiating gives **MR = P [1 − 1/|Ed|]**, a formula you should derive on paper at least once: TR rises when Ed > 1 and a price cut is made, peaks at Ed = 1, and falls when Ed < 1. Examiners set numerics in which students must infer elasticity from the direction of change in a firm's reported revenue.

#### YED and XED Rules of Thumb
**YED > 1** luxury, **0 < YED < 1** necessity, **YED < 0** inferior. **XED > 0** substitute (tea–coffee), **XED < 0** complement (cars–petrol). A negative XED test is a frequent two-mark item.

#### Elasticity vs Slope
Slope (ΔP/ΔQ) is fixed along a straight-line demand curve, but **elasticity changes along the curve**: elastic in the upper half, **unit elastic exactly at the midpoint** (PED = 1), inelastic in the lower half. This distinction is the single most-tested quirk.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Micro-Mechanisms
A **perfectly elastic** schedule is a horizontal line (PED → ∞) — characteristic of a single firm under perfect competition facing many rivals. A **perfectly inelastic** schedule is vertical (PED = 0) — approximated by life-saving drugs such as insulin for an insulin-dependent diabetic. The monopolist always produces on the **elastic segment** of its demand curve, where MR > 0; producing on the inelastic segment would mean a price cut raises quantity but lowers revenue, and a price hike raises revenue, both contradicting profit maximisation.

#### Pass-Through and Incidence
When excise duty is raised, the share borne by consumers depends on |PED| relative to |PES|. With inelastic demand and elastic supply, the consumer pays most of the tax. This links elasticity directly to fiscal-policy questions on GST rate changes for cigarettes (inelastic demand) vs restaurant meals (elastic demand).

#### Long-Run vs Short-Run
Petrol demand is highly inelastic in the short run (commuters can't switch cars immediately) but clearly elastic in the long run as households buy EVs. Likewise, agricultural PES rises sharply over a multi-year horizon as land use shifts. Always state the **time horizon** whenever quoting an elasticity number.

#### Common Mistakes
- Treating elasticity as constant along a linear demand curve (it isn't — slope and elasticity are different concepts).
- Forgetting that YED and XED use the same percentage formula structure, only the denominator variable changes.
- Reporting a negative PED for a normal good (PED is conventionally reported as an absolute value for normal goods).
- Conflating elasticity with the slope in numerical questions; slope has units of rupees per unit, elasticity is unit-free.

#### Practice Prompts
1. Price of good X falls from ₹100 to ₹80 and quantity rises from 200 to 250 units. Compute arc PED and state whether revenue rises or falls.
2. YED of good A is 1.4 and good B is 0.3. Classify each and predict the effect on demand if real income falls by 10%.
