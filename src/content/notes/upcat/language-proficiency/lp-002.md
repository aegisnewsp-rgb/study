# Grammar in Context, Not in Isolation

The University of the Philippines names Language Proficiency as one of the four UPCAT subtests and tests it in English and Filipino. UP publishes no question count, no per-subtest duration and no weightage for it, so there is no official blueprint to revise against — only the shape of the subtest itself. What follows is a StudyRoadmap study map, not a UP syllabus.

The phrase "in context" carries the whole argument. A fill-in-the-blank item can be answered by pattern-matching the form of a word. A passage item cannot, because the sentence has to fit its neighbours: it has to refer to something the passage has already named, it has to keep the passage's time frame, and it has to leave the paragraph's meaning intact. Grammar competence in the exam sense is the ability to answer three questions about any sentence — who is acting, when, and what it points back to.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

Grammar items break into five axes, and each one has a cue that tells you it is the thing being tested.

| The axis | What breaks | The cue in the sentence | The fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subject–verb agreement | the verb does not match its subject in number | a phrase sits between the subject and the verb, hiding the real subject | find the subject, delete the phrase, then check the verb |
| Tense | the verb's time frame does not match the clause | "by the time", "since", "yesterday", "by tomorrow", "if" | read the frame of the main clause first, then place the verb inside it |
| Pronoun reference | the pronoun has no antecedent, or the wrong one | a pronoun that the passage has not set up | look back two sentences for the nearest plausible antecedent |
| Modifier placement | a phrase attaches to the wrong noun | a phrase that could describe two different nouns | rewrite so the modifier follows the word it describes |
| Parallelism and conjunctions | a series mixes forms, or a conjunction joins unlike things | "either … or", "both … and", "not only … but also" | make every item in the series match grammatically |

**The scan order.** Read the sentence once for meaning. Find the verb. Find the subject — delete anything between them that starts with a preposition. Then walk the five axes above in a fixed order: agreement, tense, pronoun, modifier, parallelism. Then read the corrected sentence again, all the way through. An item usually breaks exactly one axis, so a fixed scan finds the break instead of hunting for it. The order matters because agreement is the most common break and the easiest to fix wrongly.

**The read-aloud test.** Read the candidate sentence aloud before you accept it. A missing comma between two clauses, a verb that lost its agreement, a word order that needs to be unscrambled — all three sound wrong when spoken, and none of them look wrong on a page. This is the fastest way to catch a run-on and the only reliable way to catch a word order that is technically grammatical but reads badly.

**What to memorise.**

- The real subject is often not the first noun: "The list of approved suppliers **is** on the portal", "Each of the applicants **has** paid."
- "The number of X **is**" and "a number of X **are**" — one takes a singular verb, the other a plural.
- "Neither … nor …" takes a verb matching the nearer subject.
- Present perfect for a period with no end point: "since 2019", "for three years".
- Past perfect for the earlier of two past events: "by the time we arrived, it **had** closed."
- Objective case after a preposition: "between you and **me**", "with **him**".
- A modifier must follow the noun it describes; if it can attach to two nouns, it is misplaced.
- "Only" changes meaning by moving: "only after", "only the manager", "the manager only".

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Subject–verb agreement: the subject is not always nearby

The most common agreement item puts a long phrase between the subject and the verb to trap you into reading the nearest noun as the subject.

> The list of approved suppliers **is** on the portal.
> The supplier of approved lists **are** on the portal.

In the first sentence the subject is *list*, singular. In the second it is *supplier*, also singular. The prepositional phrase is never the subject.

The same trick runs through several forms at once.

> **Each** of the students **has** submitted the form.
> **Neither** of the two answers **is** correct.
> **Neither the manager nor the assistants are** present.

*Each*, *every*, *either*, *neither* and *one of* take a singular verb even when the noun inside the phrase is plural. *Neither … nor …* takes a verb that matches the nearer subject, so *neither the manager nor the assistants are present* is right while *neither the assistants nor the manager are present* is not.

Two more distinctions are worth learning cold. **The number of** applicants is rising — singular, because *number* is the subject. **A number of** applicants are rising — plural, because *applicants* is. And a clause acting as a subject takes a singular verb: *What the committee needs **is** more time*, not *need*.

#### Tense: find the frame, then place the verb

Read the time marker first, then the verb.

| Marker | Tense it calls for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| since, for, already, yet | present perfect | She **has lived** in Cebu since 2019. |
| yesterday, last week, in 2019 | past simple | The council **approved** the budget last week. |
| by the time, before, after + past | past perfect for the earlier event | By the time we arrived, the doors **had closed**. |
| by tomorrow, next month | present perfect for the future | I **will have submitted** it by Friday. |
| if + past simple | present simple result, would for the unreal part | If she **were** late, she **would miss** the ferry. |
| reported speech after a past reporting verb | tense shifts back one step | He said that he **was** tired. |

Inside a passage, the paragraph's own frame governs. A paragraph written in past tense that switches to present for a general truth is a deliberate distractor; the passage's frame wins, and the switch is the error the item is pointing at.

#### Pronoun reference: the referent has to exist

A pronoun is only correct if the passage has already given it something to point at, and if the number and role match.

> The nurse told the patients **that she** should rest.

That sentence is defective, not because of grammar but because the reader cannot tell whether *she* is the nurse or a patient. The repair names the referent: *the nurse told the patients **that the nurse** should rest.*

The classic item type is a sentence-combination choice whose pronoun refers to something introduced only inside the option. That option has lost its antecedent and is wrong even though its grammar is perfect. The other common failure is the case error: a possessive for a possessor (*the book**'s** cover*), a subject case where an object case belongs (*between you and **I***), and *who**'s** used for *who is* and *whose* used for the possessor.

#### Modifier placement: the phrase attaches to the wrong noun

The textbook error is the dangling modifier, where the phrase has no subject to attach to:

> Walking to school, the students were soaked by the rain.

The sentence says the students were soaked, so the walking has to belong to them. The repair moves the phrase into a subordinate clause: *While they walked to school, the students were soaked by the rain.*

The **rewrite test** handles every modifier item. Put the modifier in a short sentence with only one noun and ask which noun it must describe. "The report by the analyst with the highest degree was released" fails the test twice over, because the phrase could belong to the report or to the analyst. Rewrite to disambiguate: *The analyst who had the highest degree wrote the report.*

Two smaller cases reward marks. **"Only"** changes meaning by position — *She only ate the soup*, *She ate only the soup* and *She ate the soup only* are three different claims. And a **squinting modifier** reads differently depending on which noun it clings to: *the book he borrowed yesterday* could mean the book was borrowed yesterday or that he borrowed yesterday.

#### Parallelism, conjunctions and the connective test

A series must match in grammatical form. *The course covers anatomy, how to run a lab, and basic chemistry* has one noun phrase, one infinitive phrase and one noun phrase. Either the whole series becomes noun phrases or the whole series becomes verb phrases.

Correlative conjunctions must also match: *either … or*, *both … and*, *not only … but also*, *whether … or*, *neither … nor*. And the two redundant pairs are frequent errors: *Although it rained, **but** we played* is wrong (keep *Although* and drop *but*), and *Because he was late, **so** he left* is wrong (keep *Because* and drop *so*).

#### Why context changes the answer

Everything above works the same in a fill-in-the-blank item and in a passage item, except the passage adds three constraints: the referent must already exist in the text, the tense must fit the frame the paragraph established, and the meaning of the paragraph must survive the change. A repair that improves the grammar but changes who did what, or when, is not a repair. That is the whole reason items are built from passages — to separate candidates who can spot an error from candidates who can fix one.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Worked item 1 — the pronoun that nobody owns

**Passage.** The inspection team arrived at the plant at nine in the morning. The manager had already briefed the supervisors on the new safety rules. Because the weather had turned, the walkthrough was postponed to the following week.

**Item.** The third sentence is grammatical but passive, and the passage never says who postponed the walkthrough. Which revision is best?

- **A.** Because the weather had turned, **it** decided to postpone the walkthrough to the following week.
- **B.** Because the weather had turned, **they** decided to postpone the walkthrough to the following week.
- **C.** Because the weather had turned, **the manager** decided to postpone the walkthrough to the following week.
- **D.** Because the weather had turned, **the supervisors** decided to postpone the walkthrough to the following week.

**Step 1 — list the available antecedents.** The passage names the inspection team, the manager, the supervisors, the new safety rules and the weather. Five possible referents, and "they" fits several of them.

**Step 2 — ask who can postpone a walkthrough.** A supervisor can request a postponement; the manager can decide one; the weather cannot decide anything. That immediately eliminates A.

**Step 3 — test the plural options.** B and D both use a plural pronoun, but the passage never establishes a plural decision-maker for this action. B is also plain wrong on the facts: the team arrived, the manager briefed, and nobody has been named as deciding.

**Step 4 — check what the passage supports.** The manager is the only individual the passage has put in a position of authority over the walkthrough. C names the referent, keeps the past perfect "had turned" and keeps the causative clause intact.

**Answer: C.**

> **Trap.** Choosing B because "they" sounds like the natural English pronoun for a group. An item is testing exactly that reflex. The pronoun has to point at a specific actor the passage has established, and where two readings are possible, the repair that names the referent wins.

#### Worked item 2 — the modifier that has no one to describe

**Item.** Which sentence repairs the dangling modifier in: *Covering the south wing during the renovation, the notices were posted by the wardens.*

- **A.** Covering the south wing during the renovation, **the wardens posted the notices**.
- **B.** Covering the south wing during the renovation, **the notices were posted by the wardens**.
- **C.** **While the wardens covered the south wing** during the renovation, the notices were posted.
- **D.** The notices, **covering the south wing during the renovation**, were posted by the wardens.

**Step 1 — find the intended actor.** The phrase "covering the south wing" describes a person doing a job. The wardens are the only people in the sentence.

**Step 2 — apply the rewrite test.** Put the phrase next to one noun: it attaches to *wardens*, not to *notices*. Notices do not cover wings.

**Step 3 — read the options.** A attaches the phrase to the wardens and is correct, but it changes the structure from passive to active, so the option is doing two things at once. B leaves the dangling modifier in place. D attaches the phrase to the notices, which is exactly the wrong noun. C puts the phrase in a subordinate clause with the wardens as its subject, so nothing dangles.

**Step 4 — choose on the minimal change.** C is the option that fixes the error without relocating anything else in the sentence.

**Answer: C.**

> **Trap.** Picking A because it "sounds better". Both A and C are grammatical; the item is scored on the repair that keeps the sentence's meaning and structure intact. An editing choice is not always the most elegant version — it is the version that changes the least while removing the fault.

#### The editing item: precision and economy

Editing items present two versions of a sentence and ask which is better. Four criteria decide, in this order: **referent continuity**, **parallel structure**, **precision**, **economy**.

> Passage: The researchers collected samples from each site. The samples were analysed in the laboratory. The results showed a steady decline.
>
> **Version A.** The samples were analysed in the laboratory, and the results showed a steady decline.
> **Version B.** They were analysed in the laboratory, and the results showed a steady decline.

Version A wins on referent continuity. In B, "they" has three candidates in the immediately preceding sentences — the researchers, the samples and the results — and the reader has to guess. Version A also removes choppiness by joining two independent clauses with a comma and a coordinating conjunction, which is a comma splice only when no conjunction appears. Version B is shorter by one word and still loses.

#### The diagnostic table for a broken sentence

| The error | The signal | The repair |
|---|---|---|
| Agreement | a long prepositional phrase between subject and verb | delete the phrase, then match the verb to the subject |
| Tense | a time marker that disagrees with the verb | read the main clause's frame, then fit the verb to it |
| Unclear pronoun | two or more plausible antecedents | name the referent |
| Lost antecedent | the pronoun refers to something only in the answer option | the option is wrong; find one that names it |
| Dangling modifier | the phrase describes an action with no matching subject | put the phrase in a subordinate clause |
| Squinting modifier | the phrase can attach to two nouns | rewrite to make the attachment explicit |
| Broken parallelism | a series mixing noun phrases and verb phrases | make every item in the series match |
| Redundant conjunction | "although … but", "because … so" | keep one half of the pair |
| Comma splice | two independent clauses joined by a comma alone | use a semicolon, a conjunction, or a full stop |

#### The Filipino half of the subtest

UP tests Language Proficiency in English and Filipino, and Filipino grammar breaks the habits the English half trains.

The single biggest difference is that the Filipino verb carries **aspect**, not tense, and it marks the actor through its affix. The `mag-` form is the one a dictionary lists (*magsulat*, to write); `nag-` marks a past or ongoing action (*nagsulat*, wrote or writes); `sum-` marks a completed action (*sumulat*, wrote); `naka-` marks a resulting state (*nakaupo*, is seated); `ma-` adds ability or likeness (*magaling*, excellent). Nothing on the verb changes for number — *maglalaro si Ana* and *maglalaro sila* are both correct — so English subject–verb agreement simply does not transfer.

Case marking runs on the noun phrase instead. `ang` marks a common noun as the subject, `ng` marks a common noun in another role, `si` marks a personal name as the subject and `ni` marks a personal name in a non-subject role. That is what makes the same English sentence have two Filipino forms:

> Sumulat si Ana ng lihid. — Ana wrote a letter.
> Isinulat ni Ana ang lihid. — The letter was written by Ana.

The second version treats the letter as the subject and the writer as the agent, so the affix on the verb and the markers on the nouns both change. A student who translates word by word from English marks the letter with `ang` and Ana with `ni` and produces the object-focus version when the passage is in the actor focus — the two participants stay the same, but the topic of the sentence has swapped, and the item is lost.

There is one more Filipino feature English lacks: the enclitic pronouns `ko`, `mo` and `siya` attach to the verb and restore an argument the affix left out. *Maglalaro ko* is "I will play", with the `ko` riding on the verb as the "I", and the full form *ako* can stand on its own. A Filipino item frequently tests whether the pronoun's number and role fit the affix it sits on.

#### Common mistakes

Fixing a pronoun by guessing the intended referent from the sentence's sense instead of from the passage. The passage decides.

Choosing an active-voice rewrite because it reads better. Editing items score the smallest change that removes the fault.

Treating a dangling modifier as a missing comma. It is a structural error about who did what, and no comma repairs it.

Applying English agreement to a Filipino verb. There is none, and looking for it costs you time on every Filipino item.

Assuming a repaired sentence has to be longer. The repair that removes three words and fixes the fault beats the one that adds a clause.

UP does not release UPCAT papers with answers, so the entries below describe item types rather than reproduce real items.

1. **Sentence repair from a passage:** one underlined sentence with four options, each breaking a different grammar axis.
2. **Modifier placement:** an opening phrase attached to the wrong noun, with the repair that puts it back.
3. **Pronoun reference:** a pronoun whose antecedent the passage never establishes, or establishes as two things.
4. **Tense consistency:** a verb whose frame conflicts with the paragraph's own time frame.
5. **Editing or sentence combination:** two versions of a passage, where referent continuity and economy decide.

### 💡 Pro Tips

- Circle the verb before you read the options; most items break in one of five places and all five are near the verb.
- Cross out the prepositional phrase when you hunt for the subject — it is never the subject itself.
- Say the corrected sentence aloud before you commit to it.
- When two readings of a pronoun are possible, assume the item wants the one the passage set up.
- On Filipino items, identify the aspect and the actor before reading for anything else.
- Keep a list of the four or five sentences you got wrong and re-read them on the morning of the test.
