# Idioms and Phrases

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your NAT-I test.

An idiom is a **fixed multi-word expression** whose figurative meaning is conventional and cannot be deduced from its literal words — "kick the bucket" means *to die*, not to strike a pail. NAT-I Verbal Reasoning places idioms under the 3% Verbal section and in standard papers asks 5–10 MCQs requiring either the meaning of a printed idiom or the idiom that best fits a sentence.

- **Meaning-type item**: four options, pick the closest meaning of the bold idiom.
- **Fill-in item**: a sentence with a blank, choose the idiom that fits semantically.
- **Substitution-type**: replace the underlined phrase with a synonymous idiom.

Mastery comes from rote memorisation of roughly 300–500 common English idioms and their standard meanings.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### What counts as an idiom

An idiom is a **conventionalised figurative expression** whose whole-unit meaning differs from the sum of its parts. The figure is fixed in form (word order, vocabulary, in many papers grammar), so substituting a near-synonym in standard papers destroys it. Origins trace to cricket ("hat-trick", "stumped"), the navy ("batten down the hatches"), the Bible ("the writing on the wall"), and folk sayings ("a stitch in time saves nine").

#### How NAT-I frames the question

The Verbal Reasoning section delivers idioms through three formats: *meaning selection* (find the meaning of the given idiom), *contextual insertion* (which idiom fills the blank), and *one-word substitution* style items where an idiom is paraphrased. All are four-option MCQs with no negative marking.

#### Distinguishing idioms from look-alikes

A **proverb** offers explicit advice ("a stitch in time saves nine"); an **idiom** is a figurative chunk embedded in a sentence. A **colloquialism** is informal register, not necessarily figurative. Many idioms began as metaphors and fossilised, which is why literal translation fails.

| Concept | Key point |
| --- | --- |
| Idiom (e.g., "spill the beans") | Fixed form; figurative meaning = "reveal a secret". |
| Literal reading ("spill the beans") | Physically empty a container of legumes — wrong sense. |
| Proverbs ("strike while the iron is hot") | Standalone sayings, in many papers advisory. |
| Colloquialisms ("gonna", "guy") | Informal vocabulary, not figurative. |

#### High-yield categories for NAT-I

- Body parts: "cold feet", "twist one's arm", "lend a hand".
- Animals: "let the cat out of the bag", "kill two birds with one stone".
- Time/nature: "once in a blue moon", "under the weather", "calm before the storm".
- Money/work: "break the bank", "burn the midnight oil", "back to the drawing board".

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge cases examiners exploit

Some idioms carry **both literal and figurative senses** ("break the ice" can describe ships in polar waters or social introductions), and NAT-I items always rely on the figurative reading. Others are **grammatically locked**: "by and large" cannot become "by and largely". A few idioms are **opaque to non-native speakers** because the metaphor is dead (e.g., "raking in money" — the rake is a 17th-century hay tool).

#### Common traps in the options

- **Partial-truth distractors**: an option captures *one* word of the idiom's sense (e.g., for "at one's own expense", choosing "at great expense" instead of "at personal cost/disadvantage").
- **Sound-alike pairs**: "beat around the bush" (hesitate) vs. "hit the nail on the head" (be precise); "once in a blue moon" (rarely) vs. "once upon a time" (narrative opener).
- **Grammatically correct but semantically wrong**: an idiom fits the slot's grammar but reverses or weakens the intended meaning.

#### Worked micro-example

Sentence: *"Despite the looming deadline, Raza decided to ______ and submitted the report a week late."* The correct idiom is "**bite the bullet**" (face an unpleasant task with resolve) — not "**bite the dust**" (be defeated) or "**bite off more than he can chew**" (take on too much). The exam tests whether the candidate reads the *resolve* cue, not just the word "bite".

#### Strategy on exam day

NAT-I Verbal sits inside a 120-minute paper with ~20 MCQs in the Verbal block; budget **45–60 seconds per idiom item**. Eliminate two options by meaning, then verify the survivor against the sentence's tone (positive/negative/formal).

| Mistake | Why it costs marks | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Literal translation | "Break the ice" ≠ shatter ice | Learn the figurative meaning as a unit |
| Confusing near-pairs | Two idioms, opposite senses | Memorise each separately with a cue sentence |
| Ignoring fixed form | Rewriting the idiom changes it | Treat the phrase as one vocabulary chunk |
| Guessing from grammar alone | Sentence can read smoothly but mean nothing | Match meaning first, grammar second |

#### Practice prompts

1. Choose the idiom that best fills the blank: *"After the long negotiation, both parties decided to ______ and split the profit equally."* (Answer key: "bury the hatchet" / "split the difference".)
2. Identify the meaning of "**to go the extra mile**": (a) travel further, (b) make extra effort, (c) spend more money, (d) take a detour.
