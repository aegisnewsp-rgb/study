# Synthetic Geometry and Constructions — Leaving Certificate (Established) Mathematics Notes

Sections 2.1 and 2.2 test two different things under one heading. Synthetic geometry is *proof*: given a diagram with markings, show that a statement must be true, using named results. Constructions are *procedure*: produce a required point or line with straightedge and compass, and justify what you have produced. Marks are lost in both for the same reason — doing the thing without naming the theorem that makes it legal.

---

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### The results you need by name

A proof that cites "by the third theorem" or names a theorem the marking scheme does not recognise earns nothing, however correct the argument. Learn the exact names.

**On parallel lines.** Corresponding, alternate and co-interior (allied) angles are equal. The converse holds: if a pair is equal, the lines are parallel. The co-interior angles are supplementary, and the converse of that is the test you will actually use.

**Angles.** The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference standing on the same arc. The converse: if the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference, the centre of the circle is correct.

**Angles in a triangle.** Angles sum to 180°. The exterior angle equals the sum of the two opposite interior angles — a favourite, because it lets you name a missing angle from a diagram with no computation.

**Isosceles.** Base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal, and the converse. The angle between two equal chords subtends an isosceles triangle, so equal chords subtend equal angles — the base angle result applied to the circle.

**Polygons.** Interior angles sum to (*n* − 2)×180°; each interior angle of a regular *n*-gon is (*n* − 2)×180° ÷ *n*. Exterior angles sum to 360°. A statement about a polygon usually turns on recognising which of these three applies.

**Circle theorems.** The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference. Angles in the same segment are equal. Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral sum to 180°. A tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact, and the angle between a tangent and a chord equals the angle in the alternate segment.

**Triangles.** Pythagoras. The angle between two tangents from an external point is 180° minus the central angle subtended by the points of contact.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Writing a proof

Use the shape the marking schemes reward.

1. **Given** — the markings already on the diagram, stated as facts.
2. **To prove** — the statement, written before you start reasoning, so you are not arguing towards it.
3. **Reasoning** — a chain where every step names its theorem and its subject.

Each step reads: *[subject] [the theorem name] [what it follows]*. Example: *"angle ABC = angle BAC (base angles of an isosceles triangle), so BC = AC (sides opposite equal angles are equal)."* Two marks, two named results, nothing left to the examiner's inference.

Work backwards from what you must prove to what you are given, then reverse. Most candidates reason forwards from the givens and run out of road.


#### Constructions

Every construction question has two parts and they are marked separately: **do it**, then **justify it**. A correct drawing with no justification earns roughly half. The justification states which pair of arcs or lines produced equal lengths or angles, and which theorem follows.

The constructions you need:

**Angle bisector.** From a vertex, arc to cut both arms at two points. From those two points, equal arcs intersecting inside the angle. Join the vertex to the crossing point. Justification: the two radii used are equal, making an isosceles triangle, so the line from the vertex bisects the angle.

**Perpendicular bisector of a line segment.** Equal arcs from both endpoints, above and below, meeting at two points. Join those two points. Justification: the crossing point is equidistant from both ends, so it lies on the bisector; two such points fix the line.

**Perpendicular from a point to a line.** Equal-radius arcs from the point and from a point on the line, then the same bisector procedure. Justification as above.

**Bisector of a line and an angle** (equal perpendicular distances), **tangent to a circle at a point on it** (join the centre to the point, construct the perpendicular, extend), and **parallel to a given line through a point** (copy the angle, or construct equal corresponding angles).

The examiner wants the construction set out in words as well as drawn. Name the arcs, name the intersection points, and name the theorem — a labelled sketch alone is not the full answer.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Where marks concentrate

- Proving two triangles are congruent requires stating the matching pairs and the criterion — SAS, SSS, ASA, RHS — by name. Listing "angle, side, angle" without naming the criterion loses the identifying mark.
- Showing two triangles are similar needs the criterion and a stated scale factor, with the corresponding sides identified.
- Proving a line is parallel needs a named angle relationship, not a statement that the angles "look equal".
- Constructions on a circle usually need the tangent theorem; do not prove perpendicularity by arithmetic when a named theorem does it.

## Self-check

1. What must a construction answer contain besides the drawing? *(A justification: which arcs or lines were used, what equality they produced, and which named theorem follows from it. Drawing alone forfeits the justification marks.)*
2. Angles at the centre are 100°. What is the angle at the circumference standing on the same arc? *(50°. The centre angle is twice the circumference angle on the same arc. The converse direction gives a centre angle of 200° as a reflex angle, which is why the minor arc matters.)*
3. You are given two equal chords in a circle. What can you conclude, and by what? *(The angles subtended at the circumference by equal chords are equal, because equal chords subtend equal angles at the centre, and the centre angle is twice the circumference angle.)*
4. Why do candidates lose the lead-in mark on a congruence proof? *(Because they describe the matching pairs without naming the criterion. SAS, SSS, ASA and RHS are named in the marking scheme; "angle, side, angle" is not the same entry.)*
5. A polygon has an interior angle of 140°. How do you tell a regular polygon from a general one in your reasoning? *(Check both. The sum of interior angles is (*n* − 2)×180°, and if the polygon is regular each interior angle is that sum ÷ *n*. If 140° is the *only* interior angle given, the polygon is not established as regular and you cannot divide by *n* without justification.)*
