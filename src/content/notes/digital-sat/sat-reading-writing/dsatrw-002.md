# Craft and Structure: Text Structure and Purpose — Digital SAT Reading and Writing Notes

Text Structure and Purpose questions test your ability to explain how a text is constructed and why the author wrote a specific sentence or entire passage. Rather than asking what the text says, these questions test rhetorical function: does a sentence introduce an anomaly, qualify an earlier claim, propose an alternative mechanism, or present a piece of experimental evidence?

> Review official Bluebook interface tools, including highlighting and text-line annotation, directly on https://satsuite.collegeboard.org/sat before test day.

---

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Question stems**: Look for phrasing such as "Which choice best describes the main purpose of the text?" or "Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?"
- **Rhetorical verbs**: Answer choices always begin with functional verbs: *introduce*, *criticize*, *exemplify*, *concede*, *resolve*, *corroborate*, *reconcile*.
- **Passage architecture**: Most Digital SAT passages follow a two-part or three-part structure: claim followed by counter-claim, observation followed by explanation, or puzzle followed by resolution.
- **Underlined sentence focus**: When a specific sentence is underlined, read the entire passage to see how that sentence connects to the preceding premise and the following conclusion.
- **Scope control**: Foil answers often accurately describe the content of the sentence but state the wrong structural function in relation to the whole passage.

#### Examiner traps

- Choosing an option because it states a factual claim made in the passage, even though it completely fails to identify the *purpose* or rhetorical objective of that claim.
- Confusing a claim made by the author with a claim made by an opponent whom the author is quoting merely to refute.
- Overlooking pivot words such as *nevertheless*, *yet*, *in contrast*, which signal a shift in the primary structural purpose of the text.
- Confusing an example with the general rule that the example illustrates.

#### 20-minute triage checklist

- [ ] Can you mentally map every passage as a sequence of functional blocks (for example: [Background] -> [Complication] -> [Proposed Mechanism])?
- [ ] Do you pay close attention to the opening verb of each answer choice?
- [ ] Have you verified whether the author takes a neutral, critical, or supportive stance towards the subject matter?

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Common passage structural archetypes

The College Board relies on a small set of recurring structural patterns in short informational texts:

1. **The Corrected Misconception**:
   - Sentence 1: Traditional belief or common assumption stated.
   - Sentence 2: Evidence revealing an inconsistency or flaw in that belief.
   - Sentence 3: New perspective or revised scientific consensus introduced.
   - *Primary Purpose*: To challenge an established view and suggest a revised understanding.

2. **The Empirical Puzzle and Hypothesis**:
   - Sentence 1: Description of an unusual observation or biological trait.
   - Sentence 2: Attempted explanations that failed or proved inadequate.
   - Sentence 3: A newly tested hypothesis that successfully accounts for the phenomenon.
   - *Primary Purpose*: To present a biological or historical anomaly and explain how recent research resolves it.

3. **The Qualified Defense**:
   - Sentence 1: Acknowledgment of legitimate criticisms leveled against a methodology or theory.
   - Sentence 2: Demonstration that under specific conditions, the theory retains predictive utility.
   - *Primary Purpose*: To defend a criticized concept by delimiting its scope rather than claiming universal applicability.

#### Worked test example

**Passage**:
In 1934, archaeologist Dorothy Garrod excavated the site of Tabun Cave in Mount Carmel, uncovering Neanderthal skeletal remains that she dated to roughly 40,000 years ago based on stratigraphy. For decades, this dating anchored theories proposing that Neanderthals and anatomically modern humans occupied the Levant in sequential waves. **However, recent thermoluminescence dating of burned flints from the same geological layer indicates that the Tabun deposits are in fact between 120,000 and 170,000 years old.** This chronological recalibration has forced paleoanthropologists to abandon simple replacement models and consider prolonged coexistence.

**Question**:
Which choice best describes the function of the bolded sentence in the text as a whole?
- (A) It disproves the claim that Neanderthals inhabited the Tabun Cave site.
- (B) It introduces revised chronological evidence that overturns an earlier historical model.
- (C) It criticizes Dorothy Garrod for using unreliable excavation methodologies.
- (D) It provides experimental proof that modern humans originated earlier than previously thought.

**Step-by-step solution**:
- **Analyze sentence context**: The bolded sentence begins with the contrast transition *However* and introduces thermoluminescence dating showing the site is 120,000 to 170,000 years old, far older than Garrod's 40,000-year estimate. The following sentence states that this has forced scientists to abandon earlier models.
- **Evaluate options**:
  - *(A)*: The sentence does not disprove Neanderthal habitation; it updates their timeline. Incorrect.
  - *(B)*: It introduces new dating evidence (120,000-170,000 years old) that overturned the earlier sequential model. Accurately describes structural function.
  - *(C)*: No personal criticism of Garrod is made; dating technologies simply improved. Incorrect.
  - *(D)*: The focus is Neanderthal chronology in the Levant, not the origin date of modern humans. Incorrect.
- **Correct Selection**: **(B)**.

### 🔴 Deep Dive — Mastery & Edge Cases (3mo–2yr)

#### Distinguishing nuanced rhetorical verbs

In the harder adaptive module, choices often differentiate subtle shades of authorial intent. Understanding the exact threshold for each verb is essential:

- **Advocate vs Describe**: *Advocate* requires the author to actively urge a policy or course of action; *describe* requires only detached explanation. If the passage does not say "scientists must adopt X", do not pick *advocate*.
- **Reconcile vs Compare**: *Reconcile* requires resolving a contradiction or demonstrating that two apparently conflicting views can coexist; *compare* merely notes similarities and differences.
- **Concede vs Refute**: *Concede* means admitting the validity of an opponent's point; *refute* means proving that the opponent's point is false.
