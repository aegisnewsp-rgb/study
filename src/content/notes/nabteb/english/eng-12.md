# Idioms and Figurative Expressions

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

An **idiom** is a fixed expression whose meaning cannot be inferred from its individual words — "kick the bucket" means *to die*, not to physically strike a pail. **Figurative expression** is the umbrella term covering idioms plus similes, metaphors, personification, hyperbole, and euphemisms. NABTEB tests your ability to read the intended (non-literal) sense of such expressions inside sentences and passages.

- **Simile** uses *like* or *as* (e.g. "as brave as a lion").
- **Metaphor** states the comparison directly (e.g. "time is money").
- **Hyperbole** exaggerates deliberately (e.g. "I am starving to death").
- **Euphemism** softens harsh realities (e.g. "passed away" for "died").

| Figure | Marker words | NABTEB cue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simile | like, as | Direct comparison |
| Metaphor | none (stated) | "is / becomes / = " |
| Hyperbole | very, million | Deliberate exaggeration |
| Personification | verb of action | Human trait to non-human |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Definitions

NABTEB questions cluster around six categories. **Idioms** are conventional, fixed phrases whose meaning is stored as a whole unit in the mental lexicon. **Proverbs** look similar but state a general truth or moral (e.g. "a stitch in time saves nine"), so they are not interchangeable with idioms. **Similes** and **metaphors** both compare two unlike things; similes keep the comparison visible through *like* or *as*, while metaphors fuse the two. **Personification** assigns human behaviour to objects or abstract ideas, and **hyperbole** stretches quantity or intensity beyond fact for rhetorical force. A **euphemism** replaces a blunt word with a polite alternative — relevant in passages dealing with death, dismissal, or bodily functions.

#### How NABTEB Tests Them

The exam in most keys underlines an idiom or figure inside a sentence and offers four options: the literal meaning (a distractor), a close-but-wrong meaning, a contextual meaning, and the correct idiomatic sense. Candidates who decode the phrase word-by-word fall for the literal distractor. Always read the surrounding sentence for **context clues** — words like *secretly*, *reluctantly*, or *immediately* in many papers signal which shade of meaning is intended.

| Task type | Skill required | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Explain underlined idiom | Idiomatic interpretation | Don't pick the literal option |
| Choose nearest meaning | Synonym substitution | Paraphrase, do not translate |
| Identify figure of speech | Pattern recognition | Simile needs *like* / *as* |
| Fill-in-the-blank cloze | Context-driven choice | Match register, not just topic |

- A simile must contain *like* or *as* — "She is a rose" is a metaphor, not a simile.
- Idioms are culture-specific: "to cut coat according to size" reflects Nigerian context.
- Register matters: "kicked the bucket" is informal; "passed away" is formal.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Mechanics

Some expressions sit on the border between categories. "Spill the beans" is an idiom; "let the cat out of the bag" is also an idiom but with a near-identical meaning — exam setters can swap them to test whether you recognise *meaning equivalence* rather than word identity. Mixed figures are common: "the news spread like wildfire" is a simile wrapped around an idiom. Always identify the *primary* device the examiner highlights. **Denotation** (dictionary meaning) and **connotation** (associated feeling) also surface in NABTEB comprehension — a question can ask why a writer chose "passed away" instead of "died", testing connotative awareness.

#### Common Mistakes in NABTEB Scripts

1. Translating idioms word-for-word into Pidgin or a local language, which destroys the idiomatic sense.
2. Selecting the most common meaning of a polysemous idiom when the passage demands a secondary sense.
3. Labelling any exaggeration as hyperbole, ignoring that the phrase can be a metaphor ("my teacher is a giant").
4. Treating proverbs and idioms as synonyms in fill-in tasks.

#### Worked Micro-Example

> Sentence: *"After months of delay, the contractor finally bit the bullet and resumed work."*

| Option | Choice | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. Literally bit a bullet | Distractor — literal trap |
| B. Made a difficult decision bravely | Correct — idiom means facing something unpleasant head-on |
| C. Injured himself on site | Distractor — context misread |
| D. Stopped work permanently | Distractor — wrong sense of the idiom |

#### Practice Prompts

1. In "The protest was a storm that swept the city", name the dominant figure and justify why it is not a simile.
2. Replace "He gave up the ghost" with a euphemistic alternative and explain the register shift.

> Exam tip: underline the *signal words* (*like*, *as*, "is", exaggerated numerals) before reading the options — this narrows the figure in under 10 seconds and prevents literal-distractor errors.
