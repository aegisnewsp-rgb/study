# The PICO Framework

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> A four-part question frame, not a study design — used at the desk before you open PubMed.

PICO is the workhorse mnemonic of evidence-based Family Medicine. It takes a fuzzy bedside uncertainty and turns it into a single sentence that PubMed, Cochrane, and UpToDate can actually search. Four components, every one named, no abbreviations left implicit.

- **P — Patient/Problem:** the population, setting, and clinical context (e.g., "adult men aged 40–65 with newly diagnosed essential hypertension in primary care").
- **I — Intervention:** the exposure, therapy, or test you want to evaluate (drug, procedure, screening tool, counselling strategy).
- **C — Comparison:** the alternative against which I is judged (placebo, usual care, gold-standard test, no screening).
- **O — Outcome:** the measurable endpoint with a defined time horizon (e.g., "10-year cardiovascular mortality at 0.05 absolute risk reduction").

| Element | What it must specify | Common omission |
|---|---|---|
| **P** | Age, sex, comorbidities | Vague "patient" |
| **I** | Exact modality, dose, frequency | Drug class only |
| **C** | Active alternative, not absence | Implicit "no treatment" |
| **O** | Variable + scale + follow-up duration | Mortality with no horizon |

> 💡 **Memory Hook (PICO):** "**P**opcorn **I**n a **C**lean **O**ven" — Patient, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, in that exact order on the page.

In the Saudi GP Board blueprint, PICO sits inside the 3% Evidence-Based Practice & Critical Appraisal slot. Expect a clinical vignette followed by one of three asks: name the missing element, classify the question type, or pick the study design that fits the finished PICO.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

> Pair the four letters with a question type, and the literature search runs itself.

#### Question Types That Reshape the Frame

A PICO sentence is only as useful as the question type it wraps. Family Medicine candidates confuse therapy, diagnosis, prognosis, and etiology questions constantly, then wonder why the wrong study design gets selected.

- **Therapy question:** P + I + C + O. Best answered by **RCT** or systematic review of RCTs. Most GP Board stems are therapy questions.
- **Diagnosis question:** P + I (the new test) + C (reference standard) + O (sensitivity/specificity/LR). Best answered by **cross-sectional diagnostic accuracy study**.
- **Prognosis question:** P + I (the exposure/condition) + O (future event over time) + C (optional comparator cohort). Best answered by **cohort study**.
- **Etiology/Harm question:** P + I (exposure) + C (unexposed) + O (harm event). Best answered by **cohort or case-control**, never RCT when harm is the question.

> 📌 **Formula Check:** PICO produces a *question*, not a statistic. The "output" of PICO is a search string with MeSH terms, not a number with units.

#### Comparing PICO Against Its Siblings

Students often add letters that the original mnemonic does not require. Know which extension fits which scenario.

| Mnemonic | Extra element | When to add it | When to skip it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PICO** | — | Standard therapy/diagnosis/prognosis stems | When all four core elements are already explicit |
| **PICO(T)** | **T** = Timeframe | Prognosis or follow-up-heavy therapy questions | Acute, single-visit decisions |
| **PICOS** | **S** = Study type | Systematic review screening or methodological filters | General bedside searching |
| **PICOTS** | **T** + **S** | Protocol registration, PROSPERO entries | Routine board-style MCQs |

#### Common Pitfalls in Question Formulation

A weak PICO produces a noisy search. Watch for these recurring defects when appraising a peer's question or auditing your own.

- The **P** describes a disease but omits the setting (primary care vs tertiary), which changes which evidence applies.
- The **I** uses a brand name rather than a generic class, narrowing results to sponsored trials.
- The **C** is missing entirely; "no comparison" silently forces a single-arm search and excludes the strongest studies.
- The **O** is a surrogate (LDL reduction) rather than a patient-important endpoint (MI, death, QoL).
- The question is a **background** question ("What is diabetes?") rather than a **foreground** one; background questions belong in textbooks, not in PICO.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Candidates who default to "RCT" for every PICO lose marks on prognosis and etiology stems — prognosis demands a cohort, and asking an RCT to prove harm is unethical. The question type, not the verb in the stem, dictates the design.

#### Worked Example — Building a Board-Style PICO

**Stem.** A 45-year-old woman with no past medical history asks whether daily low-dose aspirin would prevent a first heart attack. Her older brother had an MI at 52. She has no dyspepsia.

1. **Question type:** Therapy (primary prevention of cardiovascular events).
2. **P:** Asymptomatic adult woman, age 40–59, no prior CVD, average risk, primary care setting.
3. **I:** Low-dose aspirin (75–100 mg daily), started now, adherence ≥ 5 years.
4. **C:** Placebo or no aspirin; same lifestyle advice.
5. **O:** First non-fatal MI or cardiovascular death over 10 years (patient-important, with horizon).
6. **Resulting PICO sentence:** "In asymptomatic women aged 40–59 at average cardiovascular risk (P), does daily low-dose aspirin 75–100 mg (I) compared with placebo (C) reduce first non-fatal MI or cardiovascular mortality over 10 years (O)?"
7. **Best study design:** RCT with ≥ 10-year follow-up, or a systematic review of such RCTs (e.g., the Women's Health Study cohort of aspirin trials).

The trap is the **C**: students who delete "placebo" and write "vs nothing" retrieve uncontrolled observational data and miss the actual RCT evidence base.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

> Where PICO meets shared decision-making, GRADE, and the limits of the four-letter frame.

#### Edge Cases and Extensions Worth Knowing

The Saudi GP Board sometimes tests the *boundaries* of PICO rather than its centre. These are the boundary cases that appear in higher-altitude stems.

- **Qualitative questions:** PICO breaks down when the question is "What do patients with heart failure experience when starting palliative care?" Here **SPIDER** (Sample, Phenomenon of Interest, Design, Evaluation, Research type) or **PEO** (Population, Exposure, Outcome) fits better. Do not force a qualitative question into PICO just because it is the default.
- **Cost-effectiveness questions:** Add an economic dimension. PICO + **T** (Timeframe) + cost perspective converts the frame into a health-economics question best answered by modelling studies, not RCTs.
- **Prevalence/screening questions:** Frame as P + I (screening test) + C (no screening or alternative test) + O (stage shift, mortality, false-positive harm). Cohort or modelling evidence, not diagnostic-accuracy studies.
- **Time-sensitive acute questions:** When the **O** window is hours (e.g., anaphylaxis management), RCT follow-up may be shorter than the clinically meaningful horizon — flag this when appraising.

#### Connections to the Wider Evidence-Based Practice Toolkit

PICO is upstream of every appraisal tool on the board blueprint. Knowing the chain prevents the mistake of running a critical-appraisal checklist on a question that was never properly built.

1. PICO produces the searchable question.
2. The question type selects the **study design**.
3. The selected study design selects the **critical appraisal tool** (CASP for RCTs/cohort/case-control; QUADAS-2 for diagnostic accuracy).
4. The appraisal feeds **GRADE** to rate certainty (high/moderate/low/very low) for the body of evidence.
5. The certainty rating drives the **shared decision-making** conversation with the patient.

> 📌 **Formula Check (certainty chain):** PICO → study design → CASP/QUADAS-2 → GRADE rating → recommendation strength. Skipping any step invalidates the next.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

These mirror higher-cognitive stems the board uses to separate competent from mastery candidates.

1. A 58-year-old man on metformin for type 2 diabetes asks about turmeric for glycaemic control. Formulate the PICO, name the question type, and defend why a systematic review of RCTs outranks a single open-label cohort study for this decision. Then identify the GRADE downgrades likely to apply (imprecision, indirectness, publication bias for small positive trials of nutraceuticals).
2. A primary care clinic wants to introduce point-of-care CRP to guide antibiotic prescribing in adults with acute cough. Build the PICO, justify why a diagnostic-accuracy framework rather than a therapy framework applies, and state the reference standard against which point-of-care CRP must be compared. Then flag the patient-important **O** that diagnostic accuracy alone does not capture (30-day antibiotic use, hospitalization, mortality).
