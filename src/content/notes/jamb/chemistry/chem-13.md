# Amines and Amides

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your JAMB UTME paper.

**Amines** are derivatives of ammonia (NH3) in which one, two, or three H atoms are swapped for alkyl or aryl groups. They carry a lone pair on nitrogen, which makes them **Brønsted–Lowry bases**. **Amides** contain the carbonyl–nitrogen group **–C(=O)–NH₂** and are formed when a carboxylic acid derivative reacts with ammonia or an amine, releasing water.

| Class | General formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1° amine | R–NH₂ | Ethanamine, CH₃CH₂NH₂ |
| 2° amine | R₂NH | Dimethylamine, (CH₃)₂NH |
| 3° amine | R₃N | Trimethylamine, (CH₃)₃N |
| Primary amide | R–CONH₂ | Ethanamide, CH₃CONH₂ |

- **Basicity order (aqueous):** (CH₃)₂NH > CH₃NH₂ > (CH₃)₃N > NH₃ > C₆H₅NH₂.
- The **amide (peptide) linkage –CO–NH–** joins amino acids in proteins and repeats in nylon.
- Acyl chloride route: **RCOCl + 2NH₃ → RCONH₂ + NH₄Cl** (1:2 mole ratio).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students working through the syllabus over days to months.

#### Classification and nomenclature

Amines are classed by the number of carbon groups directly bonded to nitrogen: **1° (R–NH₂), 2° (R₂NH), 3° (R₃N)**. IUPAC names use the suffix *-amine* (ethanamine) or *-aniline* for aromatic amines (e.g., *N,N*-dimethylaniline). **Amides** are classed the same way, based on hydrogens left on N after acylation: *primary* (R–CONH₂), *secondary* (R–CONHR′), *tertiary* (R–CONR′R″).

#### Physical properties

Primary and secondary amines form **intermolecular N–H···N hydrogen bonds**, so they boil higher than alkanes of similar mass but lower than the corresponding alcohols. **Tertiary amines have no N–H bond**, so they cannot hydrogen-bond to themselves and boil much lower than 1°/2° isomers.

#### Basicity of amines

The lone pair on nitrogen accepts a proton, generating R–NH₃⁺:

> R–NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ R–NH₃⁺ + OH⁻   (Kb characterises the equilibrium)

Alkyl groups are **electron-donating (+I)**, raising electron density on N and increasing basicity. The phenyl group in **aniline (C₆H₅NH₂)** delocalises the lone pair into the ring, lowering basicity below that of ammonia.

#### Preparation of amines and amides

| Method | Equation | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Reduction of nitro | R–NO₂ + 6[H] → R–NH₂ + 2H₂O | Sn/HCl or Fe/HCl then NaOH |
| Ammonolysis of halide | RX + NH₃ → R–NH₂ | Gives a mixture of 1°/2°/3° |
| Hofmann degradation | R–CONH₂ + Br₂ + 4NaOH → R–NH₂ + Na₂CO₃ + 2NaBr + 2H₂O | Loses one C |
| Acyl chloride route | R–COCl + 2NH₃ → R–CONH₂ + NH₄Cl | Standard amide prep |

> **JAMB trap:** never write R–COOH + NH₃ directly; the free acid needs a strong dehydrating agent. Use the acyl chloride or anhydride route.

#### Hydrolysis of amides

Acid hydrolysis (reflux with dilute HCl) gives the carboxylic acid and ammonium chloride; alkaline hydrolysis (NaOH) gives the sodium carboxylate and free amine. The **peptide (amide) linkage** is what hydrolysis breaks in proteins and nylons.

#### JAMB question patterns

Expect **MCQs** asking you to (i) classify an amine from its formula, (ii) rank basicity given several amines, (iii) identify the product of RCOCl + NH₃, and (iv) recognise the –CO–NH– bond as the amide/peptide linkage.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students building long-term mastery.

#### Electronic effects on basicity — a quantitative view

Quantitative Kb values support the qualitative ranking. For methylamines in water:

| Amine | pKb (approx.) |
|---|---|
| (CH₃)₂NH | 3.27 |
| CH₃NH₂ | 3.36 |
| (CH₃)₃N | 4.19 |
| NH₃ | 4.75 |
| C₆H₅NH₂ (aniline) | 9.38 |

The drop from CH₃NH₂ to (CH₃)₃N reflects **steric hindrance** around nitrogen — three bulky methyl groups block the incoming H⁺ even though the +I effect is strongest. Aniline's lone pair is conjugated into the aromatic ring, leaving far less electron density available for protonation.

#### Worked example

**Question:** Arrange CH₃CH₂NH₂, (CH₃CH₂)₂NH, (CH₃CH₂)₃N, NH₃, and C₆H₅NH₂ in order of increasing basicity.

**Working:** Among the ethylamines, two ethyl groups give the strongest +I push but steric clash makes the 2° amine slightly more basic than the 1°, while the 3° amine suffers severe steric hindrance. Aniline is the weakest because of resonance delocalisation into the ring.

> **Answer (weakest → strongest):** C₆H₅NH₂ < NH₃ < (CH₃CH₂)₃N < CH₃CH₂NH₂ < (CH₃CH₂)₂NH.

#### Mechanism of acylation

When ammonia attacks the carbonyl carbon of an acyl chloride, a **tetrahedral intermediate** forms, and chloride leaves. A second NH₃ molecule then deprotonates the protonated amide, producing NH₄Cl. This is a classic **nucleophilic acyl substitution**.

#### Connections and edge cases

- **Quaternary ammonium salts** (R₄N⁺X⁻) carry a *positive* charge on N — distinct from a tertiary amine, which is neutral.
- **Hofmann rearrangement** degrades an amide to a 1° amine *with one fewer carbon*, useful in synthesis.
- **Polyamides (nylon-6,6)** are built from 1,6-diaminohexane and adipoyl chloride; the repeating unit is –NH–(CH₂)₆–NH–CO–(CH₂)₄–CO–.
- **Distinguishing test:** warming an amine salt with NaOH liberates NH₃ (turns damp red litmus blue); amides require prolonged reflux with dilute HCl before NH₄⁺ is detectable.

#### Common mistakes

> Confusing amide hydrolysis products: with HCl you get R–COOH + NH₄Cl; with NaOH you get R–COO⁻Na⁺ + R′–NH₂. The acidic pathway regenerates the free acid; the alkaline pathway releases the free amine.

#### Practice prompts

1. Write the equation for preparing ethanamide from ethanoyl chloride and state the mole ratio of reactants.
2. Explain, with reference to electronic effects, why aniline is a weaker base than ammonia.
