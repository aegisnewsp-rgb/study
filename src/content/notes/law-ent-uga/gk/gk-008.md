# Key Legislation and the Ugandan Legal Framework

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> One screen that lets you walk into the Uganda Law Admission exam room and name every source of Ugandan law without blinking.

The 1995 Constitution of Uganda sits at the top of every legal question you will meet. Beneath it sit Acts of Parliament, then Statutory Instruments (delegated legislation), then customary law — but only where customary law does not clash with the Constitution. Case law from the courts fills the gaps through the doctrine of judicial precedent.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook (Memory Hook):** Think "C-A-S-S-C-T" — **C**onstitution, **A**cts of Parliament, **S**tatutory Instruments, **S**upreme Court precedent, **C**ustomary law, **T**reaties (ratified). Read top to bottom for hierarchy, except Customary which yields upward to the Constitution.

The Supreme Court is the apex court under Article 130 of the Constitution, not the Court of Appeal — that single swap is the most common wrong answer on admission MCQs.

- **Supreme law:** 1995 Constitution of Uganda
- **Legislature:** Parliament (passes Acts)
- **Executive:** Cabinet headed by the President
- **Judiciary:** Supreme Court → Court of Appeal → High Court → Magistrates' Courts
- **Reform body:** Uganda Law Reform Commission (reviews and modernises statutes)
- **Treaties:** bind Uganda only after Parliamentary ratification

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

> Everything a working candidate needs: how each source of law ranks, who makes what, and where examiners plant their tricks.

#### Sources and their rank

Ugandan law is layered. The Constitution overrides every other source. Acts of Parliament must conform to it; if any clause contradicts the Constitution, a court will strike it down. Statutory Instruments (SIs) are made by ministers under powers an Act gives them, and they too must fit both the parent Act and the Constitution. Customary law applies only where it is not inconsistent with the Constitution or any written law — Article 37 of the Constitution preserves it, but the supremacy clause in Article 2 limits it. Treaties become binding domestic law only after Parliament ratifies them; Uganda is not a monist state.

#### Comparison matrix: easy-to-confuse pairs

| Concept | What it actually is | Common wrong belief |
|---|---|---|
| Uganda Law Reform Commission | Statutory body that reviews, reforms and modernises Ugandan statutes | That it regulates lawyers — that is the Law Society of Uganda |
| Court of Appeal vs Supreme Court | Court of Appeal is the second-highest appellate court; Supreme Court is the apex under Article 130 | That the Court of Appeal is the highest court |
| Customary law status | Applies only if consistent with the Constitution and written law | That it automatically overrides statute |
| Treaty status in Uganda | Dualist — binds only after Parliamentary ratification | That signing alone makes a treaty enforceable locally |
| Statutory Instruments | Delegated legislation made by ministers under a parent Act | That all rules must come from Parliament as Acts |

#### The three arms of government

Article 61 of the 1995 Constitution sets out the doctrine of separation of powers. Parliament makes laws through Bills that receive Presidential assent. The Executive, headed by the President and Cabinet, implements laws and runs policy. The Judiciary, headed by the Chief Justice, interprets laws and delivers justice. The Judicial Service Commission handles appointments, discipline and conditions of service of judicial officers, insulating judges from political pressure.

#### Worked exam-style question

A candidate is asked: *"Arrange the following in descending legal hierarchy: (i) Customary law, (ii) 1995 Constitution, (iii) Act of Parliament, (iv) Statutory Instrument, (v) Ratified treaty."*

Working through it: the Constitution is supreme under Article 2. Acts of Parliament come next and are valid only if they conform to the Constitution. Statutory Instruments follow the Act that creates them and must also fit the Constitution. Ratified treaties sit at the level of Acts of Parliament — they are applied as part of domestic law once ratified but yield to the Constitution. Customary law applies in residual matters but is void where it conflicts with the Constitution or any written law.

Correct order: **Constitution → Act of Parliament → Statutory Instrument → Ratified treaty → Customary law.**

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Putting customary law at the bottom is safe, but many candidates push ratified treaties below customary law because treaties "feel foreign." Once Parliament ratifies a treaty it operates at the level of an Act of Parliament — above residual customary rules.

#### Doctrine of precedent in Ugandan courts

The doctrine of judicial precedent binds lower courts to the rationes decidendi (the binding reason) of higher courts. The Supreme Court's decisions bind the Court of Appeal, the High Court and Magistrates' Courts. The Court of Appeal binds the High Court and Magistrates' Courts, except where the Supreme Court has later ruled otherwise. Obiter dicta (comments not essential to the decision) are persuasive, not binding.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

> For the candidate who wants the edges: where the framework breaks, what is contested, and how to handle the unusual MCQ.

#### Edge cases and boundary conditions

- **Constitutional amendment under Article 259.** Parliament can amend the Constitution by passing a Bill supported by at least two-thirds of all Members of Parliament, and certain entrenched clauses (such as the republican form of government, the presidential system, and specific term limits) require a referendum. Candidates confuse ordinary amendment with entrenched amendment — the threshold is the giveaway.
- **Judicial review of primary legislation.** The High Court, Court of Appeal and Supreme Court all have jurisdiction to interpret the Constitution. Once a court declares a provision unconstitutional, the position is restored as if that provision never existed. The East African Court of Justice also has jurisdiction over the East African Community Treaty but cannot invalidate Ugandan Acts on human-rights grounds — its mandate is the treaty itself.
- **Residual customary law.** Where a community's custom is silent on a matter, the law applies as if no custom existed. Custom that has never been reduced to writing is proved by oral evidence, which creates a substantial evidential problem in litigation.
- **Delegated legislation going wrong.** If a minister makes an SI outside the powers the parent Act grants, courts can strike it down for ultra vires. Candidates should note that SIs are not debated clause-by-clause in Parliament, which is why judicial review is the main safeguard.

#### Common traps examiners set

1. Treating the Uganda Law Reform Commission as part of the Judiciary — it sits under the Ministry of Justice and is advisory, not adjudicatory.
2. Saying the President makes laws — the President only assents to Bills passed by Parliament under Article 91.
3. Listing the East African Court of Justice as a Ugandan domestic court — it is a regional court established under the EAC Treaty.
4. Forgetting that the Judicial Service Commission, not the President alone, appoints judges — the President appoints the Chief Justice and Justices of the Supreme Court on the Commission's advice.
5. Calling the Magistrates' Courts part of the High Court — they are subordinate courts under the Magistrates' Courts Act, separate from the High Court Division structure.

#### Advanced practice prompts

1. Trace how a Bill becomes law in Uganda, identifying the point at which judicial review can no longer be avoided and the point at which the President must act.
2. A village council applies a customary rule that disqualifies women from inheriting clan land. A daughter challenges it. Walk through which body hears the case, which law applies, and what the likely outcome is under Article 21 and Article 26 of the Constitution.
