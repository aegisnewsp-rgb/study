# Pakistan's Governance, Economy, and Society: Core Issues and Challenges

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Pakistan's structural problems trace back to four post-1947 choices: a parliamentary federation with weak civilian oversight, a mixed economy hooked on IMF programmes and overseas remittances, an unaddressed demographic surge, and a feudal social order that delays legal reform. For **KPK PMS** Pakistan Affairs (3% weight), examiners usually ask one MCQ on the NFC Award split or one short note on federal imbalance.

- **Governance core**: civil-military imbalance; constitutional suspensions in 1958, 1969, 1977, 1999; bureaucratic red-tape; judicial activism after 2007.
- **Economic core**: low tax-to-GDP ratio (about 9–10%), circular debt in the power sector, persistent balance-of-payments crises, IMF Standby/Extended arrangements recurring roughly every few years.
- **Social core**: population growth near 2.4%, literacy around 60% nationally and lower in KPK, jirga-feudal norms clashing with formal law, gender gap in schooling.
- **Federalism core**: NFC Awards of 1991 (multiple criteria) and 2010 (divisible pool), 18th Amendment (2010) devolving ministries, 25th Amendment (2018) merging FATA into KPK.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"F-G-E-S"** — **F**ederation, **G**overnance gaps, **E**conomy (debt + deficit), **S**ocial (population + feudalism). Pair it with **"18-25-FK"** — 18th Amendment (devolution), 25th Amendment (**F**ATA → **K**PK).

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Federalism and Centre-Province Resource Sharing

Pakistan runs a parliamentary federal system under the 1973 Constitution, but provincial autonomy has been contested since One Unit (1955–1970). The **National Finance Commission (NFC)** settles vertical (centre–province) and horizontal (among provinces) shares every five years. Two awards matter for the exam:

| Award | Year | Chair | Horizontal basis | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First NFC | 1991 | Razi-uddin Ahmad | Multiple criteria (population, poverty, inverse population density, revenue collection) | First formal revenue transfer to provinces |
| Seventh NFC | 2010 | Haseeb Khan (Tarin-era) | Population + poverty + area + inverse population density, applied to divisible pool | Provinces' share rose from ~46% to ~56–57% |

The 18th Amendment (2010) deleted the Concurrent Legislative List and devolved several ministries, formally shifting Pakistan toward a more asymmetric federation. KPK aspirants must also know the **25th Amendment (2018)**: FATA's 12 agencies merged into KPK, altering representation in the National Assembly and provincial assembly seats and creating the first such integration since 1970.

#### Governance Defects

Civil-military relations swing on three fulcrums: (i) constitutional authority to dissolve assemblies (Article 58(2)(b), partially clipped by the 18th Amendment), (ii) the Army's role in security and now economic sectors (e.g., Fauji Foundation, Army Welfare Trust), and (iii) judicial review, sharply active since the Lawyers' Movement (2007–2009). Bureaucratic red-tape remains severe because the civil service structure was inherited from the 1935 Government of India Act and modified mainly through the **Devolution Plan 2001**, which created the Local Government system but kept bureaucratic hierarchies intact.

> 📌 **Formula Check:** Vertical share is set as a percentage of the divisible pool (post-2010 ≈ 57.5% provinces / 42.5% centre). Horizontal share uses weights — never memorise the exact weight without confirming in the official Finance Division notification.

#### Economy: Structural Weaknesses

Pakistan's recurring IMF negotiations reflect three structural deficits — **fiscal** (revenue < expenditure), **current account** (imports > exports plus remittances), and **energy** (circular debt, capacity payments). The tax-to-GDP ratio has hovered around **9–10%**, far below the 15–20% benchmark for developing peers. Agriculture still absorbs roughly **37%** of the labour force, while services dominate GDP. Since 1988, almost every elected government has signed an IMF Standby or Extended Fund Facility, often accompanied by **Structural Adjustment Programmes (SAPs)**: privatisation, subsidy cuts, and tax-base broadening.

#### Comparison Matrix: Frequently Confused Concepts

| Concept A | Concept B | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|
| 17th Amendment (2003) | 18th Amendment (2010) | 17th validated Musharraf's actions and created a PMC; 18th deleted the Concurrent List and restored parliamentary supremacy |
| FATA merger | AJK / Gilgit-Baltistan status | FATA merged into KPK in 2018; AJK and GB remain separate with their own councils, not full provinces |
| NFC Award 1991 | NFC Award 2010 | 1991 used multiple criteria (4 factors); 2010 increased provincial share and shifted weight to divisible pool |
| Islamisation (Zia era) | Shariat Appellate Bench | Zia enacted Hudood Ordinances (1979) and Zakat Ordinance (1980); the Shariat Appellate Bench reviews laws against Quran & Sunnah |
| Devolution Plan 2001 | Local Government Act 2013 (KPK) | 2001 was Musharraf-era, all provinces; 2013 KPK's own Local Government Act under PTI, more decentralised |

#### Social Indicators and KPK-Specific Issues

Demographic pressure: with growth near 2.4% annually, Pakistan adds the equivalent of a medium-sized city every year. Literacy (≈60% national; lower in KPK rural areas) and gender enrolment gaps compound the demographic challenge. Feudal and tribal customs — **jirga** decisions, **swara**, honour crimes — continue to compete with formal Pakistani law, especially in southern Punjab, Balochistan and the merged tribal districts of KPK.

1. Open with the constitutional/legal anchor (1973 Constitution, relevant Article or Amendment).
2. Identify the structural cause (federation imbalance, fiscal deficit, demographic pressure).
3. Add the historical trigger (e.g., 1971 secession, 1977 martial law, 2008 financial crisis).
4. Close with a KPK-specific note when the question touches federalism or governance.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** "The 2010 NFC Award is often cited as the most significant federal concession to provinces since 1991. Critically examine the change in horizontal-distribution criteria between the two awards and its impact on KPK."

#### Solution:

- **Step 1 — Vertical share context (formula):** After the 2010 NFC, provinces collectively receive about **57.5%** of the divisible pool, up from the earlier ~46%. Vertical share = (provinces' combined share ÷ total divisible pool) × 100.
- **Step 2 — Horizontal criteria 1991:** Population, poverty, revenue effort, and inverse population density — each weighted separately.
- **Step 3 — Horizontal criteria 2010:** The same four factors are used, but applied to the enlarged divisible pool with population receiving the heaviest weight; thus population-rich provinces gained.
- **Step 4 — Impact on KPK:** KPK's share of the divisible pool rose modestly compared to Punjab's, because KPK's population weight is smaller, but post-merger (25th Amendment, 2018) the merged FATA population increased KPK's notional share. Net transfers funded health, police, and the Sustainable Development Unit for newly merged districts.
- **Step 5 — Conclusion:** The 2010 formula is a hybrid: it preserves the 1991 multi-factor approach but ties it to a bigger divisible pool, rewarding population while protecting smaller provinces from extreme loss.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Candidates often quote "47% provinces / 53% centre" as the current split — that was the pre-2010 position. After the 7th NFC Award the figures flipped in favour of provinces. Always confirm the latest Finance Division notification before quoting exact percentages.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Constitutional Layering

Pakistan's federal asymmetry is not symmetric: AJK and Gilgit-Baltistan have their own legislative councils but no full provincial status; their residents were granted voting rights in national elections via the 23rd (2017) and 24th (2018) Amendments, but they still elect MNAs on separate seats. The IRSA (Indus River System Authority) Act 1991 governs water sharing under the 1991 Water Apportionment Accord — a parallel mechanism that bypasses the NFC. Cases involving Kalabagh Dam and the ongoing Centre-Balochistan resource disputes often surface in CSS/PMS essays.

#### Energy and Circular Debt Mechanics

**Circular debt** is not a single number but a chain: the Central Power Purchasing Agency (CPPA) owes generators capacity payments; DISCOs owe CPPA; the federal government subsidises DISCOs. The result is a stock that has accumulated through decades of tariff suppression and theft losses. The Independent Power Producers (IPPs) signed under Musharraf-era policies (1994–2008) carry take-or-pay clauses, locking the system into capacity payments even when demand is low. For KPK, the financial weight is felt through lower captive hydel generation despite the province hosting large hydropower sites (e.g., Tarbela, the upcoming Dasu).

#### CPEC and the Western Route

The China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC) runs both the eastern and western routes. KPK benefits primarily from the western route: the **Hazara Motorway, Peshawar–Karachi motorway segments, and the Special Economic Zones** near Rashakai and D.I. Khan. Aspirants should connect CPEC to: (i) provincial revenue through the BLA mechanism, (ii) security externalities along the Afghan corridor, and (iii) potential displacement and labour migration in the merged districts.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. **Compare the 17th and 18th Amendments as competing visions of parliamentary supremacy.** Discuss how the 18th Amendment clipped Article 58(2)(b) and what remained in the executive's toolkit afterward.
2. **Evaluate whether the 25th Amendment solved the FATA governance problem.** Consider judicial access, police integration, the merger of the FATA Tribunal into Peshawar High Court, and the slow rollout of the Tribal Districts Fund.
