# Health Education and Communication

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your NEET PG exam.

## Health Education — Key Facts

### Definition
Health education uses communication strategies to help individuals and communities improve health by increasing knowledge and influencing attitudes.

### Health Belief Model (HBM) — Most Important
Developed by Hochbaum, Rosenstock and Cairns (1952–1966); the modern form is usually attributed to Rosenstock. Components:
1. **Perceived Susceptibility**: "Will I get it?"
2. **Perceived Severity**: "How serious is it?"
3. **Perceived Benefits**: "Will action help?"
4. **Perceived Barriers**: "What stops me?"
5. **Cues to Action**: "What triggers action?"
6. **Self-Efficacy**: "Can I do it?" (added by Rosenstock, Strecher and Becker in 1988, drawing on Bandura's self-efficacy construct)

### PRECEDE-PROCEED Model
**PRECEDE** (Planning): Social → Epidemiological → Behavioral → Educational → Administrative diagnosis
**PROCEED** (Implementation): Policy → Implementation → Evaluation

**3 Types of Factors**:
- **Predisposing**: Knowledge, attitudes, beliefs
- **Reinforcing**: Family, peers, teachers, HCWs
- **Enabling**: Skills, resources, accessibility

⚡ **NEET PG High-Yield**: Health Belief Model is most frequently asked model.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

## Chapter: Health Education and Communication

### 1.1 Concepts of Health Education

#### Definition
Health education concerns itself with communication of information about the existence of risk or absence of risk so that individuals and communities can make appropriate decisions.

#### Objectives
1. Create awareness about health problems
2. Promote healthy behaviors
3. Enable informed decisions
4. Reduce misconceptions
5. Motivate health service utilization

### 1.2 Health Belief Model

#### Components

1. **Perceived Susceptibility**: One's opinion of chances of getting condition
   - Example: "I might get diabetes because of family history"

2. **Perceived Severity**: Opinion of how serious condition is
   - Example: "Diabetes can lead to blindness, kidney failure"

3. **Perceived Benefits**: Belief in efficacy of advised action
   - Example: "Losing weight will prevent diabetes complications"

4. **Perceived Barriers**: Obstacles to action
   - Example: "Dieting is too difficult and expensive"

5. **Cues to Action**: Strategies to activate readiness
   - Example: "Doctor's advice, family member's illness"

6. **Self-Efficacy**: Confidence in ability (Rosenstock, Strecher & Becker, 1988)
   - Example: "I can exercise regularly and follow my diet"

**Modified Health Belief**: Perceived Susceptibility × Perceived Severity = Perceived Threat
**Decision**: Benefits > Barriers

#### Question Pattern
Common NEET question: "What motivates a person to undergo screening?" → Answer: Health Belief Model

### 1.3 PRECEDE-PROCEED Model

#### PRECEDE (Phases 1-5)

| Phase | Diagnosis | Focus |
|-------|-----------|-------|
| Phase 1 | Social | Quality of life issues |
| Phase 2 | Epidemiological | Priority health problems |
| Phase 3 | Behavioral & Environmental | Risk factors |
| Phase 4 | Educational & Ecological | Predisposing, Reinforcing, Enabling |
| Phase 5 | Administrative & Policy | Feasibility assessment |

#### PROCEED (Phases 6-9)
- Phase 6: Implementation
- Phase 7: Process evaluation
- Phase 8: Impact evaluation
- Phase 9: Outcome evaluation

### 1.4 IEC and BCC

**IEC (Information, Education, Communication)**:
- Broader term
- Any combination of communication for awareness
- Cycle: Identify needs → Develop → Pre-test → Disseminate → Evaluate

**BCC (Behavior Change Communication)**:
- Specifically targets behavior change
- Uses multiple channels: Counseling, social mobilization, media, advocacy
- Based on formative research

### 1.5 Behavior Change Theories

**Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura)**:
- Reciprocal determinism: Behavior, cognition, environment interact
- Observational learning (modeling)
- Self-efficacy is central concept

**Transtheoretical Model (Stages of Change)**:
- Precontemplation → Contemplation → Preparation → Action → Maintenance
- Used in smoking cessation, weight loss programs

**Theory of Planned Behavior (Ajzen)**:
- Intention → Behavior
- Intention influenced by: Attitude, Subjective norm, Perceived behavioral control

### 1.6 Health Communication Methods

#### Interpersonal Communication (IPC)
- Face-to-face, two-way
- Used in: Counseling, doctor-patient, ASHA-Anganwadi
- Barriers: Language, literacy, time

#### Mass Media
- TV, radio, print, social media
- Wide reach, one-way
- Advantage: Large reach
- Disadvantage: No feedback

#### Small Media
- Posters, pamphlets, flipcharts, wall paintings
- Bridge between mass and IPC

#### Folk Media
- **Nukkad Natak**: Street plays
- **Puppet Shows**: Effective for child health
- **Bhajan/Kirtan**: Religious songs with health messages
- **Advantage**: Culturally appropriate, low cost

### 1.7 Counseling Skills

#### Qualities of Good Counselor
- **Empathy**: Understanding client's perspective
- **Warmth**: Non-judgmental acceptance
- **Genuineness**: Being authentic
- **Confidentiality**: Privacy assurance
- **Active listening**: Hearing and responding

#### Process (CORE)
1. **Contact**: Establish rapport
2. **Opportunity**: Allow expression
3. **Response**: Provide feedback
4. **Encounter**: Work through problems

### 1.8 Doctor-Patient Communication

#### Importance
- Better treatment adherence
- Improved patient satisfaction
- Reduced medical errors
- Better outcomes

#### Barriers
- **Patient**: Fear, anxiety, literacy, language
- **Doctor**: Time pressure, fatigue, medical jargon
- **System**: Crowding, privacy issues

### 1.9 ASHA — Community Health Worker

**ASHA** = Accredited Social Health Activist
- Link between community and health system
- **Not a nurse or ANM** — volunteer community health activist
- **Honorarium-based** (not salary)

#### Communication Tasks
- Home visits (pregnant women, new mothers, children)
- VHSND organization
- Community mobilization
- Counseling (immunization, nutrition, family planning)
- Distributing ORS, zinc, contraceptives

### 1.10 Health Promotion — Ottawa Charter

**Ottawa Charter (1986)** — 5 Action Areas:
1. **Build Healthy Public Policy**: Legislation, taxation
2. **Create Supportive Environments**: Safe living/working
3. **Strengthen Community Actions**: Participation, empowerment
4. **Develop Personal Skills**: Education, life skills
5. **Reorient Health Services**: From curative to preventive

**Jakarta Declaration (1997)** added priorities:
- Social responsibility
- Investment and infrastructure
- Consolidate partnerships
- Increase community capacity

### 1.11 National Health Programs

**Janani Suraksha Yojana (JSY)**:
- Cash assistance for institutional delivery
- ASHA as communication link

**Immunization Communication**:
- Focus on parental misconceptions
- Social mobilization through ASHA

**National AIDS Control Program**:
- Condom promotion (social marketing + IPC)
- Red Ribbon Express: Train peer educators

### 1.12 Evaluation of Health Education

**Types**:
1. **Process**: Was program implemented as planned?
2. **Impact**: Short-term changes (KAP — Knowledge, Attitude, Practice)
3. **Outcome**: Long-term changes (morbidity, mortality)

**KAP Survey Limitations**:
- KAP doesn't always translate to actual behavior change

### 1.13 NEET PG High-Yield Points

⚡ **Commonly Asked**:
1. **Health Belief Model** components (6) — most asked model
2. **PRECEDE-PROCEED**: Predisposing, Reinforcing, Enabling factors
3. **IEC vs BCC**: IEC broader, BCC targets specific behavior
4. **ASHA**: Link worker, incentive-based, NOT salaried
5. **Ottawa Charter**: 5 action areas
6. **Self-efficacy**: Added to HBM by Rosenstock, Strecher & Becker (1988), drawing on Bandura's construct
7. **Empathy, warmth, genuineness**: Qualities of good counselor
8. **Transtheoretical model**: 5 stages
9. **Social Cognitive Theory**: Bandura, reciprocal determinism
10. **Jakarta Declaration**: 1997, added priorities to Ottawa Charter

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Choosing the right model for the right vignette

Almost every model-matching question is really asking one thing: *what stage of change is this person at, and what is stopping them?* Work backwards from the answer key.

| Vignette tells you | The model it belongs to | The discriminating feature |
|---|---|---|
| Weighs the odds of a disease, judges how bad it would be | Health Belief Model | Susceptibility × Severity = threat |
| "My father walks 30 minutes, so I should too" | Social Cognitive (Bandura) | Reciprocal determinism, modelling |
| "I've never really thought about quitting" | Transtheoretical | Precontemplation — no intent yet |
| Attitude + what neighbours think + "can I actually do it" | Planned Behaviour (Ajzen) | Intention is the sum of three antecedents |
| District has sanctioned the budget but no approval exists for the new staff posts | PRECEDE-PROCEED | Phase 5 administrative & policy diagnosis |

**Examiner trap:** every model contains a behavioural construct. Students who answer "Health Belief Model" for any question mentioning behaviour are guessing. HBM's behavioural construct is *health action*; SCT's is *the behaviour itself*; TPB's is *intention* as the immediate determinant.

A second trap runs the other way. "Which phase argues a health centre is needed in that location?" is **Phase 3**, the behavioural and environmental diagnosis, because placement is an environmental determinant. Phase 5 is entered only *after* the problem is defined, to ask whether money, posts and policy make it doable.

#### PRECEDE-PROCEED read as a single sentence

PRECEDE is a *planning* engine that works backwards from the desired outcome to the resources needed; PROCEED is the *execution* half that evaluates as it goes.

1. Social diagnosis — whose quality of life is at stake
2. Epidemiological diagnosis — which problems actually account for it
3. Behavioural and environmental diagnosis — which factors are modifiable
4. Educational and ecological diagnosis — predisposing, enabling, reinforcing
5. Administrative and policy diagnosis — can we actually fund and staff it

If a question asks which phase identified "faculty shortage in the PHC", the answer is Phase 5, not Phase 4. Phase 4 diagnoses *learner* factors; Phase 5 diagnoses *system* constraints.

#### The three E's of Phase 4, and which one a barrier belongs to

- **Predisposing** — knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, values *before* the behaviour. A nurse who has never been taught that sugar drives caries is predisposing-deficient.
- **Enabling** — the resources that make action possible: availability, distance, affordability, waiting time. Even a willing mother cannot immunise her child if the nearest centre is 12 km away.
- **Reinforcing** — the social response *after* the behaviour. A scolded patient will not return for follow-up; a praised one will.

A well-constructed question gives you a distinguishing verb. "Does not know" → predisposing. "Centre is too far" → enabling. "Family mocks the practice" → reinforcing.

#### Worked problem — the non-respondent

> A district launches an anti-smoking drive. Telephone survey coverage is 41%, and among those reached, quit attempts cluster in the highest income quintile. Rural respondents are absent from the follow-up list entirely.

Diagnose the failure before proposing a fix.

1. The 41% coverage figure is a **selection bias**, not a random sample. Telephone ownership is itself income-linked, so the sample systematically excludes the poor.
2. Skewing toward the top income quintile confirms this — the result tracks the sampling frame, not the biology of quitting.
3. Rural absence points to an **enabling** deficit at Phase 4, and possibly a Phase 5 staffing failure if no outreach worker was assigned.
4. The correct next move is a cluster sample with household visits, not a larger telephone wave. Increasing the size of a biased sample does not reduce bias.

**Common examiner trap:** a question showing low participation and offering "send more SMS reminders" as the answer is testing whether you notice the frame is wrong. Reminders act on *reinforcing* and *predisposing*; neither fixes *enabling* failure.

#### Choosing a communication channel

| Channel | Direction | Best used when | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interpersonal (counselling) | Two-way | The decision is complex or emotionally loaded | Clinician has under two minutes |
| Mass media | One-way | Awareness of a widely-held risk | Behaviour must be negotiated |
| Small media (poster, pamphlet) | One-way | Reinforcing an already-accepted message | Illiteracy, or no chance to read it later |
| Folk media (nukkad natak, puppetry) | Two-way in effect | Reaching a community that trusts its own performers | Urban, literate, time-pressured audience |

Nukkad natak earns its place in Indian public-health teaching because it is *acceptable to the source culture* and it carries a health message inside an entertainment frame the audience already trusts. It is the standard answer when a question asks how to reach a rural, low-literacy group about a culturally sensitive topic.

#### Counselling skills that are actually examinable

The CORE sequence (Contact, Opportunity, Response, Encounter) describes *structure*. The microskills describe *what happens inside each stage*, and the microskills are what most often appear as the options.

- **Active listening** — the whole of the patient's message, including the part not asked about
- **Reflection** — mirroring back content, e.g. "so the cough has been worse at night"
- **Clarification** — checking the ambiguous referent, e.g. "when you say 'it', do you mean the whole tablet?"
- **Empathy** — acknowledging the emotion, not merely the facts

A vignette of a doctor who gives a 40-second lecture and says "any questions?" has broken *both* the opening (no real rapport) and the closing (no invitation to disclose). Asked which skill failed first, answer rapport/contact — a lecture cannot be rescued by a better ending.

#### KAP survey — knowing, believing, doing

| Component | The question it answers | A campaign fails when |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge | Do they know the rule? | They do not know it |
| Attitude | Do they believe it matters? | They know and reject it |
| Practice | Do they actually do it? | They agree but cannot comply |

Splitting a gap this way tells you which instrument to use next: information for a knowledge gap, persuasion or norm-shifting for an attitude gap, and a subsidy or service redesign for a practice gap. A campaign that keeps repeating health messages against a *practice* barrier produces rising knowledge with flat behaviour — which is exactly the result most surveys mistake for programme failure.

#### Health promotion, health protection, health education

- **Health education** — gives people information and skills so they can choose. Voluntary, no coercion.
- **Health protection** — makes the healthy choice the easy or the only one. Seat belts, smokeless zones, iodised salt.
- **Health promotion** — the combination, per the Ottawa Charter: build supportive policy, strengthen community action, develop personal skills, reorient services, and guarantee a healthy environment.

Seat-belt legislation is protection, not education. Telling a driver to wear one is education. Programmes containing only the first never change crash rates.

#### Examiner trap sheet for this chapter

- ASHA is a **volunteer community health activist on an honorarium** — not a nurse, not an ANM, not a salaried employee.
- HBM was framed by **Rosenstock, Strecher and Becker**; the *Modified* version multiplies susceptibility by severity to give threat, then compares benefits against barriers.
- TPB adds **perceived behavioural control**, which Ajzen treats as both a direct predictor of behaviour and a modifier of intention.
- SCC (Sustainability) is not part of PRECEDE-PROCEED; if an option offers a tenth phase, it is wrong — the model runs five in, four out.
- Ottawa Charter has **five action areas**. If an MCQ offers a six that reads plausibly, it is the distractor.
