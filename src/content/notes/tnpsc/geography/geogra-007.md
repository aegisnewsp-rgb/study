# A **region** (3)

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **region** is an area of the earth's surface distinguished by one or more shared physical, cultural or economic attributes that give it internal unity. In TNPSC Group 1 Geography, Unit VII asks you to define a region, classify regions, and apply criteria to agricultural regionalisation.

- **Three regional types** — Formal (uniformity of feature), Functional (nodal interdependence), Comparative (researcher-defined for study).
- **Whittlesey's 13 Agricultural Regions** are tested by listing the five criteria: crop-livestock combination, intensity of land use, degree of commercialisation, methods of farming, and orientation to market.
- Examiner trap: writing that a region is the same as a State — regional boundaries are conceptual, not political.

| Region type | Defining attribute | Boundary meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Formal | Uniformity of one feature | Where uniformity ends |
| Functional | Interaction with a node | Limit of nodal influence |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Definition and Purpose
A **region** is a spatial unit possessing internal homogeneity or functional cohesion that sets it apart from neighbouring areas. The regional concept is a tool for **areal generalisation** — compressing complex spatial data into manageable units for comparison, planning, and identifying disparities.

#### Classification of Regions
Geographers recognise three principal types. **Formal (Uniform/Homogeneous) regions** share a single measurable attribute across space, such as a rainfall belt, a language zone, or a black-soil tract. **Functional (Nodal) regions** are organised around a focal point — a city, market, or transport hub — with intensity of interaction declining outward, producing a defined hinterland. **Comparative (Generic) regions** are research constructs using chosen criteria to compare similar phenomena across the globe.

#### Regional vs Systematic Approach
The **regional approach** studies the totality of phenomena within an area, producing a holistic portrait (e.g., a district monograph). The **systematic approach** studies the global distribution of one phenomenon (e.g., world distribution of tea). TNPSC questions frequently ask candidates to distinguish these two methodologies.

#### Criteria for Delineation
Regions are demarcated using **physical criteria** (relief, climate, vegetation), **cultural criteria** (language, religion, ethnicity), and **economic criteria** (crop combination, industrial concentration, market access).

| Criterion type | Example indicators | Typical region produced |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical | Rainfall isohyets, soil order | Climatic belt |
| Cultural | Language, religion density | Linguistic region |
| Economic | Crop combination, factory density | Agricultural/Industrial region |

- Functional regions require **complementarity, spatial interaction, and transport connectivity** between node and hinterland.
- Whittlesey's typology remains the classic framework for world agricultural regionalisation.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Worked Example — Functional Region of Chennai
Chennai functions as the **node** of a nodal region whose hinterland covers parts of Kanchipuram, Chengalpattu, Tiruvallur and even Arakkonam. The boundary is not a line on a map but the **outer limit of commuter flows, newspaper circulation, and wholesale-trade interaction**. Commuting distance, transport route density, and banking linkages jointly define this limit; beyond it, interaction with Madurai or Bengaluru becomes stronger.

#### Whittlesey's Agricultural Regions — Five Delineating Criteria
1. **Crop and livestock combination** — the dominant enterprise mix.
2. **Intensity of land use** — extensive vs intensive.
3. **Degree of commercialisation** — subsistence vs market-oriented.
4. **Methods of farming** — mechanised, traditional, plantation.
5. **Orientation to market** — local, regional, export.

Whittlesey classified the world into **13 agricultural regions** in 1936, later refined by others. Students should memorise at least the names: **Nomadic Herding, Shifting Cultivation, Rudimentary Sedentary Tillage, Commercial Plantation, Commercial Grain Farming, Commercial Mixed Farming, Mediterranean Agriculture, Dairy Farming, Market Gardening, Factory Farming, Horticulture, Intensive Subsistence (rice and wheat), and Primitive Hunting-Gathering** (some lists merge the last two).

#### Common Mistakes in Answers
- Treating the boundary of a Functional region as a fixed line rather than a **zone of declining interaction**.
- Confusing **Regionalism** (a political/identity movement) with the academic *regional concept* in geography.
- Omitting Tamil Nadu-specific parameters (rainfall cm, soil order, cropping pattern) when naming the State's agricultural regions.

#### Practice Prompts
1. Distinguish Formal and Functional regions with one example each, and explain why their boundaries differ in nature.
2. List Whittlesey's five criteria and apply three of them to identify Tamil Nadu's rice-growing delta region.
