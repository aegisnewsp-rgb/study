# MM8 Graphs of Functions

MM8 is the family that turns everything else into pictures. The specification lists seven requirements: sketching the common functions, the effect of four standard transformations on $y = f(x)$, what happens to $y = mx+c$ when $m$ and $c$ move, what happens to $y = a(x+b)^2 + c$ when $a$, $b$ and $c$ move, using differentiation to establish shape, finding where a graph meets the axes and how many real roots a polynomial can have, and — the item people skip — the geometric meaning of an algebraic solution.

That last one is the spine of MM8. Solving $f(x) = g(x)$ is exactly the same act as finding where the two graphs cross.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> Four transformations, and the pair that gets confused on every single paper.

| Transformation | What happens | In one phrase |
|---|---|---|
| $y = a f(x)$ | Vertical stretch by $a$ | Each $y$ multiplied; a **negative** $a$ reflects in the $x$-axis |
| $y = f(x) + a$ | Vertical shift | Up by $a$, down by $-a$ |
| $y = f(x+a)$ | Horizontal shift | **Left** by $a$ — the plus sign moves the graph the other way |
| $y = f(ax)$ | Horizontal stretch | Left by $a$; a **negative** $a$ also reflects in the $y$-axis |

For $y = mx+c$: $m$ is the gradient and $c$ is the $y$-intercept. For $y = a(x+b)^2+c$: the vertex is $(-b, c)$, the axis of symmetry is $x = -b$, and the graph opens upwards if $a>0$.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### The transformations, with the argument attached

Why does $f(x+a)$ shift left? Because $f(x+a) = f\big(x - (-a)\big)$, and $f(x-h)$ always shifts right by $h$. Substituting $x = 0$ shows it directly: if the original graph passes through $(2, 5)$ then $y = f(x+5)$ passes through $(-3, 5)$, three units to the left. Any point $(p,q)$ on $y = f(x)$ becomes $(p-a, q)$ on $y = f(x+a)$ and $(pa, q)$ on $y = f(ax)$.

Compositions are where this bites. MM8.2 asks you to know the notation $f(g(x))$ and to chain the transformations. In $y = 3f(2x+1)$, the inner bracket shifts the graph left by 1, the $2$ then compresses horizontally by a factor of 2, and the $3$ stretches vertically. Applying them left to right — shift, then compress, then stretch — is the only order that works, and mixing it up is the standard error.

#### What $a$, $b$ and $c$ do in $y = a(x+b)^2 + c$

Take $y = -2(x-3)^2 + 5$. Here $a = -2$, $b = -3$, $c = 5$. The vertex is $(-b, c) = (3,5)$. Since $a < 0$ the parabola opens downwards, so the vertex is a maximum. The axis of symmetry is $x = 3$. The $y$-intercept is found by putting $x = 0$: $-2(9) + 5 = -13$, so $(0, -13)$.

The roots come from $-2(x-3)^2 + 5 = 0$, giving $(x-3)^2 = \tfrac52$ and $x = 3 \pm \sqrt{\tfrac52}$. Two real roots, because the vertex is above the axis. Contrast with $y = -2(x-3)^2 - 5$: same shape, vertex dropped to $(3,-5)$, now entirely below the axis and no real roots at all. The sign of $c$ relative to the vertex decides how many times the graph crosses — which is MM8.6.

#### Differentiation establishes the shape

MM8.5 asks for stationary points excluding inflexions, and for where the graph rises and falls. Take $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2$:

$$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x = 3x(x-2)$$

Stationary points occur where $f'(x) = 0$, so $x = 0$ and $x = 2$. The values are $f(0) = 0$ and $f(2) = 8 - 12 = -4$, giving stationary points $(0,0)$ and $(2,-4)$.

The sign of $f'$ tells you the rest. It is positive for $x < 0$, negative for $0 < x < 2$, and positive for $x > 2$. So the graph rises to $x=0$, falls from $0$ to $2$, and rises thereafter — $(0,0)$ is a local maximum, $(2,-4)$ a local minimum. One sign chart settles both questions at once.

#### How many real roots can a polynomial have?

MM8.6 asks you to appreciate the possible numbers. A polynomial of degree $n$ has **at most** $n$ real roots, counted with multiplicity. For a quadratic that means $0$, $1$ or $2$, decided by the discriminant. A cubic always has at least one and has either $1$ or $3$. A quartic has $0$, $2$ or $4$. The count is the same as the number of times the graph crosses or touches the horizontal axis — which is why drawing the graph is a legitimate way to check your algebra.

#### The common functions you must be able to sketch

MM8.1 names them: lines, quadratics, cubics, trigonometric functions, logarithms, exponentials, square roots and the modulus function.

- $y = \sqrt{x}$: domain $x \ge 0$, vertical tangent at the origin, and always above the $x$-axis. It is the left half of a parabola lying on its side.
- $y = \log_a x$: domain $x > 0$, vertical asymptote at $x = 0$, passing through $(1,0)$ and $(a,1)$. It is the reflection of $y = a^x$ in $y = x$.
- $y = a^x$: passes through $(0,1)$ with horizontal asymptote $y = 0$.
- $y = |x|$: a V with corner at the origin, symmetric in the $y$-axis, entirely non-negative. $y = |x-2|+1$ is the same V translated so the corner sits at $(2,1)$.
- $y = x^3$: an S through the origin with a horizontal tangent there.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (3mo+)

#### Intersections are simultaneous equations

MM8.7 draws the line explicitly: the solutions of $f(x) = g(x)$ are the $x$-coordinates of the intersections of $y = f(x)$ and $y = g(x)$, one for one. A question reading "the line $y = 2x + 1$ meets the curve $y = x^2$ at two points" is not a graph question at all — it is $x^2 = 2x+1$, i.e. $x^2 - 2x - 1 = 0$, i.e. $x = 1 \pm \sqrt2$. Two solutions, two intersection points, and the $y$-coordinates follow from either equation.

This is why the graph of a quadratic against the line $y = 0$ is the graph of the quadratic equation, and it is the bridge between MM8, MM3.1 and MM1.4. Sketching first also bounds the answer: if you can see there is one crossing, you can reject the option claiming three.

#### The two quadrant traps

An intersection with the $y$-axis is found by setting $x = 0$; an intersection with the $x$-axis by setting $y = 0$. Students swap them constantly, because the axis names do not match the coordinates. On $y = x^3 - 3x^2$ the $y$-intercept is $(0,0)$ (set $x=0$) and the only $x$-intercept is $(3,0)$ (solve $x^2(x-3)=0$ and discard the repeated root $x=0$). One intersection, even though the algebra produced three values of $x$ — a clean demonstration of why repeated roots are a sketch question as much as an algebra one.

#### Where sketching runs out

Three things defeat a sketch. A general polynomial can oscillate far more than its degree suggests at a glance, so a picture is evidence rather than proof. Transformations compose badly under sketching — $y = f(f(x))$ moves points to new places, not just across. And MM8.5 explicitly excludes inflexions from examination, so a candidate who spends time locating them is solving a question that was not asked. Understanding the shape is worth marks; producing every feature is not.

### Common mistakes

Shifting $f(x+a)$ to the right instead of the left.

Reading $y = f(ax)$ as a shift rather than a stretch.

Confusing the $x$-axis and $y$-axis intercepts and setting the wrong coordinate to zero.

Assuming a cubic has exactly one real root — it has one or three.

Forgetting that $\log_a x$ and $\sqrt{x}$ have restricted domains, and sketching them across all of $\mathbb{R}$.

Claiming the vertex of $y = a(x+b)^2+c$ is $(b,c)$ rather than $(-b,c)$.

Reading a stationary point as a point of inflexion and hunting for the latter when the question wanted the former.

Answering a question about intersections with $y$-coordinates when the solutions are $x$-coordinates.
