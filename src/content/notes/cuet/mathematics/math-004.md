# Circles

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **circle** is the locus of points in a plane that stay a fixed distance from the centre; that fixed distance is the **radius** r. The boundary is the **circumference** of length **C = 2πr**, enclosing area **A = πr²**, and the longest chord through the centre is the **diameter d = 2r**.

For coordinate geometry, the **standard form** reads **(x − h)² + (y − k)² = r²**, with centre (h, k) and radius r. The expanded **general form x² + y² + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0** has centre **(−g, −f)** and radius **r = √(g² + f² − c)**, valid only when g² + f² − c > 0.

- **Tangent from external point**: length **LT = √S₁**, where S₁ is the value obtained by substituting the point into S ≡ x² + y² + 2gx + 2fy + c.
- **Thales' theorem**: any angle inscribed in a semicircle is 90°.
- **Cyclic quadrilateral rule**: opposite angles sum to 180°.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Centre, Radius, and the Two Forms

Every circle equation in the CUET syllabus collapses into one of two forms. Memorise both the formula and the sign pattern — the most common error is forgetting the minus signs on g and f when reading the centre from the general form.

| Form | Equation | Centre | Radius |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard | (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r² | (h, k) | r |
| General | x² + y² + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0 | (−g, −f) | √(g² + f² − c) |

The radius formula only makes sense when **g² + f² − c > 0**; if the expression equals zero you have a point-circle, and if negative there is no real circle at all.

#### Tangents and the Power of a Point

For an external point P(x₁, y₁), the length of the tangent drawn to circle S = 0 is **PT = √S₁**, where S₁ is the value of x₁² + y₁² + 2gx₁ + 2fy₁ + c. The same quantity governs secants: for a secant PAB intersecting the circle at A and B, **PT² = PA · PB = |PO² − r²|**. This is the **Power of a Point** theorem and appears in roughly one of every three CUET circle questions.

A tangent meets the radius at the point of contact at exactly **90°**, so any "find the angle" question involving a tangent almost always drops out of this perpendicularity.

#### Angles, Chords, and Cyclic Quadrilaterals

The **inscribed angle theorem** says the central angle subtending an arc is twice any inscribed angle on the remaining arc. In particular, an arc that is a semicircle forces every inscribed angle to **90°** (Thales). For a **cyclic quadrilateral**, opposite angles always sum to **180°** — a direct consequence of the same theorem applied to opposite vertices sharing the same chord.

The **Alternate Segment Theorem** ties tangent lines to chord geometry: the angle between a tangent and a chord through the point of contact equals the inscribed angle standing on the chord from the other side.

#### Typical CUET Question Types

- Identify centre and radius from x² + y² − 6x + 4y − 12 = 0 (answer: (3, −2), r = 5).
- Find the equation of a circle with given centre and a passing point.
- Compute tangent length from (4, 6) to x² + y² = 25 (answer: √27).
- Apply cyclic-quadrilateral property to find a missing angle.

> **Scoring tip**: Most CUET circle questions are 1–2 step substitutions. Master the centre/radius conversion table and the tangent formula PT = √S₁ — these two alone cover the majority of marks.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Worked Example

Find the centre, radius, and length of the tangent drawn from point P(2, 5) to the circle x² + y² − 4x + 6y − 7 = 0.

1. Compare with x² + y² + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0 to get 2g = −4, 2f = 6, c = −7, so g = −2, f = 3, c = −7.
2. Centre = (−g, −f) = (2, −3).
3. Radius² = g² + f² − c = 4 + 9 − (−7) = 20, so **r = 2√5**.
4. S₁ = 2² + 5² − 4(2) + 6(5) − 7 = 4 + 25 − 8 + 30 − 7 = 44.
5. Tangent length PT = √44 = **2√11** units.

#### Edge Cases and Adjacent Links

- A "point-circle" (radius zero) arises when g² + f² = c and counts as a degenerate circle in coordinate geometry but not in NCERT Class 10 perimeter/area.
- The **power of a point** formula unifies secants and tangents — if P lies *inside* the circle, the quantity is negative and the product PA · PB is taken as positive.
- The same tangent-radius perpendicularity reappears in **parabola, ellipse, and hyperbola** geometry at CUET level, so a clear grip here reduces revision load later.
- **Chord vs. secant**: a chord is the segment inside the circle joining two boundary points; a secant is the full line extending beyond. Power of a point works for both.

#### Common Traps

- Writing centre as (g, f) instead of (−g, −f) when expanding (x − h)² + (y − k)².
- Forgetting the square root in PT = √S₁ and writing PT = S₁.
- Applying Thales' theorem only to the semicircle case — the inscribed-angle theorem is the broader principle.
- Mixing degrees and radians in arc-length (l = rθ) and sector-area (A = ½r²θ) problems.

#### Practice Prompts

1. The equation x² + y² + kx + 4y + 9 = 0 represents a point-circle. Find k.
2. A cyclic quadrilateral ABCD has ∠A = 65°. Without drawing, determine ∠C and justify with the relevant theorem.
