# MM4 Trigonometry

MM4 runs from the two rules that solve triangles to the periodicity that lets you solve a trigonometric equation at all, and the specification sets six requirements that map onto that journey: the sine and cosine rules with the triangle area formula and the ambiguous case, radian measure for arc length and sectors, the exact values, the graphs and symmetries, two identities, and solution of equations in a given interval.

Read MM4.6 carefully, because it tells you what the last of those means. It gives three worked examples, and all three specify an interval — $-\pi < x < \pi$, $-2\pi < x < 2\pi$, $0^\circ < x < 360^\circ$. **The interval is part of the question.** An answer is wrong without it.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> Exact values are a lookup, and the rules are a lookup. The skill is knowing which one you need.

| $\theta$ | $0^\circ$ | $30^\circ$ | $45^\circ$ | $60^\circ$ | $90^\circ$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\sin\theta$ | $0$ | $\tfrac12$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$ | $1$ |
| $\cos\theta$ | $1$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$ | $\tfrac12$ | $0$ |
| $\tan\theta$ | $0$ | $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}$ | $1$ | $\sqrt3$ | undefined |

**The four rules.**

$$\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C} \qquad\text{(sine rule)}$$

$$a^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc\cos A \qquad\text{(cosine rule)}$$

$$\text{Area} = \tfrac12 ab\sin C \qquad\qquad \sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$$

$$\tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}, \qquad s = r\theta, \qquad \text{sector area} = \tfrac12 r^2\theta$$

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Choosing between the two rules

Both rules relate sides to angles, and picking the wrong one wastes minutes you do not have.

**Sine rule** — use when you have a pair of opposite items (a side and the angle across from it) plus one more. It converts everything into the same constant.

**Cosine rule** — use when you have three sides and no angle, or two sides with the angle *between* them. It finds the third side.

Worked, cosine rule: with $b = 5$, $c = 7$ and the included angle $A = 60^\circ$,

$$a^2 = 25 + 49 - 2\cdot5\cdot7\cdot\tfrac12 = 74 - 35 = 39 \implies a = \sqrt{39}$$

Worked, area: the same triangle has area $\tfrac12 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot \sin 60^\circ = \tfrac{35\sqrt3}{4}$.

#### The ambiguous case is the specification's own word

MM4.1 says the sine rule "includes an understanding of the 'ambiguous' case (angle–side–side)". This is SSA data: an angle, a side, and another side. The procedure is fixed.

1. Compute $\sin B = \dfrac{b\sin A}{a}$.
2. If that exceeds 1, **no triangle exists**.
3. Otherwise $B$ has two candidates, $\theta$ and $180^\circ - \theta$.
4. Keep each candidate only if $A + B < 180^\circ$.

Example: $A = 30^\circ$, $a = 4$, $b = 6$. Then $\sin B = \dfrac{6 \cdot \tfrac12}{4} = \tfrac34$, so $B \approx 48.6^\circ$ or $131.4^\circ$. With $A = 30^\circ$, both give $A+B < 180^\circ$ — $78.6^\circ$ and $161.4^\circ$ — so **two triangles exist**. Contrast $A = 30^\circ$, $a = 7$, $b = 5$: $\sin B = \dfrac{5 \cdot \tfrac12}{7} = \tfrac{5}{14}$, so $B \approx 20.9^\circ$ or $159.1^\circ$, and $30^\circ + 159.1^\circ > 180^\circ$, so the second is discarded and only **one** triangle exists. The paper has no penalty for wrong answers, but this is still a wasted item — decide before you commit.

The specification also says problems might be set in two or three dimensions, which means you should be willing to drop a perpendicular and work in a cross-section rather than trying to apply a rule to a skew triangle.

#### Radians are not a formality

MM4.2 requires radian measure for arc length and for the areas of sectors and segments. Arc length is $s = r\theta$ with $\theta$ in radians, and sector area is $\tfrac12 r^2\theta$. Segment area is $\tfrac12 r^2(\theta - \sin\theta)$, which is the sector minus the triangle — on the unit circle that is $\tfrac12(\theta - \sin\theta)$.

Worked, unit circle, $\theta = \tfrac{\pi}{3}$: arc length $\tfrac{\pi}{3}$, sector area $\tfrac{\pi}{6}$, segment area $\tfrac12\left(\tfrac{\pi}{3} - \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)$.

A quarter circle is $\theta = \tfrac{\pi}{2}$ radians, not $90$ — inserting $90$ gives an answer 57 times too large. If an item gives degrees and expects a radian formula, convert first.

#### Graphs, symmetries, periodicity

$y = \sin x$ has period $2\pi$ and is symmetric about the origin (odd); $y = \cos x$ has period $2\pi$ and is symmetric about the $y$-axis (even); $y = \tan x$ has period $\pi$, half as long, because $\tan(x+\pi) = \tan x$. That halved period is the single most useful fact for MM4.6, and it is why an interval of $-2\pi < x < 2\pi$ contains eight full periods of tangent but only four of sine.

#### The identities do real work

$\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$ lets you replace a square, and $\tan\theta = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$ lets you convert. Together they are enough to solve every equation the specification offers.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (3mo+)

#### Solving in a given interval

Three specification examples, worked through.

**$\tan x = -\tfrac13$ for $-\pi < x < \pi$.** Tangent is negative in the second and fourth quadrants, so there are two answers:

$$x = \pi - \arctan\tfrac13 \qquad\text{and}\qquad x = -\arctan\tfrac13$$

**$\sin^2(2x + 3\pi) = \tfrac12$ for $-2\pi < x < 2\pi$.** First simplify: $\sin(\theta + 3\pi) = -\sin\theta$, and squaring removes the sign, so this is $\sin^2 2x = \tfrac12$. Then $\sin^2 u = \tfrac12 \iff u = \tfrac{\pi}{4} + \tfrac{k\pi}{2}$, giving $x = \tfrac{\pi}{8} + \tfrac{k\pi}{4}$. The condition $-\tfrac{11\pi}{8} < \tfrac{k\pi}{4} < \tfrac{27\pi}{8}$ gives $k = -2, -1, 0, \ldots, 13$ — sixteen solutions, which matches the interval being eight periods of $\sin^2(2x)$ with two hits each.

**$12\cos^2 x + 6\sin x - 10 = 2$ for $0^\circ < x < 360^\circ$.** Replace the squared cosine using the identity:

$$12(1 - \sin^2 x) + 6\sin x - 10 = 2 \implies \sin x(6 - 12\sin x) = 0$$

So $\sin x = 0$ or $\sin x = \tfrac12$, giving $x = 180^\circ$, $30^\circ$ and $150^\circ$. Three answers, all inside the interval. Note the mixed units — the interval is in degrees while the equation is solved with pure algebra — and note that stopping at the first factor would have cost a mark.

#### The step that decides almost every item

Before substituting anything, work out how many solutions the interval *permits*, and check you have found them all. $\sin x = \tfrac12$ has one solution per $360^\circ$; $\sin^2 x = \tfrac12$ has two. $\tan x = \tfrac13$ has one per $180^\circ$. A count that disagrees with the interval is the fastest way to catch a lost branch.

#### Where the routine runs out

Three cases resist the standard methods. A sine rule question where the ambiguous case produces two valid triangles cannot be resolved by algebra — you have to decide which the context accepts, or return both. A three-dimensional problem usually reduces to a two-dimensional cross-section, and finding the right one is the difficulty. And an equation in a doubled angle, $\cos 2x$ or $\sin 2x$, has period $\pi$ rather than $2\pi$ and roughly doubles the number of solutions in a fixed interval, which is where most candidates under-count.

### Common mistakes

Mixing degrees and radians, especially in the arc-length and sector-area formulas.

Reporting one solution where the interval holds two — the dominant error in MM4.6.

Forgetting that tangent's period is $\pi$, not $2\pi$.

Writing $\sin(180^\circ - x) = -\sin x$. It is $+\sin x$; the sign flips for $\sin(180^\circ + x)$ and $\sin(-x)$.

Dropping the second candidate in the ambiguous case — and equally, accepting one whose angles already exceed $180^\circ$.

Substituting a degree value into $s = r\theta$ and getting a nonsense arc.

Using the cosine rule where the sine rule was needed, or vice versa.

Stopping at the first factor of a factored trigonometric equation when there are two.
