# Factors and Multiples (HCF and LCM)

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A **factor** of a whole number *n* is any integer that divides *n* exactly (remainder 0). Every integer ≥ 2 has at least the factors **1 and itself**, and only finitely many factors. A **multiple** of *n* is the result of multiplying *n* by a whole number (1, 2, 3, …), so multiples are infinite. The **Highest Common Factor (HCF)** — also called Greatest Common Divisor (GCD) — is the largest number that divides two or more given numbers without a remainder. The **Lowest Common Multiple (LCM)** is the smallest positive integer that is a multiple of each given number. The shortcut relationship to memorise is **HCF × LCM = product of two numbers**, which lets you find one once the other is known. For NCEE, expect 1–2 questions testing listing of factors/multiples, finding HCF or LCM by prime factorisation, and one short word problem on equal sharing or repeating events.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Definitions and Basic Properties
A number **a** is a factor of **b** if b ÷ a leaves no remainder. Multiples are the reverse: **b** is a multiple of **a** whenever b = a × k for some whole number k. The set of factors of 12 is {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12}; the set of multiples of 12 starts {12, 24, 36, 48, …} and never ends.

#### Prime Factorisation Method
Break each number into primes using index notation, then:

- **HCF** = product of each **common** prime raised to its **lowest** power appearing in any number.
- **LCM** = product of each prime (common or not) raised to its **highest** power appearing in any number.

*Worked pair:* 72 = 2³ × 3² and 90 = 2 × 3² × 5.
HCF(72, 90) = 2¹ × 3² = **18**. LCM(72, 90) = 2³ × 3² × 5¹ = **360**.

#### Division Method
For HCF of two numbers, divide the larger by the smaller, then divide the smaller by the remainder, repeating until the remainder is 0; the last non-zero divisor is the HCF. For LCM of small numbers, successive division by common primes works similarly.

#### Useful Identity
For two numbers **a** and **b**: **HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b**. This identity is the fastest way to answer NCEE "find the missing value" questions once any three of the four quantities are given.

#### Co-prime Numbers
Two numbers are **co-prime** (or relatively prime) when their HCF is **1**, e.g. 8 and 15. For co-prime pairs, LCM equals the product of the numbers.

#### Typical NCEE Question Patterns
- Listing all factors of a number under 100.
- "Find the greatest number that divides 36 and 84 leaving remainder 0" — answer is HCF.
- "Two bells ring every 6 seconds and 8 seconds; when do they ring together again?" — answer is LCM.
- Word problems on arranging items into equal rows/groups (HCF) and aligning repeating cycles (LCM).

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Special Behaviour
- **HCF and LCM with zero:** undefined — division by zero is not allowed, so the HCF or LCM of any number with 0 is the non-zero number itself, never a division-by-zero product.
- **Three or more numbers:** the identity HCF × LCM = product does **not** hold beyond two numbers. For three numbers, HCF(a, b, c) × LCM(a, b, c) ≠ a × b × c in general; rely on prime factorisation instead.
- **Scaling rule:** if HCF(a, b) = h, then HCF(ka, kb) = k·h and LCM(ka, kb) = k·LCM(a, b). This often appears in NCEE "what happens when both numbers are tripled" extensions.
- **Perfect squares:** when a and b are perfect squares, their HCF and LCM are also perfect squares; for cube numbers, the same holds for cubes.
- **Even and odd factors:** the largest odd factor of an even number 2ⁿ × m (where m is odd) is **m**, obtainable by stripping the powers of 2 — a frequent NCEE trick.

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics
HCF and LCM link directly to **fractions** (lowest terms uses HCF of numerator and denominator), **ratio** (simplifying a ratio is an HCF operation), **time and calendar problems** (LCM of intervals), and **mensuration** (tiles and rods problems use HCF of dimensions).

#### Common Mistakes
1. Treating "common multiple" as the first match instead of the **smallest** positive one.
2. Forgetting that **1** is a factor of every integer — students often start factor lists at 2.
3. Using the **highest** power for HCF (wrong) and the **lowest** for LCM (wrong); always re-check which way round.
4. Applying HCF × LCM = product to three numbers.
5. Misreading "leaves remainder 3" problems — first subtract 3 from each, **then** find the HCF.

#### Practice Prompts
1. *Without dividing, find HCF and LCM of 324 and 144.* — Use prime factorisation: 324 = 2² × 3⁴, 144 = 2⁴ × 3². HCF = 2² × 3² = 36; LCM = 2⁴ × 3⁴ = 1296. Verify: 36 × 1296 = 46656 = 324 × 144 ✔.
2. *Three traffic lights change every 12, 18 and 24 seconds. After how many seconds will all three change together for the first time?* — LCM(12, 18, 24). Prime forms: 2²×3, 2×3², 2³×3 → LCM = 2³ × 3² = **72 seconds**.

#### NCEE Strategy
This topic carries roughly 4 % of the paper weight (about 2 questions). Spend at most 4 minutes per item. Always write the prime factorisation explicitly — it is the most reliable method and earns full method marks even if a final arithmetic slip occurs.
