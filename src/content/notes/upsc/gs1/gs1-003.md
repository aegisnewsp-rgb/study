# Geography World

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Distinction that decides marks: Wegener's *continental drift* described Pangaea's breakup; *plate tectonics* (Holmes–Hess) gave it the mantle-convection mechanism. UPSC tests this gap every few years.

World Geography in UPSC GS1 is the study of planetary systems — atmosphere, lithosphere, hydrosphere and biosphere — and how physical processes distribute resources, climates and settlements across the globe. Weightage hovers near 3% in the paper, but questions cluster in monsoon, ENSO, pressure belts and ocean currents.

- **Lithosphere** — plate tectonics, three boundary types (divergent → ridges/rifts; convergent → trenches/fold mountains; transform → strike-slip faults).
- **Atmosphere** — troposphere → stratosphere → mesosphere → thermosphere; lapse rate ≈ 6.5 °C/km in troposphere.
- **Hydrosphere** — thermohaline circulation, ENSO, salinity ≈ 35 ‰ open-ocean average.
- **Biosphere** — biomes (tundra, taiga, savanna, steppe, rainforest, Mediterranean scrub).
- **Solar insolation** on a horizontal surface: **I = S sin h**, where **S** = solar constant ≈ 1.366 kW/m² and **h** = sun's altitude angle in degrees; units of **I**: W/m².

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook — "T.S.T.F" for atmospheric layers from surface up:** **T**roposphere, **S**tratosphere, **M**esosphere, **T**hermosphere (with ionosphere overlapping). Ozone lives in the stratosphere; weather lives in the troposphere.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Core Mechanisms You Must Derive, Not Memorise

The Coriolis effect steers every moving parcel on a rotating Earth. Per unit mass, the acceleration is **a = 2 v Ω sin φ**, where **v** = parcel speed (m/s), **Ω** = Earth's angular velocity ≈ 7.2921 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s, and **φ** = latitude. Zero at the equator, maximum at the poles; that's why trade winds deflect right in the Northern Hemisphere and left in the Southern.

Pressure-gradient force and Coriolis balance in the free atmosphere give the **geostrophic wind**: **f V_g = (1/ρ) · ∂P/∂n**, where **f** = 2Ω sin φ (s⁻¹), **V_g** = geostrophic wind (m/s), **ρ** = air density (kg/m³), **∂P/∂n** = horizontal pressure gradient (Pa/m). This is why upper-air winds blow parallel to isobars, not across them.

The global energy budget starts with solar insolation **I = S sin h**. Latitude controls the sine term: low h near poles → low I → cold poles; high h near the ITCZ → high I → rising air → rain belts. Heat is then redistributed by three atmospheric cells (Hadley, Ferrel, Polar) and ocean currents.

#### Salinity, Currents and ENSO at a Glance

| Variable / Concept | Definition | Typical value | Unit / notation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open-ocean salinity | Total dissolved solids in seawater | 33–37, global avg ≈ 35 | ‰ (g/kg) |
| Chlorinity–salinity (Knudsen) | Empirical link used to compute salinity from titration | S (‰) = 0.030 + 1.805 × Cl | dimensionless ‰ |
| Solar constant | Solar flux at top of atmosphere, perpendicular to rays | ≈ 1.366 kW/m² | W/m² |
| Thermohaline circulation | Deep-ocean overturning driven by density (T + S) | Timescale ~1000 years | — |
| ENSO warm phase (El Niño) | Sea-surface warming in eastern equatorial Pacific | SOI becomes strongly negative | index, dimensionless |

Warm currents flow poleward along western ocean margins (Gulf Stream, Kuroshio, Brazil, East Australian); cold currents flow equatorward along eastern margins (Peru/Humboldt, Benguela, California, Oyashio). The west-wind drift in the Southern Ocean runs east, unimpeded by land — the only zonal current on the planet.

#### Climate Types UPSC Loves to Confuse

- **Tropical monsoon** (Köppen *Am*): wet summer from wind reversal, dry winter, annual rainfall > 1500 mm.
- **Mediterranean** (*Csa/Csb*): dry summer, wet winter; western coasts of continents between roughly 30°–45° N/S.
- **Savanna** (*Aw*): tropical wet-and-dry grassland with scattered deciduous trees, not the same as **steppe** (*BS*), which is temperate semi-arid grass with no trees.
- **Taiga** (*Dfc*): boreal coniferous forest south of tundra.
- **Tundra** (*ET*): treeless, permafrost-underlain, polar or alpine.

#### Worked Problem (Prelims-style)

> **Q.** Westerlies in the Southern Hemisphere are stronger and more persistent than those in the Northern Hemisphere. Which of the following best explains this?
>
> (A) Greater landmass in the Northern Hemisphere increases surface friction
> (B) Higher Coriolis force at southern mid-latitudes
> (C) Greater temperature contrast between equator and pole in the south
> (D) Stronger subtropical high pressure in the Southern Hemisphere

**Resolution.** Wind strength scales with the pressure gradient. The Southern Hemisphere has far less land between 40°–60° S, so there is no continental heating contrast to break the thermal gradient — the polar–equatorial temperature difference stays steep and uninterrupted. The Southern Ocean is a continuous fetch, allowing stronger, more sustained westerlies ("Roaring Forties", "Furious Fifties"). Northern Hemisphere westerlies are broken into cells by continents and weakened by friction over land.

Answer: **(C)**.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Option (B) is a trap — Coriolis is actually *weaker* near the equator and stronger toward the poles, but in the 40°–60° belt of westerlies the two hemispheres have nearly the same value of sin φ. The land–sea asymmetry, not Coriolis, is the differentiator.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### Edge Cases and Boundary Conditions

Pressure belts do not sit at fixed latitudes. They migrate with the **thermal equator**, which lags the Sun by 2–3 weeks over land because ground heats and cools slowly. Result: the ITCZ sits near 5°–10° N in July (the "monsoon trough" over India) and dips south of the equator in January. Maps drawn from equinox assumptions alone lose this tilt and misrepresent monsoon onset.

Geostrophic balance breaks near the equator where **f → 0**. There, the pressure-gradient force is balanced not by Coriolis but by **centrifugal force in cyclostrophic flow** (e.g. tropical cyclones near their core) and by **friction in the boundary layer**. This is why equatorial latitudes experience easterly trade winds, not geostrophic westerlies.

Salinity deviates from the 35 ‰ benchmark in two predictable ways: it rises in subtropical highs (≈ 20°–30°) where evaporation exceeds precipitation and runoff (e.g. Red Sea ≈ 40 ‰), and falls near the ITCZ and at high latitudes where precipitation and ice melt dominate. ENSO disturbs the eastern Pacific cold tongue: warm phase suppresses upwelling off Peru, killing fisheries; La Niña intensifies it, strengthening the Walker circulation.

#### Common Mistakes That Cost Marks

1. Reversing ENSO phases — El Niño = warm eastern Pacific + weaker Indian monsoon; La Niña = cool eastern Pacific + stronger Indian monsoon in many years, modulated by the Indian Ocean Dipole.
2. Calling the **Coriolis force** a real force — it is an inertial (fictitious) acceleration seen in the rotating frame.
3. Locating ocean currents on the wrong side — warm currents on the **western** margins, cold on the **eastern**, by gyre geometry.
4. Treating the **westerlies** and **trade winds** as one system — they belong to different cells (Ferrel vs Hadley) and blow in opposite zonal directions.
5. Equating **Mediterranean climate** with monsoon climate because both have a wet season — they have opposite wet seasons.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. Explain with diagrams how the shift of pressure belts between January and July produces the Indian summer monsoon, and why the onset line advances northwestward in stages.
2. Discuss how ENSO and the Indian Ocean Dipole together influence rainfall over India in any given monsoon season, citing at least two recent years as evidence.
