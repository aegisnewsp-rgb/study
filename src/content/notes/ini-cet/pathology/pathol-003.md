# Cell Injury

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Cell injury** is the sequence of events triggered when a noxious stimulus exceeds a cell's adaptive capacity, progressing along the continuum **reversible injury → irreversible injury → cell death**. Reversible injury manifests as **cellular (hydropic) swelling** from failed Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and **fatty change (steatosis)** from triglyceride accumulation. Irreversible injury is defined by **mitochondrial permeability transition** and **plasma membrane rupture**, the biochemical "point of no return". Cell death occurs by **necrosis** (uncontrolled, ATP-independent, inflammatory) or **apoptosis** (programmed, energy-dependent, non-inflammatory). The five core mechanisms are **ATP depletion, mitochondrial damage, calcium influx, oxidative stress (free radical injury), and membrane damage**. INI CET high-yield: brain infarct = **liquefactive** necrosis (NOT coagulative); TB = **caseous**; pancreatic fat necrosis; immune vasculitis = **fibrinoid**.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Mechanisms of Cell Injury
Five mechanistically linked pathways converge on cell death:

1. **ATP depletion** — ischemia/hypoxia cripples oxidative phosphorylation; Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase failure → hydropic swelling; switch to anaerobic glycolysis → lactate, ↓ intracellular pH → chromatin clumping.
2. **Mitochondrial damage** — opening of the **mitochondrial permeability transition pore (MPTP)** releases cytochrome c, a hallmark of irreversible injury.
3. **Calcium influx** — cytosolic Ca²⁺ activates phospholipases, proteases, endonucleases, and ATPases, digesting membranes and cytoskeleton.
4. **Oxidative stress** — **reactive oxygen species (ROS)** generated by xanthine oxidase, mitochondria, and leukocytes attack lipids (lipid peroxidation), proteins, and DNA.
5. **Membrane damage** — the executing lesion of irreversible injury.

#### Reversible vs Irreversible Injury
| Feature | Reversible | Irreversible |
|---|---|---|
| Gross | Swollen, pale organ | Loss of nuclei, membrane rupture |
| Ultrastructure | Cell swelling, ribosome detachment, fatty change | MPTP opening, severe membrane damage, Ca²⁺-activated enzyme digestion |
| ATP | Depleted but partially restored | Cannot be restored |
| Outcome | Recovery if stimulus removed | Cell death — necrosis or apoptosis |

#### Patterns of Necrosis
- **Coagulative** — ischemia in solid organs (heart, kidney, spleen); protein denaturation preserves cell outline 1–2 days.
- **Liquefactive** — **brain infarct** and bacterial abscesses; hydrolytic enzymes digest tissue into pus.
- **Caseous** — tuberculosis; "cheese-like" friable debris with granulomas.
- **Fat** — pancreas (released lipases), breast trauma; chalky white saponification.
- **Fibrinoid** — immune vasculitis, malignant hypertension; pink fibrin-like vessel wall deposits.
- **Gangrenous** — limb ischemia ± bacterial superinfection (dry vs wet).

#### Apoptosis
- **Intrinsic (mitochondrial)** pathway — Bax/Bak, cytochrome c → APAF-1 → **caspase-9** → executioner caspases-3, -6, -7.
- **Extrinsic (death receptor)** pathway — Fas/FasL or TNF/TNFR → FADD → **caspase-8** → executioner caspases.
- Morphology: cell shrinkage, chromatin condensation (pyknosis), karyorrhexis, **apoptotic bodies** phagocytosed without inflammation.

#### Intracellular Accumulations
- **Lipids** — steatosis (fatty liver, atherosclerosis with cholesterol clefts).
- **Proteins** — hyaline change, Russell bodies (plasma cells), Mallory bodies (alcoholic hepatitis).
- **Glycogen** — diabetes, glycogen storage diseases.
- **Pigments** — lipofuscin ("wear-and-tear" pigment), hemosiderin, melanin, bilirubin, anthracosis.

#### INI CET Question Patterns
- "Type of necrosis in brain infarct?" → liquefactive.
- "Point of no return in cell injury?" → mitochondrial permeability transition.
- "Apoptosis vs necrosis" — comparison MCQs with caspase identifiers.
- "Pigment in Kupffer cells of aging liver?" → lipofuscin.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury
Restoration of blood flow paradoxically worsens injury through (1) **ROS burst** from accumulated hypoxanthine + xanthine oxidase, (2) **calcium paradox** as mitochondrial Ca²⁺ overload triggers MPTP, and (3) recruited neutrophils amplifying oxidative damage. Clinically relevant in MI thrombolysis and organ transplantation.

#### Free Radical Pathogenesis
ROS include superoxide (O₂·⁻), hydroxyl (·OH), and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). Lipid peroxidation propagates as a chain reaction disrupting membranes. **Antioxidant defenses**: superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase, catalase, vitamin E, and ceruloplasmin. Pathology traces free radical damage to atherosclerosis, aging, chemical/radiation injury, and oxygen toxicity (retrolental fibroplasia in neonates).

#### Common Mistakes
- Confusing **apoptotic bodies with Councilman bodies** — Councilman bodies are apoptotic hepatocytes in viral hepatitis/yellow fever.
- Stating "brain infarct → coagulative necrosis" — the CNS is liquefactive due to high lysosomal lipid content and minimal stromal support.
- Calling **hemosiderin** the "wear-and-tear pigment" — that descriptor belongs to **lipofuscin** (residual body from lipid peroxidation).
- Assuming **fatty change is irreversible** — it is the classic reversible lesion, demonstrated in alcoholic and CCCl₄ hepatic steatosis.

#### Worked Micro-Example
A 55-year-old develops left middle cerebral artery occlusion → 24 hours later, imaging shows an infarct. **Mechanism**: ischemia → ATP depletion → anaerobic glycolysis → lactic acidosis → MPTP opening → release of hydrolytic enzymes from abundant lysosomes → tissue digested into fluid-filled cavity (liquefactive necrosis). Contrast with a renal cortical infarct, where ischemia produces **coagulative necrosis** with preserved cellular outlines for days.

#### Practice Prompts
1. A TB lymph node biopsy shows structureless eosinophilic debris. Identify the necrosis type, list three features distinguishing it from liquefactive necrosis, and name the immune cells forming the surrounding granuloma.
2. A patient receives streptomycin; oxidative stress generates ROS. Trace how lipid peroxidation damages the hepatocyte plasma membrane, list two enzymatic antioxidants, and identify the MPTP consequence marking irreversible injury.
