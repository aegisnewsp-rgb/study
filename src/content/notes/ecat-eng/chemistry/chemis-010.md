# Organic Chemistry — ECAT (Engineering)

Organic Chemistry is the ECAT (Engineering) topic where your knowledge of functional groups, nomenclature and reaction mechanisms gets tested across the full spread of alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, aromatics, alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, amines and their derivatives. The MCQs mix three styles: identifying the product of a given transformation, naming or sketching an IUPAC structure, and ranking reactivity among related substrates. Success on this topic rests on three pillars: a clean mental map of functional-group transformations (alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid, for example), the ability to read and write reaction mechanisms with curved arrows showing electron flow, and fluency with IUPAC nomenclature. The topic also leans on hybridisation and intermolecular forces from Chemical Bonding.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h-1d)

**Functional groups at a glance.** Know how to spot each one and what it does.

| Class | Functional group | Typical suffix/prefix | Quick reactivity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkane | C–C, C–H only | -ane | Free-radical halogenation, combustion |
| Alkene | C=C | -ene | Electrophilic addition (HX, H₂O, X₂) |
| Alkyne | C≡C | -yne | Electrophilic addition, acidic terminal H |
| Aromatic | Benzene ring | -benzene | Electrophilic aromatic substitution |
| Alcohol | –OH on C | -ol | Oxidation, substitution, dehydration |
| Aldehyde | –CHO | -al | Oxidation to carboxylic acid, reduction to alcohol |
| Ketone | >C=O | -one | Reduction, no easy oxidation |
| Carboxylic acid | –COOH | -oic acid | Acid-base, esterification, reduction |
| Ester | –COOR | -oate | Hydrolysis, transesterification |
| Amine | –NH₂, –NHR, –NR₂ | -amine | Base, nucleophile |

**IUPAC naming — quick rules.**
1. Find the longest carbon chain containing the principal functional group.
2. Number the chain to give the lowest locants to (a) the principal functional group, then (b) double/triple bonds, then (c) substituents.
3. Name the substituents in alphabetical order, with their locants.
4. Combine as: locants-substituents + parent-chain-locants-unsaturation-principal-suffix.

Example: 4-bromo-3-methylhex-1-ene is a six-carbon chain with a double bond at C1–C2, a methyl at C3, and a bromine at C4.

**Isomerism recap.**
- **Structural (constitutional):** same formula, different connectivity — chain, positional, functional-group.
- **Stereoisomers:** same connectivity, different 3D — geometric (cis-trans in alkenes) and optical (enantiomers around a chiral centre).

**Three mechanism families.**
- **Free radical** — alkane + X₂ in UV light; arrow pushing starts with homolytic bond cleavage.
- **Electrophilic addition** — alkene + HX, X₂, H₂O/H⁺; arrow pushing from the C=C π bond to the electrophile.
- **Nucleophilic substitution** — alkyl halide + OH⁻/CN⁻/NH₃; proceeds by SN1 (tertiary, two-step via carbocation) or SN2 (primary, concerted with inversion).

**Quick check before the exam.**
- Can you draw and name the three isomers of C₅H₁₂?
- Can you write the product of CH₃CH=CH₂ + HBr (Markovnikov's rule)?
- Can you identify the chiral centre in 2-bromobutane?

### 🟡 Standard — Exam Prep (3d-3w)

#### Nomenclature and isomers

IUPAC rules have a strict priority order: principal characteristic group (highest), then unsaturation, then substituents. Carboxylic acid > ester > amide > nitrile > aldehyde > ketone > alcohol > amine > alkene > alkyne > halide > nitro. If the molecule contains none of these, the principal characteristic group is whatever is most oxidised; alkenes and alkynes become the suffix and other groups become prefixes.

Worked example — drawing the IUPAC name of a complex structure:

**Compound:** CH₃–CH(OH)–CH(Br)–CH₃ (four carbons, hydroxyl on C2, bromine on C3).
- Longest chain containing OH: 4 carbons → butane.
- Principal characteristic group: –OH → suffix "-ol", locant 2 (number from the end closer to OH).
- Substituent: –Br → prefix "bromo", locant 3.
- IUPAC name: **3-bromobutan-2-ol**.

**Isomers.** For C₅H₁₂, three chain isomers exist: pentane, 2-methylbutane, and 2,2-dimethylpropane. For C₄H₈, the isomers include the four butenes (1-butene, cis-2-butene, trans-2-butene, isobutylene) plus cyclobutane and methylcyclopropane. Count carefully: a question asking "how many isomers of C₆H₁₂" needs both chain and ring isomers.

**Chirality and enantiomers.** A carbon is chiral if it is bonded to four different groups. Look for C bonded to: –H, –CH₃, –CH₂CH₃, –Cl → chiral. C bonded to: –H, –H, –CH₃, –Cl → not chiral. The R/S assignment uses the Cahn-Ingold-Prelog (CIP) priority rules on atoms directly bonded to the chiral centre; equal-priority atoms break the tie by going to the next shell.

#### Alkanes and free-radical halogenation

Alkanes are saturated, sp³ hybridised, and relatively unreactive. The main reaction is **free-radical halogenation**: alkane + X₂ + UV light → alkyl halide + HX. The mechanism is **initiation** (X₂ → 2X• under UV), **propagation** (X• + RH → HX + R•; R• + X₂ → RX + X•), and **termination** (R• + R• → R–R; R• + X• → RX; X• + X• → X₂).

Selectivity order for halogenation: tertiary C–H > secondary > primary > methyl. So 2-methylpropane + Cl₂ → mostly 2-chloro-2-methylpropane (tert-butyl chloride). Fluorination is so exothermic it is uncontrolled; bromination is so selective that it picks the weakest C–H bond almost exclusively; chlorination is intermediate.

**Combustion.** CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ + (3n+1)/2 O₂ → n CO₂ + (n+1) H₂O. For methane: CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O; ΔH ≈ −890 kJ/mol.

#### Alkenes and electrophilic addition

Alkenes are sp² hybridised with a π bond perpendicular to the molecular plane. The π electrons are accessible to electrophiles; this is why alkenes undergo **electrophilic addition** so readily.

**Markovnikov's rule:** when HX adds to an unsymmetrical alkene, H goes to the carbon with more H's already (giving the more stable carbocation intermediate). Example: propene + HBr → 2-bromopropane (Markovnikov), not 1-bromopropane (anti-Markovnikov). Peroxide reverses this to **anti-Markovnikov** (the Kharasch effect) via a radical mechanism — for HBr only.

**Specific additions.**
- Alkene + H₂/cat (Pt, Pd, Ni) → alkane (catalytic hydrogenation).
- Alkene + H₂O/H⁺ → alcohol (Markovnikov).
- Alkene + X₂ → vicinal dihalide (anti-addition via halonium ion intermediate).
- Alkene + cold dilute KMnO₄ → vicinal diol (syn-addition).
- Alkene + hot acidic KMnO₄ → cleavage to ketones/carboxylic acids (depending on substitution).

#### Alkynes and acidic hydrogen

Alkynes have a C≡C triple bond and (for terminal alkynes) an acidic terminal ≡C–H with pKa ≈ 25. This acidity allows terminal alkynes to be deprotonated by NaNH₂ (sodium amide) to form acetylide anions, which then attack primary alkyl halides to extend the carbon chain.

**Catalytic hydrogenation** of an alkyne with Pd or Pt gives the alkane; with Lindlar's catalyst (Pd/CaCO₃ poisoned with Pb) the reaction stops at the **cis-alkene**; with Na or Li in liquid NH₃ it gives the **trans-alkene**.

#### Aromatic compounds and electrophilic aromatic substitution

Benzene is sp² hybridised, planar, with 6 π electrons delocalised in a ring above and below the plane (Hückel's rule: 4n+2 π electrons in a planar cyclic system → aromatic). It does not undergo addition like an alkene; instead it undergoes **electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS)**, preserving aromaticity.

**Five canonical EAS reactions.**
- **Halogenation** (Cl₂, Br₂) with FeCl₃ or FeBr₃ as Lewis-acid catalyst.
- **Nitration** (conc. HNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄) → nitrobenzene.
- **Sulfonation** (conc. H₂SO₄ or fuming H₂SO₄) → benzenesulfonic acid.
- **Friedel-Crafts alkylation** (RX + AlCl₃) → alkylbenzene.
- **Friedel-Crafts acylation** (RCOCl + AlCl₃) → aryl ketone.

**Directing effects of substituents.** Electron-donating groups (–OH, –NH₂, –OCH₃, alkyl) are ortho/para directors and activators. Electron-withdrawing groups (–NO₂, –CN, –COOH, –SO₃H) are meta directors and deactivators. Halogens are ortho/para directors but deactivators. Predicting the major product requires combining directing effects when more than one substituent is present.

#### Carbonyl chemistry: aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids

Aldehydes and ketones have a polar C=O with the carbon as the electrophilic site. Nucleophilic addition is the canonical mechanism:
- Aldehyde/ketone + HCN → cyanohydrin (with base catalyst).
- Aldehyde/ketone + NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄ → alcohol (reductive).
- Aldehyde/ketone + 2,4-DNPH → orange/yellow precipitate (qualitative test for carbonyl).
- Aldehyde + Tollens' reagent (Ag(NH₃)₂⁺) → carboxylic acid + silver mirror.
- Aldehyde + Fehling's reagent (Cu²⁺-tartrate) → carboxylic acid + red Cu₂O precipitate.

**Carboxylic acids** are weak acids (pKa around 4–5 for most). They react with alcohols in the presence of acid catalyst to give **esters** (Fischer esterification), with NaHCO₃ to give CO₂ (a useful qualitative test), and with SOCl₂ or PCl₅ to give acyl chlorides (very reactive, used as intermediates).

#### Worked example

**Problem.** Identify the products (and name them) when propene (CH₃–CH=CH₂) is treated with (a) HBr, (b) H₂O/H₂SO₄, (c) Br₂ in CCl₄, (d) hot acidic KMnO₄.

**Solution.**
(a) **HBr** — Markovnikov addition → 2-bromopropane (CH₃–CHBr–CH₃). With peroxide, anti-Markovnikov → 1-bromopropane.

(b) **H₂O/H₂SO₄** — Markovnikov hydration → propan-2-ol (CH₃–CHOH–CH₃).

(c) **Br₂ in CCl₄** — anti-addition via bromonium ion → 1,2-dibromopropane (CH₃–CHBr–CH₂Br). The reaction is qualitative: bromine colour disappears.

(d) **Hot acidic KMnO₄** — oxidative cleavage of the C=C → since the alkene has =CH₂ at one end, that end gives CO₂; the other carbon (=CH–CH₃) becomes a carboxylic acid (acetic acid, CH₃COOH) and CO₂.

### 🔴 Deep — Mastery (1mo+)

#### Reaction mechanism arrows

Curved arrows show the movement of an electron pair (two electrons). A double-barbed arrow for two electrons, a single-barbed "fish-hook" arrow for one electron (radical mechanisms). Every arrow must start at a lone pair, a bond, or a π bond, and end at an atom, a bond, or a new bond position. Conservation of charge and octets must hold at every step.

For SN1 vs SN2:
- **SN1** (tert-butyl bromide + H₂O) → rate depends only on [substrate]. Two-step mechanism: ionisation to form a planar carbocation (sp²), then nucleophile attack from either face → racemisation at a chiral centre.
- **SN2** (CH₃Br + OH⁻) → rate depends on [substrate][nucleophile]. Concerted mechanism: nucleophile attacks from the back side opposite the leaving group → **Walden inversion** at the chiral centre (configuration flips, retention is impossible). SN2 is suppressed by steric bulk at the electrophilic carbon.

For E1 vs E2 elimination:
- **E1** competes with SN1 from the same carbocation; favours higher temperatures, weaker bases, tertiary substrates.
- **E2** competes with SN2; anti-periplanar geometry required; Zaitsev's rule gives the more substituted alkene.

#### Aromaticity rules

A molecule is aromatic if it is:
- Cyclic,
- Planar (or near-planar),
- Conjugated (every atom in the ring has a p orbital),
- Has 4n+2 π electrons (Hückel's rule: 2, 6, 10, 14, …).

If it is cyclic, planar, conjugated, and has 4n π electrons, it is **antiaromatic** (less stable than a non-aromatic reference). Cyclobutadiene (4 π e⁻) is antiaromatic; cyclooctatetraene (8 π e⁻) is non-aromatic because it puckers out of planarity. Heterocycles (furan, pyrrole, pyridine) follow the same rule, with the heteroatom lone pair either part of the π system (furan, pyrrole) or not (pyridine).

#### Stereochemistry beyond cis-trans

**E/Z designation** uses the CIP priority rules at each end of the double bond. The higher-priority groups on the same side give **Z**; on opposite sides give **E**. Cis/trans is a special case for alkenes with two identical substituents on at least one carbon.

**Conformations of cyclohexane.** Chair cyclohexane has six axial and six equatorial positions; substituents prefer equatorial to minimise 1,3-diaxial interactions. A substituent in axial on C1 sterically clashes with axial on C3 and C5 (1,3-diaxial). For a disubstituted cyclohexane (1,2-, 1,3-, 1,4-), the more stable conformation has the larger substituent equatorial. cis-1,2-disubstituted → one axial, one equatorial; trans-1,2 → both equatorial or both axial.

#### Practice prompts

1. **Naming.** Provide the IUPAC name for: (a) CH₃–CH(C₂H₅)–CH(CH₃)–COOH, (b) (CH₃)₃C–CH=CH–CH₃, (c) HO–C₆H₄–CH₂–CH₃ (the ortho, meta, and para isomers separately).
2. **Mechanism.** Write the full curved-arrow mechanism for the acid-catalysed esterification of acetic acid with ethanol, including every intermediate.
3. **Product prediction.** Predict the major product and name it: (a) toluene + conc. HNO₃/H₂SO₄, (b) nitrobenzene + Cl₂/FeCl₃, (c) cyclohexene + cold dilute KMnO₄.
4. **Chirality.** Identify the chiral centres and the number of stereoisomers in 2,3,4-trihydroxybutanal and in tartaric acid.

#### Connections to adjacent topics

Organic Chemistry is the most "applied" of the ECAT chemistry topics — it consumes everything from Chemical Bonding (hybridisation, polarity, intermolecular forces), Acid-Base Equilibria (the pKa of carboxylic acids, amines, phenols), Thermochemistry (heats of combustion, bond energies), and Kinetics (reaction rate laws, activation energy). Students who treat organic chemistry as a memorisation exercise struggle; those who see the underlying physical-organic logic — what the electrophile, nucleophile, leaving group, and reaction conditions are doing — find the topic manageable.

### Common traps

- **Confusing Markovnikov and anti-Markovnikov.** "Alkene + HBr → tertiary alkyl bromide" is only true without peroxide. With peroxide (radical initiator), the product is the primary alkyl bromide. ECAT questions are explicit about the conditions.
- **Writing "cis-2-butene" when the substituents are different.** Cis/trans only applies when each sp² carbon has two identical substituents. Use E/Z (or cis/trans only when both ends have a pair of identical substituents).
- **Believing that benzene "has alternating single and double bonds".** It has six identical C–C bonds (bond length 140 pm) due to delocalisation. Drawing the Kekulé structure with fixed double bonds is misleading and often loses a mark.
- **Treating Friedel-Crafts as universal.** It fails on strongly deactivated rings (nitrobenzene won't undergo F-C alkylation) and polyalkylation can occur because the alkyl group is itself activating. For mono-substitution, the acyl version (Friedel-Crafts acylation) is usually safer.
- **Forgetting that carboxylic acids need a strong acid catalyst to esterify.** Mixing an alcohol and a carboxylic acid at room temperature gives essentially no ester; you need H₂SO₄ or HCl gas and often heat. Without the catalyst, the equilibrium favours the acid + alcohol.
