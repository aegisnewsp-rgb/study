# Question Types — Form, Note, Table, Flow-Chart Completion — IELTS Listening Notes

Completion items are the most mechanical part of the IELTS Listening test, and that is exactly why they are worth the most preparation time. Every one of them works the same way: a text with gaps, a stated word limit, and an answer taken from the recording. The four forms differ only in the shape of the text you are filling.

IELTS groups them under a single heading. You "have to fill in gaps in an outline of part or all of the recording", and that outline "will focus on the main ideas/facts in the recording" (ielts.org, Listening test format). The outline may be a form, a set of notes, a table, or a flow chart. IELTS then states what the task tests: "This type of question focuses on the main points the person listening would naturally write down." That last clause is the whole brief. A gap wants what a careful listener would jot down, not a restatement of the recording in your own words.

The rules that catch candidates are published in one short block, and they are worth memorising exactly, because IELTS says you lose marks for breaking them:

- "You should read the instructions very carefully as the number of words or numbers you may use to fill the gaps can change."
- "A word limit is given, for example, 'NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER'."
- "You will lose the mark for writing more than the word limit."
- "Contracted words such as 'they're' will not be tested."
- "Hyphenated words such as 'check-in' count as single words."

One more line settles a perennial argument: "You do not have to change the words in the recording in any way." You are not required to rephrase. You are also not required to copy. The gap wants the recording's own words, within the limit.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Form completion.** A form, described by IELTS as "often used for facts, such as names".
- **Note completion.** "A set of notes: used to summarise information and show how different points relate to one another."
- **Table completion.** "A table: used to summarise information that can be divided into clear categories, e.g. place/time/price."
- **Flow-chart completion.** "A flow chart: used to summarise the stages in a process, with the direction of the process shown by arrows."
- **Two ways of answering.** IELTS says you may have to "select your answers from a list" or "select words from the recording which fit into gaps". The first is closed; the second is open and carries the word limit.
- **Word limit.** Stated per set, e.g. "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER". Going over loses the mark.
- **Hyphenated words** such as "check-in" count as single words.
- **Number of questions.** IELTS records this as "Variable" for every question type. Do not plan around a fixed count of table or note gaps.
- **Marking.** 1 mark per correct answer, no negative marking, result reported as a band in whole or half bands (ielts.org, Listening test format).

#### The five traps that cost marks on completion items

1. **One word over the limit.** The extra word costs the mark. "check-in guest house" fails a two-word limit.
2. **Writing the article.** A gap expecting "brown owl" fails if you write "the brown owl".
3. **Paraphrasing instead of using the recording's words.** Right meaning, wrong wording, no mark.
4. **Losing the order.** Questions follow recording order, so a skipped gap is not one you can return to.
5. **Assuming a table fills row by row.** IELTS guarantees only that questions follow the recording.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Form completion: facts, names and the spelled-out trap

A form collects discrete facts, most often in Part 1 of a conversation. The gap wants a surname, a number, an address component or a date. Three habits carry most of the marks.

**Read the label, not the blank.** The printed word above the blank constrains the answer more tightly than the blank does. "Postcode", "Telephone", "Membership number" each rule out a whole family of plausible words before the audio starts.

**Spellings get corrected.** The recording spells a name, mispronounces or self-corrects it, and the corrected form is the answer. IELTS built this into the task deliberately.

**Digits are usually safer than words.** A form slot for a telephone number or a date wants digits. Writing "twenty-five" where "25" is expected is a formatting error, not a vocabulary error.

#### Note completion: relations, not sequences

IELTS describes notes as the format that summarises information "and show[s] how different points relate to one another". That means the hierarchy on the page carries meaning: a main heading, sub-points beneath it, and gaps at more than one level. Read the hierarchy and you know whether the gap wants a category name or a detail belonging to one.

Notes also compress. The note-taking style means abbreviations and shortened phrases are normal on the page. Do not let the brevity of the printed notes convince you the recording is brief; the detail is spoken at length and you must expand it to the limit.

#### Table completion: find the column before you hear the row

Because a table summarises "information that can be divided into clear categories" (ielts.org), the category is the hard part and the cell contents are the easy part. Do the category work in the preview.

1. Read the column headings or the row labels. Write them out as a list.
2. Decide which axis the recording will move along — across the columns, or down the rows.
3. Predict the type of value each cell wants: a place, a price, a date, a duration, a feature.
4. Now listen for the signpost that names a category, then fill only that cell.

The classic error is filling cells in reading order while the speaker visits them in a different order. IELTS does not promise row-by-row alignment. It promises the questions are in recording order, so use the question numbers as your guide and let the table headings tell you which cell.

#### Flow-chart completion: arrows carry the sequence

IELTS describes a flow chart as summarising "the stages in a process, with the direction of the process shown by arrows". Read the arrows. A chart that runs left to right and one that runs top to bottom are the same process, but a chart with a branch or a return arrow means the recording backtracks, and candidates who assume a straight line fill the wrong box.

Gaps at the head and tail of a chart often want a verb phrase — what happens, not what something is. A gap after "Step 3: the sample is ___" wants a past participle or an action, not a noun.

#### The closed version: selecting from a list

Where IELTS says you may "select your answers from a list", the item becomes easier and the discipline moves. You cannot invent wording, so you are choosing among printed options. Read the list first, and watch for options that are near-identical. When two options differ by one word, the recording will contain both, and only one fits the arrow direction, the row label or the grammatical slot.

#### Word-limit discipline in practice

- Count before you write, not after.
- Hyphenated words such as "check-in" count as one (ielts.org).
- "30%" is one word; "30 percent" is two.
- A number plus a unit may be two words under "TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER".
- Contracted words such as "they're" are not tested, so if the recording uses one, the gap will not.
- Never spend the gap after the answer correcting capitalisation. The published rules for these items cover the word limit, contractions and hyphenation; capitalisation is not among them.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Why completion items separate band scores anyway

If completion items are mechanical, they should be the easiest marks in the test. They are not, for one reason: the word limit converts a comprehension problem into a writing problem, and that problem is invisible until the marker counts. A candidate who understood the answer perfectly and wrote three words for a two-word gap scores exactly what one who never heard it scores.

#### Diagnosing which completion habit is failing

Mark a practice set by looking at the shape of your errors, not just the count.

- **Answers right in meaning, wrong in length** — you are not reading the limit until after you write. Re-read the instruction block at the top of every set, aloud, before the audio plays.
- **Answers are your own words** — you are paraphrasing under pressure. Write the recording's word, even if it is the less natural one.
- **Table gaps correct only when they run left to right** — you are filling in reading order. Practise one table set per session, moving between columns deliberately.
- **Flow-chart gaps correct only in the first half** — the recording branched or backtracked and you assumed linearity. Draw the arrows before you listen.
- **Note-completion gaps correct only at the top level** — you are skipping the sub-points. Track one level deeper for a full week.
- **Whole sets lost after one bad item** — order discipline. Abandon the item instantly and resume at the next question number.

#### A six-week drill sequence

**Week 1 — limit-only.** Ignore the content. Write, count the words, adjust. The constraint is the exercise.
**Week 2 — trigger phrases.** Predict the words that should precede each answer. Whether you missed the item or mis-framed it becomes obvious.
**Week 3 — hierarchy.** Expand each note outline into a full sentence per sub-point before playing.
**Week 4 — table orientation.** Predict every cell's value type before playing; score on prediction, not just answers.
**Week 5 — flow-chart branching.** Draw the direction, mark every branch or return arrow, then play.
**Week 6 — full mock.** One official test on computer, typed, unpaused.

#### Edge cases worth rehearsing

1. **A gap wants a word the recording never says in that form.** The answer is there in a different grammatical form. Re-read the printed words around the gap.
2. **Two gaps want the same word.** Legal. Do not force variety.
3. **A list option is obviously too long.** Count before you choose; the longest option is rarely right under a tight limit.
4. **The recording corrects a number or a name.** The second figure is the answer.

### High-Yield — Form, Note, Table and Flow-Chart Completion

Eight real contexts that generate these four forms, and what each one usually asks of you.

1. **Membership or registration form.** Names, reference numbers, dates, fees. Digits and spelled-out surnames.
2. **Accommodation enquiry form.** Address, room type, length of stay, contact.
3. **Course enrolment notes.** A hierarchy of sub-points — topic, tutor, assessment.
4. **Facilities table in a local monologue.** IELTS gives place, time and price as the canonical table categories.
5. **Transport timetable table.** Times by destination, or destinations by time. Digits throughout.
6. **Process flow-chart.** How something is made, delivered, reviewed or recycled.
7. **Technical flow-chart in a lecture.** Stages of a process, with a branch where conditions apply.
8. **Research notes.** A hierarchical summary of a discussion, with gaps at both levels.

The first three are transactional and sit most often in Part 1. The rest are academic and usually sit in Parts 2, 3 and 4. Practise both registers, because the answer style differs: transactional items want exact facts, academic items want category names and stage verbs.

### Previous Year Patterns — IELTS Listening Completion

The structural facts are published and do not move: four parts of 10 questions, 40 in total, approximately 30 minutes, 1 mark per correct answer, no negative marking. The four completion forms sit in the standing question-type list, which ielts.org enumerates as form completion, note completion, table completion and flow-chart completion, alongside multiple choice, matching, plan/map/diagram labelling, summary completion, sentence completion and short answer.

What does not stay fixed is the mix. IELTS records the number of questions for each type as "Variable", so a candidate who has memorised "Part 2 always has a table" has memorised something never promised. Build revision around the four forms as skills and treat every practice set as a sample of the types, not a template.

The one constant is the rule set, printed on the question paper every time: read the instruction, respect the word limit, count hyphenated words as one, do not reword. Prepare that once and it applies to every completion set you will meet.

Note that flow-chart completion is not a frequent item, and a candidate who has never practised reading a chart meets it cold. This is the form most worth a dedicated hour.

### Pro Tips — Completion Question Types

1. **Say the limit out loud before the audio.** One sentence, every set. The limit changes between sets and the printed instruction is the only authority.
2. **Count words as you write.** Build the habit in training so the exam does it automatically.
3. **Use the recording's words.** IELTS does not ask you to reword, and your synonym is not the answer.
4. **Read the printed words around the gap.** They give the grammatical form and often the category.
5. **Find the axis before you listen.** For a table, columns or rows; for notes, which level you are on.
6. **Draw the arrows first.** The direction is on the page; the recording backtracks around it.
7. **Skip without stopping.** An abandoned gap does not cost you the next one.
8. **Leave a lost gap blank rather than long.** No negative marking, so a blank and a wrong guess score the same — but the time is better spent on the next item.
9. **Type on computer delivery.** No transfer time exists, so a tidy-up rewrite costs you the following gap.
10. **Practise the closed version too.** Where you select from a list, read the options first and watch for near-identical ones.
