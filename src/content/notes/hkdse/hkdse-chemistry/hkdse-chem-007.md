# VII. Redox Reactions, Chemical Cells and Electrolysis — HKDSE Chemistry Notes

This topic covers Topic VII of the Compulsory Part of HKDSE Chemistry. It addresses oxidation and reduction in terms of electron transfer and oxidation number; balancing redox equations; common oxidising and reducing agents; electrolysis of molten compounds and aqueous solutions; and industrial applications of electrolysis.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Oxidation**: loss of electrons; increase in oxidation number.
- **Reduction**: gain of electrons; decrease in oxidation number.
- **Redox reaction**: a reaction in which both oxidation and reduction occur.
- **Oxidising agent**: species that gains electrons (gets reduced); causes oxidation in another species.
- **Reducing agent**: species that loses electrons (gets oxidised); causes reduction in another species.
- **Oxidation number rules**: element in its standard state = 0; group 1 elements = +1; group 2 = +2; group 17 = -1 (usually); O = -2 (usually); H = +1 with non-metals, -1 with metals; sum in a neutral compound = 0; sum in a polyatomic ion = the ion's charge.
- **Balancing redox equations**: half-equation method (separate oxidation and reduction, balance atoms and charges, multiply to balance electrons, add together) or oxidation-number method.
- **Common oxidising agents**: KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇, Cl₂, O₂, concentrated HNO₃.
- **Common reducing agents**: metals (Zn, Fe, Mg), H₂, CO, SO₂, halide ions (I⁻, Br⁻).
- **Electrolysis**: using an electric current to drive a non-spontaneous chemical reaction.
- **Electrolyte**: a substance that conducts electricity when molten or dissolved (free ions).
- **Electrodes**: anode (positive in electrolytic cell, oxidation); cathode (negative in electrolytic cell, reduction).
- **Electrolysis of molten compounds**: e.g. molten NaCl → Na (cathode) + Cl₂ (anode).
- **Electrolysis of aqueous solutions**: the products depend on the relative ease of discharge of ions. At the cathode: more reactive metals (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al) are not discharged from aqueous solution — H₂ is produced instead. Less reactive metals (Zn, Fe, Cu, Ag, Au) are discharged preferentially. At the anode: halide ions are discharged preferentially (in order I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > OH⁻ > F⁻ > SO₄²⁻ > NO₃⁻).
- **Industrial applications**: extraction of aluminium (Hall-Héroult), extraction of sodium (Down's process), electroplating, electrorefining of copper.

#### Examiner traps

- Confusing the sign of the anode/cathode in electrolytic vs. galvanic cells.
- Forgetting to check if the electrolyte is aqueous or molten — the products differ.
- Failing to apply the ease-of-discharge rules for aqueous electrolysis.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Oxidation numbers

Rules for assigning oxidation numbers (ON):

- An element in its standard state has ON = 0.
- In a compound, the sum of ONs equals zero.
- In a polyatomic ion, the sum of ONs equals the charge on the ion.
- Group 1 elements have ON = +1 in compounds.
- Group 2 elements have ON = +2 in compounds.
- Aluminium has ON = +3 in compounds.
- Fluorine has ON = -1 in compounds.
- Oxygen has ON = -2 in compounds (except in peroxides where it is -1, and in OF₂ where it is +2).
- Hydrogen has ON = +1 with non-metals, -1 with metals (e.g. metal hydrides).

#### Balancing redox equations

**Half-equation method** (in acidic solution):

1. Write the unbalanced equation.
2. Identify the species oxidised and reduced. Write unbalanced half-equations for oxidation and reduction.
3. Balance atoms other than O and H in each half-equation.
4. Balance O by adding H₂O; balance H by adding H⁺.
5. Balance the charge by adding electrons.
6. Multiply half-equations so that electrons cancel.
7. Add the half-equations; cancel species appearing on both sides.

For alkaline solutions, add OH⁻ to both sides to neutralise H⁺ (or use OH⁻ and H₂O from the start).

#### Common redox reactions

- **Metal + acid**: Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂ (Zn is oxidised, H⁺ is reduced).
- **Metal + salt solution**: Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu (Zn is oxidised, Cu²⁺ is reduced).
- **Metal + oxygen**: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO.
- **Combustion of hydrocarbons**: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O (C is oxidised, O is reduced).

#### Electrolysis

**Molten NaCl**:

- Cathode: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na (liquid sodium).
- Anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻.

**Aqueous NaCl (brine)**:

- Cathode: 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻ (H⁺ from water is reduced preferentially; Na⁺ stays in solution).
- Anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ (Cl⁻ is discharged preferentially over OH⁻ in concentrated brine).
- Overall: 2NaCl + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂ + Cl₂ (the chlor-alkali process).

**Aqueous CuSO₄ with copper electrodes**:

- Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu.
- Anode: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ (the copper anode dissolves; this is electrorefining or electroplating with a sacrificial anode).

#### Industrial applications

**Hall-Héroult process (aluminium extraction)**: electrolysis of alumina (Al₂O₃) dissolved in molten cryolite. Cathode: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al. Anode: 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ (oxygen reacts with carbon anode to form CO₂).

**Down's process (sodium extraction)**: electrolysis of molten NaCl. Cathode: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na. Anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻.

**Electroplating**: a thin layer of metal (e.g. chromium, silver, gold) is deposited on an object by electrolysis. The object is the cathode; a bar of the coating metal is the anode; the electrolyte is a solution of a salt of the coating metal.

**Electrorefining of copper**: impure copper is the anode; pure copper is the cathode; the electrolyte is CuSO₄ solution. The impurities either dissolve (more reactive than Cu) or fall to the bottom as anode sludge (less reactive).

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Cross-topic links

- **VI. Microscopic World II**: standard electrode potentials determine the products of electrolysis.
- **III. Metals**: metal extraction is based on electrolysis of reactive metals and reduction with carbon for less reactive metals.
- **VIII. Chemical Reactions and Energy**: the electrical energy in electrolysis is related to the Gibbs free energy change.

#### Exam technique

- **For redox equations**: use the half-equation method in acidic solution; show all steps.
- **For electrolysis questions**: identify the electrolyte (molten/aqueous), apply the ease-of-discharge rules, and write the half-equations at each electrode.
- **For "explain why" questions**: cite the relative electrode potentials or the ease-of-discharge series.

#### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

- "Oxidation means adding oxygen." The modern definition is loss of electrons; many oxidations do not involve oxygen.
- "The anode is always positive." In an electrolytic cell the anode is positive; in a galvanic cell the anode is negative (electrons leave the cell here).
- "Sodium is discharged from aqueous solution at the cathode." Sodium is too reactive; H₂ is discharged preferentially from water.
