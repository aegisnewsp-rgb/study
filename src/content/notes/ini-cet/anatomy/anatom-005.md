# Upper Limb Anatomy — Bones, Joints, Muscles, Nerves and Clinical Correlations

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

The upper limb comprises **30 bones**: 2 pectoral girdle (clavicle, scapula), 1 humerus, 2 forearm (radius, ulna), 8 carpals, 5 metacarpals, and 14 phalanges. The **brachial plexus** (C5–T1) forms 5 terminal branches — musculocutaneous, axillary, median, radial, ulnar — through roots → trunks → divisions → cords. The **rotator cuff (SITS)** stabilises the glenohumeral joint, the most mobile and most dislocated synovial joint in the body.

| Structure | Number / Identity | Key clinical hook |
|---|---|---|
| Pectoral girdle + free limb bones | 30 (2+1+2+8+5+14) | Clavicle = first to ossify, last to fuse; commonest fractured bone |
| Rotator cuff muscles | SITS (Supraspinatus, Infraspinatus, Teres minor, Subscapularis) | Suprascapular n. supplies S+I; axillary supplies Teres minor; subscapular n. supplies Subscapularis |
| Cubital fossa contents (medial→lateral) | Median nerve, Brachial artery, Biceps tendon | Radial nerve lies deep, between brachioradialis and brachialis |
| Carpal tunnel | 9 tendons + 1 median nerve ("MN EM" pattern) | Compression → thenar wasting, Tinel's, Phalen's |

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"Reach for the Stars"** = Erb's palsy C5–C6 (Waiter's tip: adducted, internally rotated shoulder + extended elbow + pronated forearm). **"Claw hand"** = Klumpke's palsy C8–T1. **"Lady Between Two Majors"** = Lateral cord, Between (posterior cord), Medial cord — around the axillary artery.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Osteology of the Upper Limb

The **clavicle** is the only long bone lying horizontally; first to ossify (5th week IUL, intramembranous) but the last to fuse (medial epiphysis around 25 years). It is the commonest fractured bone, typically at the junction of its middle and outer thirds. The **scapula** has the glenoid cavity, supraspinous/infraspinous fossae, acromion, coracoid process, and the **suprascapular notch** (suprascapular nerve passes below the transverse scapular ligament, artery above). The **humerus** carries three surgically relevant landmarks: **surgical neck** (axillary nerve and posterior circumflex humeral vessels), **radial (spiral) groove** (radial nerve on the posterior shaft), and **supracondylar ridge** (median nerve and brachial artery in children — supracondylar fracture is the commonest elbow fracture in paediatrics).

#### Joint Architecture

| Joint | Type | Movements / Stability | Classic clinical link |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glenohumeral (shoulder) | Ball-and-socket, multiaxial | Flex/ext, abd/add, medial/lateral rotation; stability from SITS + labrum + coraco-acromial arch | Anteroinferior dislocation → axillary nerve injury, Hill-Sachs lesion |
| Elbow (composite) | Hinge + pivot (humeroulnar + radiocapitellar + superior radioulnar) | Flex/ext, pronation/supination; carrying angle ~13° (♂), ~16° (♀) | Medial epicondyle fracture → ulnar nerve palsy |
| Wrist (radiocarpal) | Ellipsoid (biaxial) | Flex/ext, abd/add; scaphoid + lunate + triquetrum articulate with distal radius + articular disc | Scaphoid fracture → AVN of proximal fragment |
| 1st carpometacarpal (thumb) | Saddle (biaxial, biconcave–biconvex) | Flex/ext, abd/add, opposition — unique to humans | Osteoarthritis at the trapezium saddle |

The **carpal bones** form a proximal row (scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum, **pisiform — a sesamoid in FCU tendon**) and a distal row (trapezium, trapezoid, capitate, hamate). Mnemonic: **"So Long To Pinky, Here Comes The Thumb"** (proximal lateral→medial, then distal medial→lateral).

#### Brachial Plexus and Terminal Branches

Roots (C5–T1) → **Upper / Middle / Lower trunks** → **Anterior / Posterior divisions** → **Lateral / Posterior / Medial cords** (named for relation to axillary artery) → **5 terminal branches**. Two key trunks worth memorising:

- **Upper trunk (C5–C6)** injury → **Erb's palsy**: adducted + internally rotated shoulder, extended elbow, pronated forearm — "waiter's tip."
- **Lower trunk (C8–T1)** injury → **Klumpke's palsy**: intrinsic-minus "claw hand" with hyperextension at MCP and flexion at IP joints; possible Horner's syndrome if T1 root is avulsed.

#### Applied Anatomy of Three Critical Windows

- **Cubital fossa**: bounded laterally by **brachioradialis**, medially by **pronator teres**; floor = brachialis + supinator; roof = bicipital aponeurosis + skin. Contents medial to lateral: **median nerve, brachial artery, biceps tendon**; the radial nerve lies deep between brachioradialis and brachialis.
- **Anatomical snuffbox**: radial border = tendons of **abductor pollicis longus + extensor pollicis brevis**; ulnar border = **extensor pollicis longus**; floor = **scaphoid + trapezium**; the **radial artery** passes through the floor, and the superficial branch of the radial nerve crosses it.
- **Carpal tunnel**: roof = flexor retinaculum; contents = **median nerve + 9 tendons** (FDS ×4, FDP ×4, FPL ×1). Compression presents with nocturnal paraesthesia in digits 1–3½, thenar wasting, positive Tinel's and Phalen's tests.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

**Question:** A 22-year-old male falls on an outstretched hand. Tenderness is elicited in the anatomical snuffbox, and a radiograph appears normal on day 1. Which bone is most likely fractured, and what complication should the clinician anticipate based on its blood supply?

#### Solution:
1. Tenderness localised to the **anatomical snuffbox** strongly suggests a **scaphoid fracture**, the most commonly fractured carpal bone, typically through its waist.
2. The scaphoid receives ~80% of its arterial supply through the **dorsal carpal branch of the radial artery**, entering via the **dorsal ridge near the waist and distally** — i.e., retrograde flow to the proximal pole.
3. A waist fracture therefore **disrupts the proximal fragment's blood supply**, predisposing it to **avascular necrosis (AVN)** — risk highest in proximal-pole fractures.
4. Management: thumb-spica cast and **repeat radiographs at 10–14 days** (or MRI/CT) because occult fractures may only become visible after resorption at the fracture line.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students frequently write that "the scaphoid's proximal pole has a rich blood supply" — the truth is the **opposite**: the proximal pole is supplied by **retrograde** vessels entering distally, so a waist fracture devascularises the proximal fragment. Also do not dismiss a negative day-1 X-ray; the "clinical scaphoid fracture" must be immobilised and re-imaged.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Nerve Injuries, Roots and Resulting Deformities

| Nerve | Typical injury | Resulting deficit | Sensory loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| Axillary (C5–C6, post. cord) | Surgical neck humerus fracture / anterior shoulder dislocation | Deltoid paralysis → loss of abduction beyond 15°; teres minor weak | "Regimental badge" area over deltoid |
| Musculocutaneous (C5–C7, lat. cord) | Rare; coracobrachialis compression | Weak biceps + brachialis → loss of forearm flexion in supination | Lateral forearm (becomes lateral cutaneous n. of forearm) |
| Radial (C5–T1, post. cord) | **Saturday night palsy** in spiral groove; humeral shaft fracture | Wrist drop; loss of extension at wrist, MCP, thumb | Dorsum of hand (1st web space — superficial branch spared in pure spiral-groove lesion) |
| Median (C5–T1, lat.+med. cords) | Supracondylar humerus fracture; carpal tunnel | Thenar wasting; loss of opposition/lateral finger flexion; **pointing index** (Benediction) on making fist | Lateral 3½ digits palmar |
| Ulnar (C8–T1, med. cord) | Medial epicondyle fracture; cubital tunnel | **Claw hand** (mainly 4th–5th digits), **Froment's sign** positive (flexor pollicis longus substitutes for lost adductor pollicis); interossei wasted | Medial 1½ digits |

#### Surgical Anatomy Edge Cases

1. **Erb's point** is the union of C5–C6 roots forming the upper trunk, located ~2 cm above the clavicle posterior to sternocleidomastoid — surgical landmark for supraclavicular brachial plexus block.
2. **Lymphatics of the upper limb**: superficial lymphatics follow the cephalic and basilic veins; deep lymphatics accompany the brachial artery; **axillary nodes** (20–30) drain the entire upper limb and most of the breast — levels I, II, III behind pectoralis minor are used in breast cancer staging.
3. **Axillary artery** is divided into three parts by pectoralis minor; it is **palpable** in the medial upper arm and is the continuation of the subclavian artery beyond the lateral border of the first rib.
4. **Bicipital aponeurosis** (lacertus fibrosus) is a key surgical landmark — it protects the median nerve and brachial artery during venepuncture in the cubital fossa but is itself incised in forearm fasciotomies.
5. **Palmar cutaneous branches** of both median and ulnar nerves pass **superficial** to the flexor retinaculum — they are spared in carpal tunnel release, preserving thenar and hypothenar skin sensation.
6. **Rotator interval** is the triangular gap between supraspinatus and subscapularis tendons, containing the long head of biceps and the coracohumeral ligament — relevant to adhesive capsulitis and biceps pulley lesions.

#### Two Advanced Practice Prompts

1. **Image-based**: A diagram shows the cubital fossa labelled only by letters. Identify the structure marked "L" if it lies lateral to the biceps tendon and deep to brachioradialis, and explain why venepuncture at this site carries risk of nerve injury. *(Answer: radial nerve at its bifurcation; deep branch may be pierced during "blind" antecubital venepuncture.)*
2. **Clinical scenario**: A newborn presents with the right upper limb held in adduction, internal rotation, with the forearm pronated and the elbow extended following a difficult shoulder delivery. Name the injury, list the affected root values, and describe the muscle groups weakened. *(Answer: Erb-Duchenne palsy from traction on C5–C6; weakness of deltoid, supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, biceps and brachialis — producing the "waiter's tip" posture.)*
