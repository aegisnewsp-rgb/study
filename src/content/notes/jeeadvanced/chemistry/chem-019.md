# Metallurgy

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Metallurgy is the science and technology of extracting a metal from its ore and shaping it for use. The four-stage pipeline is **concentration → extraction → reduction → refining**, with the Ellingham diagram (ΔG° of oxide formation vs T) deciding which reductant works at a given temperature.

- **Calcination** heats carbonate/hydrate ores without sulphur (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂); **roasting** heats sulphide ores and releases SO₂ (2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂).
- **Electrolytic refining** of Cu uses impure blister Cu as the **anode**, a thin pure Cu strip as the **cathode**, and acidified CuSO₄ as the electrolyte; mass deposited uses m = I·t·M/(n·F) with F = 96485 C·mol⁻¹.
- **Hall–Héroult** dissolves Al₂O₃ in molten cryolite (Na₃AlF₆) at ~950 °C, then electrolyses it with a consumable graphite anode: 2Al₂O₃ + 3C → 4Al + 3CO₂.

| Process | Use case | Key reagent / condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cyanide leaching | Native Au/Ag | 4Au + 8NaCN + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4Na[Au(CN)₂] + 4NaOH |
| Mond process | Ni refining | Ni + 4CO ⇌ Ni(CO)₄ at 330 K, decomposed at 450 K |
| van Arkel–de Boer | Ti, Zr, Hf | Ti + 2I₂ ⇌ TiI₄, decomposed on hot W filament |
| Zone refining | Si, Ge, B, Ga | Ultrapure semiconductors, no chemistry involved |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Extraction stages and their chemistry

**Concentration** removes gangue. Hydraulic washing suits oxide ores (density-based), magnetic separation grabs magnetite/ chromite, and froth flotation concentrates sulphide ores using pine-oil collectors and frothing agents. **Leaching** dissolves the valuable component: bauxite is digested in concentrated NaOH (Bayer process) to give soluble NaAlO₂, while gold is leached with aerated NaCN solution.

**Calcination vs roasting** is a frequently tested distinction. Calcination is a dry, controlled heating of carbonate/hydrated ores — ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂; roasting is an oxidising heating of sulphide ores where metal sulphide becomes metal oxide plus SO₂ and is often exothermic enough to be self-sustaining. Self-reduction (auto-reduction) of copper from CuFeS₂ uses leftover FeS after partial roasting: Cu₂S + 2Cu₂O → 6Cu + SO₂.

#### Ellingham diagram and choice of reductant

A plot of ΔG°(formation of oxide) vs T lets you read thermodynamic feasibility. The lower a metal's line sits on the diagram, the more stable its oxide and the stronger that metal acts as a reductant. The **C/CO line slopes upward** (1 mol gas → 2 mol gas, ΔS > 0) and crosses most metal-oxide lines at high T, so coke beats those metals only above the crossover. The **C/CO₂ line slopes downward** (2 mol → 1 mol), so it does not cross most metals — useful in exam traps.

#### Refining methods

| Method | Metals | Purity / principle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Electrolytic | Cu, Zn | 99.99%, anode dissolves, pure metal plates on cathode |
| Zone refining | Si, Ge, B | ppm-level; impurity partitioning between molten zone and solid |
| Vapour phase / van Arkel | Ti, Zr, Hf | Volatile iodide decomposed on hot filament |
| Mond | Ni | Volatile Ni(CO)₄ formed and decomposed |
| Liquation / distillation | Sn, Pb / Zn, Hg | Differential melting or boiling points |
| Poling / amalgamation | Cu / Au, Ag | Stirring with green poles; Hg dissolves the metal |

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Iron and steel in the blast furnace

The blast furnace reduces Fe₂O₃ stepwise: Fe₂O₃ → Fe₃O₄ → FeO → Fe between 900–1500 K, using CO generated from coke (C + O₂ → CO₂; CO₂ + C → 2CO). Limestone (CaCO₃) decomposes to CaO, which combines with silicate gangue to form **slag** (CaSiO₃) that floats on molten pig iron. The molten metal tapped at the bottom contains ~4% C plus Si, Mn, P, S; subsequent **basic oxygen / open-hearth / electric-arc** processes convert it to steel by controlling C and adding Cr/Ni/Mn.

#### Worked example — Faraday's law in Cu refining

A Cu-refining cell runs at I = 200 A for t = 9650 s. With n = 2 (Cu²⁺), M = 63.5 g·mol⁻¹:

> m = (I·t·M)/(n·F) = (200 × 9650 × 63.5)/(2 × 96485) ≈ 63.5 g of Cu deposited.

Watch for **valency traps**: Al³⁺ → n = 3 in Hall–Héroult, Ag⁺ → n = 1, Zn²⁺ → n = 2.

#### Common mistakes examiners exploit

1. Forgetting cryolite's only role is to **lower Al₂O₃'s melting point** (from 2050 °C to ~950 °C) and raise conductivity — it is neither ore nor reductant.
2. Writing "Zn reduces Au from ore" in cyanide extraction; the correct step is Zn displacing Au from the **complex** Na[Au(CN)₂].
3. Assuming the graphite anode in Hall–Héroult is inert — it is consumed (C + O²⁻ → CO/CO₂), so carbon enters the overall cell reaction.
4. Reversing anode/cathode in electrolytic refining; the impure metal always **dissolves** at the anode.

#### Practice prompts

1. On an Ellingham diagram, the Mg/MgO line lies below the C/CO line at low T. Predict which reductant is preferred at 800 K vs 1800 K and justify using the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation.
2. Justify why Hall–Héroult uses a consumable graphite anode rather than an inert platinum anode, citing the standard reduction potential of Al³⁺/Al and the cost factor.
