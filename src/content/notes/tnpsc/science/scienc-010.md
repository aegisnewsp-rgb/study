# Mechanics: Work, Energy, and Power

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Topic 10 of TNPSC Group 1 Science covers the **mechanical and energetic foundations of physics**: force, work, energy, power, gravitation, and the laws of motion that govern everyday phenomena. Every numerical answer in this unit traces back to Newton's second law, F = m × a, where F is force in newtons, m is mass in kilograms, and a is acceleration in m/s².

- **Force:** F = m × a (N = kg·m/s²)
- **Work:** W = F × d (joules)
- **Power:** P = W / t (watts)
- **Kinetic Energy:** KE = ½ × m × v²
- **Potential Energy:** PE = m × g × h (g = 9.8 m/s²)

High-yield pointers: remember **g ≈ 9.8 m/s²** at Earth's surface (not 10), distinguish **mass (kg)** from **weight (N)**, and watch for the **½ factor** in KE — three traps TNPSC examiners set repeatedly in General Science MCQs.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Newton's Laws in Context

Newton's first law (inertia) states that a body remains at rest or in uniform motion unless acted on by a net external force. The second law quantifies this: F = m × a, with F measured in newtons. The third law pairs every action with an equal and opposite reaction. In TNPSC objective questions, these are often tested through scenario-based MCQs — for instance, asking why a passenger lurches forward when a bus brakes (first law) or why a rocket ascends (third law).

#### Work, Energy, and Power

Work is done only when a force produces displacement along the line of action: W = F × d (joules). Energy is the capacity to do work, and the **work-energy theorem** states that the net work on a body equals its change in kinetic energy. Power measures how fast work is performed: P = W / t, expressed in watts (1 W = 1 J/s). A 100 W bulb converts 100 J of electrical energy per second into light and heat.

#### Gravitation and Thermodynamics

Newton's universal law of gravitation: F = G × (m₁ × m₂) / r², where G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg². The acceleration due to gravity g = 9.8 m/s² varies slightly with latitude and altitude. Thermodynamics introduces four laws: zeroth (thermal equilibrium defines temperature), first (energy conservation: ΔU = Q − W), second (entropy always increases in an isolated system), and third (absolute zero is unattainable).

| --- | --- | --- |
| Force | F = m × a | Newton (N) |
| Work | W = F × d | Joule (J) |
| Power | P = W / t | Watt (W) |
| Kinetic Energy | KE = ½ m v² | Joule (J) |
| Potential Energy | PE = m g h | Joule (J) |

#### Exam Patterns and Traps

- Unit confusion: writing work in newtons instead of joules loses marks instantly.
- Treating g as exactly 10 m/s² without noting the approximation.
- Confusing scalar quantities (work, energy, power) with vectors (force, velocity, acceleration).

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Vector–Scalar Distinctions

Work is a scalar because it is the dot product of force and displacement vectors: W = F · d · cos θ, where θ is the angle between F and d. When θ = 90°, no work is done even if the force and displacement are large — a student walking horizontally while carrying a backpack does zero work against gravity on the horizontal path. Similarly, power can be written as P = F · v · cos θ for moving objects such as vehicles, useful for transport-engineering problems.

#### Conservation Laws and Cross-Topic Links

Conservation of energy and conservation of mass (later refined by Einstein's E = mc²) underpin classical mechanics and feed directly into nuclear physics in higher syllabi. The first law of thermodynamics is essentially an energy-conservation statement applied to heat and internal energy. These links mean a single TNPSC question on energy units may pull from mechanical, thermal, or electrical contexts.

#### Common Mistakes in Calculations

1. Omitting the ½ in KE = ½ m v² — students write KE = m v² and double the true value.
2. Using weight (mg) where mass (m) is required, especially in PE calculations on the Moon where g ≈ 1.62 m/s².
3. Adding kinetic and potential energies without first converting to a common reference frame.

#### Worked Micro-Example

A 2 kg ball is dropped from 5 m. Using PE = m g h = 2 × 9.8 × 5 = **98 J** at the top. By conservation, just before impact all PE converts to KE: 98 = ½ × 2 × v², giving v² = 98, so v ≈ 9.9 m/s downward.

| Scenario | Correct Approach | Typical Error |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mass vs weight | Convert mg only when force/weight is needed | Using kg when newtons are required |
| KE formula | ½ m v² | Writing m v² |
| Power unit | W = J/s | Confusing W (watts) with W (work) |
| g value | 9.8 m/s² unless stated | Assuming exactly 10 m/s² |

#### Practice Prompts

1. A 1500 kg car accelerates at 2 m/s². Calculate net force and the work done over 50 m.
2. A 60 W bulb runs for 5 minutes. Find the total energy consumed in joules and kilowatt-hours.
