# Carboxylic Acids

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

> The carboxyl group is the single most heavily tested functional group in JEE Advanced organic chemistry — get its acidity and four named reactions right and most questions become free marks.

A **carboxylic acid** carries the **–COOH** group, where a carbonyl (C=O) and a hydroxyl (–OH) sit on the same sp² carbon. The O–H bond is what makes the compound acidic (pKa ≈ 4–5 for aliphatic monoacids), not the C=O. When –COOH loses H⁺, the resulting **carboxylate anion (RCOO⁻)** is resonance-stabilised — both oxygens carry half a negative charge, which is why these compounds are more acidic than alcohols (pKa ≈ 16–18).

| Property | Value to remember |
|---|---|
| Hybridisation of C in –COOH | sp² (planar, bond angle ≈ 120°) |
| C=O bond length | ≈ 1.21 Å (1.21 × 10⁻¹⁰ m) |
| C–O (single) bond length | ≈ 1.43 Å |
| pKa of CH₃COOH | 4.76 |
| pKa of HCOOH | 3.75 |
| pKa of ClCH₂COOH | 2.87 |

> 💡 **Memory Hook — "ACES"** for the four named reactions you must not mix up: **A**cetic acid via oxidation · **C**arboxylate electrolysis is **Kolbe's** (R–R) · **E**sterification (Fischer, H₂SO₄) · **S**ubstitution at α-C is **HVZ** (Br₂/red P). Kolbe → alkane, HVZ → α-bromo acid.

Acidity order across homologous acids: HCOOH > CH₃COOH > C₂H₅COOH, because alkyl groups donate electrons and destabilise RCOO⁻. Electron-withdrawing groups (Cl, NO₂) close to the –COOH pull electron density away, stabilise the anion, and drop the pKa.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

> Master the four mechanisms, the resonance picture of the anion, and the leaving-group ladder — JEE Advanced paragraphs on this topic almost always revolve around them.

#### The –COOH Group and Why It Is Acidic

The carboxyl carbon is sp²-hybridised and trigonal planar. Its carbonyl oxygen pulls electron density away from the O–H oxygen, weakening the O–H bond and lowering pKa. The acid dissociates as:

$$\text{RCOOH} \rightleftharpoons \text{RCOO}^- + \text{H}^+ \qquad K_a = \frac{[\text{RCOO}^-][\text{H}^+]}{[\text{RCOOH}]}$$

pKa = –log₁₀ Ka (dimensionless). The carboxylate anion shows two equivalent resonance structures, each oxygen carrying –½ charge, which is why K_a of acetic acid (≈ 1.8 × 10⁻⁵) dwarfs that of ethanol (≈ 10⁻¹⁶).

#### Acidity Trends and the Inductive Effect

Hammett's substituent constant σ (dimensionless) for a substituent X is defined as:

$$\sigma = \log\!\left(\frac{K_a(\text{X–CH}_2\text{COOH})}{K_a(\text{CH}_3\text{COOH})}\right)$$

Positive σ (electron-withdrawing, e.g. –NO₂, –Cl) increases Ka; negative σ (electron-donating, e.g. –CH₃, –C₂H₅) decreases Ka. Distance matters: σ drops roughly by a factor of 2–3 per insulating CH₂.

#### Conversion Map for JEE Advanced

| Reagent / Reaction | Transformation | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| KMnO₄ / K₂Cr₂O₇ | RCH₂OH → RCOOH (primary alcohols); RCHO → RCOOH | Oxidative; acidic or alkaline medium |
| RCN + H₂O / H⁺ (or OH⁻ then H₃O⁺) | RCN → RCOOH | Hydrolysis of nitriles |
| RMgX + CO₂, then H₃O⁺ | R–MgX → RCOOH | Grignard + dry ice; adds one carbon |
| Soda-lime (CaO/NaOH, Δ) | RCOONa → R–H + Na₂CO₃ | Decarboxylation |
| Br₂ / red P | RCH₂COOH → RCHBrCOOH | Only acids with α-H react (HVZ) |
| SOCl₂ | RCOOH → RCOCl + SO₂ + HCl | Acyl chloride formation |
| P₂O₅ or (CH₃CO)₂O / Δ | 2 RCOOH → (RCO)₂O + H₂O | Anhydride formation |
| LiAlH₄ (dry ether), then H₃O⁺ | RCOOH → RCH₂OH | Reduction; NaBH₄ does NOT reduce –COOH |

#### Reactivity Ladder of Acyl Derivatives

Acyl chloride > anhydride > ester ≈ carboxylic acid > amide (towards H₂O, ROH, RNH₂). Leaving-group ability of Cl⁻, RCOO⁻, RO⁻, R₂N⁻ follows the conjugate-base pKa trend — the weaker the conjugate base, the better the leaving group.

#### Worked Illustration — Identifying HCOOH Among Isomers

A compound with molecular formula C₂H₄O₂ gives a positive Tollens' test and effervesces with NaHCO₃. Identify it and write one reaction that distinguishes it from acetic acid.

Step 1: The NaHCO₃ effervescence (CO₂ release) confirms a free –COOH group — both HCOOH (methanoic acid, M = 46.03 g mol⁻¹) and CH₃COOH (ethanoic acid, M = 60.05 g mol⁻¹) qualify.

Step 2: Tollens' reagent (Ag(NH₃)₂⁺) is reduced to metallic silver only by an aldehydic –CHO group. HCOOH contains an aldehydic H (its structure is effectively H–C(=O)–OH), while CH₃COOH does not. Therefore the compound is **HCOOH**.

Step 3: A clean distinction: warm HCOOH with Tollens' reagent gives a silver mirror — CH₃COOH stays inert.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students lose the mark by writing "HCOOH is acidic" as the distinction. Acidity is common to both. The specific behaviour — silver mirror with Tollens' (and red precipitate with Fehling's) — comes from the aldehydic hydrogen on the same carbon as the –OH, which is unique to HCOOH among carboxylic acids.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

> The four named reactions, decarboxylation, and formic acid's hybrid behaviour together account for roughly 70% of paragraph-based questions on this topic in the last decade.

#### Edge Cases and Boundary Conditions

- **Acidity at the limit:** Trifluoroacetic acid (CF₃COOH) has pKa ≈ 0.23, making it stronger than many mineral acids in water. It crosses the threshold where the usual K_w framework starts masking its dissociation.
- **Formic acid is the odd one out:** HCOOH lacks an R group, so it shows aldehyde-like chemistry (Tollens', Fehling's, decolourises KMnO₄ hot) that no other RCOOH does. It is the only monocarboxylic acid that gives a positive silver mirror.
- **Pivalic acid, (CH₃)₃CCOOH**, has no α-hydrogen — HVZ bromination fails. Examiners use this as a one-step MCQ trap.
- **Oxalic acid (HOOC–COOH, pKa₁ ≈ 1.25)** is diprotic and unusually strong because the second –COOH stabilises the first anion inductively.

#### Advanced Traps List

1. Confusing Hunsdiecker (RCOOAg + Br₂ → R–Br + CO₂ + AgBr) with Kolbe (2 RCOO⁻ → R–R + 2 CO₂ + 2 e⁻ at anode). Both lose CO₂ but Hunsdiecker needs silver salt and gives alkyl bromide, Kolbe uses electrolysis and gives alkane.
2. Quoting decarboxylation as "RCOOH → R–H + CO₂" — the soda-lime reaction works cleanly on **sodium salts**, not on free acids.
3. Treating NaHCO₃ test as universal for –OH acids — only –COOH liberates CO₂; phenol is too weak (pKa ≈ 10) and stays silent.
4. Forgetting that LiAlH₄ reduces –COOH all the way to –CH₂OH, but NaBH₄ does not. This pair appears often as a "choose the stronger reducing agent" sub-question.
5. Mis-counting α-hydrogens in HVZ: each α-H replaced needs one Br₂; e.g. (CH₃)₂CHCOOH has 1 α-H, so monobromination gives (CH₃)₂CBrCOOH; integer-type questions test this exact arithmetic.
6. Assuming esterification goes to completion — it is reversible (ΔH ≈ –9 kJ mol⁻¹); Le Chatelier's principle is invoked by removing water or using excess alcohol.
7. Believing Grignard + CO₂ adds "two carbons" — it adds one, because CO₂ contributes a single carbon to the resulting –COOH.

#### Adjacent Topic Links

- **Phenols** — same NaHCO₃-trap logic, opposite outcome (phenols do NOT effervesce).
- **Aldehydes and Ketones** — HCOOH behaves partly like an aldehyde because of its H–C(=O)– fragment.
- **Amines** — Hofmann bromamide degradation (RCONH₂ + Br₂ + 4 NaOH → RNH₂ + Na₂CO₃ + 2 NaBr + 2 H₂O) starts from an amide derived from the corresponding carboxylic acid.
- **Alcohols** — Fischer esterification ties the two families; the reverse reaction (saponification) closes the loop.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. Arrange ClCH₂COOH, CH₃COOH, Cl₂CHCOOH and Cl₃CCOOH in increasing pKa order, citing the inductive contribution of each substituent without tabulating values.
2. A monocarboxylic acid A (C₄H₈O₂) reacts with Br₂/red P to give a single monobromo acid B, which on hydrolysis with aq. NaOH followed by acidification regenerates A. Identify A, B, and the mechanism that allows B → A.
