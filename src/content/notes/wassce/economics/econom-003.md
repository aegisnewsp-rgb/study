# Elasticity

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Elasticity** measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in price, income, or the price of a related good. The most tested measure is **Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)**, the ratio of the percentage change in quantity demanded to the percentage change in price.

- **PED formula:** PED = (% ΔQ) ÷ (% ΔP). Result is negative because of the inverse price–quantity relationship, but the **absolute value** is used for interpretation.
- **Classification:** |PED| > 1 = elastic, |PED| = 1 = unit elastic, |PED| < 1 = inelastic, 0 = perfectly inelastic, ∞ = perfectly elastic.
- **Total Revenue (TR) test:** TR = P × Q. When demand is elastic, a price fall raises TR; when inelastic, a price fall lowers TR.
- WASSCE (Ghana) Economics usually tests PED, YED, and XED through 2–4 mark calculations and a Paper 2 essay on demand or government pricing.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### The Three Measures of Demand Elasticity

Three elasticity measures appear repeatedly in WASSCE Paper 1 items. Each is a unitless ratio of percentage changes.

- **Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)** — responsiveness of quantity demanded of Good X to the price of Good X.
- **Income Elasticity of Demand (YED)** — responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in consumer income (Y, measured in GHS).
- **Cross Elasticity of Demand (XED)** — responsiveness of quantity demanded of Good A to the price of Good B.

#### Formula Table

| Measure | Formula | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PED | (% ΔQ) ÷ (% ΔP) | Negative; use absolute value. |
| YED | (% ΔQ) ÷ (% ΔY) | Positive = normal good; negative = inferior good. |
| XED | (% ΔQ of A) ÷ (% ΔP of B) | Positive = substitutes; negative = complements. |
| Midpoint PED | [ΔQ ÷ average Q] ÷ [ΔP ÷ average P] | Used when the "base" is unclear. |

#### Determinants of PED

- Availability and closeness of **substitutes** (e.g., MTN and AirtelTigo data bundles).
- **Proportion of income** spent on the good (salt is cheap; housing is expensive).
- Whether the good is a **necessity** (inelastic) or **luxury** (elastic).
- **Time horizon** — long run is usually more elastic than short run.
- **Number of uses** — a good with many uses (electricity) tends to be inelastic.

#### Applying the Total Revenue Test

When only price and revenue figures are given, elasticity can be inferred from the direction of TR after a price change. A price cut that increases TR signals elastic demand; a price cut that decreases TR signals inelastic demand. This is a common 4-mark WASSCE structured question.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Exam Traps

WASSCE markers reward candidates who show the limits of elasticity, not just its calculation. Three edge cases routinely appear.

- **Point vs arc PED.** On a straight-line demand curve, elasticity is **not constant**. It is elastic above the midpoint, unit elastic at the midpoint, and inelastic below — even though the slope is unchanged. Confusing slope with elasticity is a frequent 2-mark loss.
- **Perfectly inelastic demand** (vertical demand curve) gives PED = 0. Examples used in Ghanaian textbooks include life-saving insulin. The trap is to write that quantity "never changes" — in reality it can change in the long run.
- **YED sign confusion.** A negative YED means an **inferior good** (e.g., second-hand clothing as incomes rise); this is independent of whether YED is greater or less than 1.

#### Worked Micro-Example

Suppose the price of a bag of rice falls from GHS 120 to GHS 100 and quantity demanded rises from 50 bags to 70 bags per week.

Using the midpoint formula:
- % ΔQ = (70 − 50) ÷ [(70 + 50)/2] = 20 ÷ 60 = 33.33%
- % ΔP = (100 − 120) ÷ [(100 + 120)/2] = −20 ÷ 110 = −18.18%
- PED = 33.33 ÷ (−18.18) ≈ −1.83 → |PED| = 1.83 (elastic)

Total revenue moves from GHS 6,000 to GHS 7,000, confirming the TR test for elastic demand.

#### Policy and Business Applications

- **Government taxation:** Tax incidence falls more heavily on the side of the market that is less elastic. Excise duties on fuel in Ghana are largely borne by consumers because fuel demand is inelastic in the short run.
- **Pricing decisions:** A firm facing elastic demand avoids raising price because revenue falls; an inelastic good (e.g., kerosene for low-income households) can absorb a price rise.
- **Subsidy removal debates:** Elasticity explains why subsidy cuts on petroleum products cause larger quantity drops for elastic uses (commercial transport) than for inelastic uses (household cooking).

#### Practice Prompts

1. A WASSCE item gives price falling from GHS 8 to GHS 6 and quantity rising from 20 to 35 units. Compute arc PED and state whether a further price cut would raise or lower total revenue.
2. Explain, using elasticity concepts, why the Ghana Revenue Authority imposes higher excise taxes on tobacco and alcohol than on bread.
