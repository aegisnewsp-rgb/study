# Question Types — Multiple Choice, Matching, Labelling, Short Answer — IELTS Listening Notes

These four types share one property: nothing is handed to you in the question paper except options. Completion items give you a sentence and ask you to find a word. These give you a set of possibilities and ask you to decide, or they give you an open question and ask you to retrieve a fact. Both are selection work, and both punish a candidate who treats the paper as something to read rather than something to predict from.

IELTS organises the Listening paper into six types. This note covers the four selection types and leaves the completion types to their own note. Each type has a published "What's involved?", a published "What skills are tested?", and a published count that is the same for all six: "Variable".

That word matters. IELTS does not promise that multiple choice appears in Part 4, or that matching appears in Part 3. Any revision plan built on that kind of guess is built on nothing. Build the skills instead.

The paper-level facts are fixed and printed on every test: "Time allowed: Approximately 30 minutes", "Number of parts: 4", "Number of questions: 40", and "Marking: Each correct answer receives 1 mark. Your final score is given as a band score in whole or half bands, e.g. 5.5 or 7.0." There is no negative marking anywhere in Listening, which makes skipping a genuinely impossible item rational rather than a gamble.

One more published detail shapes every one of these types. The recording is heard "once only", and "Different accents, including British, Australian, New Zealand and North American, are used." If you are waiting to hear a phrase a second time, you will not get it.

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)

#### Core facts in one pass

- **Multiple choice.** "A question with three possible answers or the first half of a sentence with three possible sentence endings. You have to choose one correct answer." Sometimes you get "a longer list of possible answers" and "have to choose more than one answer" — so "read the question carefully to check how many answers you need to choose."
- **What multiple choice tests.** "Many listening skills, e.g. a detailed understanding of specific points, or general understanding of the main points of the recording."
- **Matching.** "You have to match a list of items from the recording to a list of options, then choose the correct letter."
- **What matching tests.** Listening for detailed information, following a conversation between two people, and recognising "how facts in the recording are connected to each other".
- **Plan/map/diagram labelling.** "You have to complete labels on a visual which may be: a diagram (e.g. of a piece of equipment), a set of pictures, a plan (e.g. of a building), a map (e.g. of part of a town)." You "select your answers from a list."
- **What labelling tests.** Understanding "a description of a place, and how this description relates to the visual", and following directions such as "straight on/through", "the far door".
- **Short answer.** "Read a question and write a short answer using information from the recording."
- **What short answer tests.** "Listen for facts, such as places, prices or times, heard in the recording."

#### The five traps

1. **Assuming one answer per multiple choice question.** The published wording allows more than one answer from a longer list. The stem tells you how many.
2. **Treating options as the only words you need.** The stem may be half a sentence, and the options are the endings.
3. **Matching in reading order.** The recording decides the order, not the page.
4. **Labelling by position instead of by label.** The recording describes; you pick from the list.
5. **Paraphrasing a short answer.** Write the recording's words, inside the stated limit.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Multiple choice: the stem does the filtering

Two forms exist. A full question with three possible answers, or the first half of a sentence with three possible endings. In the second form the stem is doing half the work, because each ending must complete the stem grammatically and factually.

IELTS's own warning is the one most candidates skip: "Sometimes you are given a longer list of possible answers and you have to choose more than one answer." So the first thing to read is not the options but the instruction above the question, which tells you the number of boxes. Choose one per box.

For a question form, IELTS says this type tests "many listening skills". In practice the differentiator between candidates is whether they treat the options as a set to be eliminated or as candidates to be confirmed. Elimination needs only partial hearing; confirmation needs the specific detail. On a question asking about a specific point, aim for confirmation.

#### Matching: the direction of travel matters

You match "a list of items from the recording to a list of options". The audio is heard once, so you cannot go back to re-audit a pairing you skipped. Two failures follow from that.

**Losing your place.** Mark each option as soon as it is used. An unused-option list is your position in the recording; when it shrinks irregularly, you know you have missed a segment.

**Assuming alphabetical order.** Nothing in the published description suggests it, and real recordings describe items in whatever order the speaker chooses. Always confirm from the audio.

IELTS lists three sub-skills for matching, and the third is the one candidates rarely train: recognising "how facts in the recording are connected to each other". Where an item is defined by its relationship — "the room opposite the kitchen", "the option that comes after the cheapest" — you are not retrieving a label, you are tracking a relation.

#### Plan/map/diagram labelling: description, not direction-finding

The visual may be a diagram, a set of pictures, a building plan or a town map. In every case you "select your answers from a list", so the answer is already written somewhere on the paper.

The skill IELTS names is relating a description to a visual: understanding "how this description relates to the visual". So the winning move is to hold the visual in your head as a picture and place each label as you hear it described.

The published description also covers directional language — "straight on/through", "the far door". Treat those as anchors. When a speaker says "the far door", that phrase resolves an entire region of the visual at once, and it is the cheapest information in the whole test if you catch it.

Two failure modes: labelling by your own sense of the layout rather than by the description, and assuming the speaker visits the visual in a fixed sequence such as clockwise. The recording chooses the sequence.

#### Short answer: retrieve, do not reconstruct

You read a question and write a short answer from the recording. The word limit is stated, and the same rules that govern completion items govern here: "You will lose the mark for writing more than the word limit", "Contracted words such as 'they're' will not be tested", and hyphenated words such as "check-in" count as single words.

Two further instructions are specific to this type: "Type in your answers", and "Sometimes you are given a question which asks you to write two or three different answers." That second sentence matters more than it looks. If the question asks for several answers, the recording will deliver them at different points, and if you bank the first answer in your head you will lose the rest.

The skill tested is narrow and worth practising narrowly: facts such as "places, prices or times". Practise retrieving a single named item against a clock rather than summarising a stretch of talk.

### 🔴 Deep — Long-Term Mastery (1mo–6mo)

#### Why the published "Variable" is the most useful thing on this page

Every type's count is listed as "Variable". Most candidates read that as no information. It is a precise instruction: the mix is set per test, so the only sound strategy is to be equally ready for all four types in any part.

Rotate your practice. A week of only matching sets will feel comfortable and leave your labelling untested. Rotate all four inside every practice session, and finish with a full test where you had no idea in advance which types would appear.

#### Diagnosing which selection habit is failing

Score a practice set by the shape of the error, not just the total.

- **Multiple choice correct only when the option was mentioned last** — you are answering on recency, not on meaning. Trace each distractor to the moment it was mentioned.
- **Sentence-ending items wrong, question-form items right** — you are reading options without fitting them to the stem. Read the stem aloud, then test each ending against it.
- **Matching wrong in the middle only** — you lose your position after a missed pair. Mark used options visibly.
- **Matching wrong where the item is relational** — you are looking for a label instead of tracking the relation.
- **Labelling wrong only on maps** — you are navigating the visual instead of hearing the description. Practise with the visual covered between descriptions.
- **Short answer right in content, wrong on format** — limit, plural, or form. Transcribe exactly and count.
- **Whole sections lost after one hard item** — order discipline. Questions follow the recording order.

#### A six-week drill sequence

**Week 1 — stems and endings.** Sentence-ending items only. Read the stem aloud, then test each ending against it for grammar and meaning.
**Week 2 — elimination.** For every wrong option, write the sentence that caused you to reject it.
**Week 3 — matching under time pressure.** Shorter sets, faster. Mark used options. Score on whether you lost position or chose wrongly.
**Week 4 — relational matching.** Say the relation aloud before you choose.
**Week 5 — labelling.** Practise on plans and diagrams; after each label, state the phrase that placed it.
**Week 6 — full mock.** One official test on computer, typed, unpaused.

#### Edge cases worth rehearsing

1. **A multiple choice item with more answers to choose than boxes.** The instruction says how many.
2. **An option that is factually true but does not answer the question.** True is not sufficient.
3. **A matching option used that is actually the answer to a later item.** The recording decides, not your shortage of options.
4. **A directional anchor that resolves several labels at once.** Catch the anchor first.
5. **A short-answer question asking for two or three answers.** Bank them in order.

### High-Yield — Multiple Choice, Matching, Labelling and Short Answer

**Multiple choice.** A discussion where speakers disagree; a lecture where a claim is qualified or corrected; a proposal the second speaker rejects. In all three the wrong options are stated, which is why elimination beats recall.

**Matching.** People each described by role; facilities with attributes; items defined by position in a sequence. Matching rewards candidates who hold a list in mind without looking at it constantly.

**Labelling.** A tour of a building; a site plan with lettered markers; an annotated diagram; a town map. The common thread is a visual being described out loud.

**Short answer.** A speaker naming a place, a price or a time — usually embedded in longer talk, which is exactly why it is hard: the fact is one clause inside a sentence you are still processing.

**The one skill that serves all four.** Hold the recording's structure rather than its words. Each type is answered by locating a point in a structure you predicted — options eliminated, list positions consumed, visual regions filled, facts banked in order.

### Previous Year Patterns — IELTS Listening Selection Types

The paper-level constants do not move: four parts, 40 questions, approximately 30 minutes, 1 mark per correct answer, no negative marking, results in whole or half bands. The types are fixed too — IELTS lists six Listening question types, and this note covers four.

The distribution is not fixed. Every type's count is published as "Variable", and the recordings vary in topic and accent, with IELTS naming British, Australian, New Zealand and North American among those used. A student who has seen thirty sets and can still be surprised by a type appearing somewhere new has learned the test correctly. One who has memorised a pattern of appearances has learned nothing transferable.

The transferable part is the printed rule set. The word limit, the hyphenation rule and the instruction to read how many answers to choose appear every time and apply whether the item is a completion or a selection. Learn those once.

### Pro Tips — Selection Question Types

1. **Count the boxes before you read the options.** Multiple choice can require more than one answer.
2. **Read sentence-ending stems aloud.** You are testing endings, not facts.
3. **Reject distractors by locating them.** Write down where each wrong option came from.
4. **Mark used matching options.** It is your position in a recording you cannot replay.
5. **Say the relation aloud** before choosing for a relational matching item.
6. **Place labels as descriptions arrive,** not afterwards from memory.
7. **Catch directional anchors early.** They resolve a whole region at once.
8. **Bank multi-part answers in order.** They are given at different points in the recording.
9. **Skip what you will not get.** No negative marking means a blank costs only the mark you were already losing.
10. **Type your answers.** On computer delivery there is no transfer time to lose.
