# f-Block

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your NEET attempt.
The f-block contains the **inner transition elements**: lanthanoids (4f, Z = 58–71, Ce–Lu) and actinoids (5f, Z = 90–103, Th–Lr), where the differentiating electron enters an (n − 2)f orbital. The defining feature of the series is the **lanthanoid contraction** — ionic radii of Ln³⁺ shrink by ≈0.105 Å from La³⁺ to Lu³⁺ because 4f electrons shield the nucleus poorly (σ₄f ≈ 1.0), so Z*_eff rises steadily.

- **Common oxidation state:** +3 throughout both series; Ce also +4; Eu and Yb also +2.
- **Consequence of contraction:** Zr/Hf, Nb/Ta, Mo/W pairs have nearly identical radii.
- **NEET hot picks:** lanthanoid contraction causes, Ce⁴⁺/Eu²⁺ anomalies, Pm (Z = 61) being the only radioactive lanthanoid.

| Series | Orbitals filled | Stable OS exceptions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lanthanoids (4f) | 4f¹ → 4f¹⁴ | Ce(+4), Eu(+2), Yb(+2) |
| Actinoids (5f) | 5f¹ → 5f¹⁴ | U(+6), Pu(+6), Am(+5) |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Why the Series Contract
Each proton added across the lanthanoids is poorly screened by the added 4f electron (shielding constant σ₄f ≈ 1.0), unlike a d-electron (σ_d ≈ 0.85). The result is a steady rise in effective nuclear charge and a near-uniform ionic-radius decrease of about 0.75 pm per element. Actinoids contract by roughly 1.5× this amount because 5f electrons shield even less effectively in the early part of the series.

#### Consequences You Must Memorise
The contraction carries physical and chemical consequences that NEET exploits year after year. Heavier lanthanoid hydroxides (Ln(OH)₃) become less basic from La(OH)₃ to Lu(OH)₃ because the smaller, higher-charge cation polarises O–H bonds more strongly. Yttrium (Y³⁺, 0.90 Å) sits with the heavy lanthanoids (Er³⁺ ≈ 0.89 Å) in yttrium-group minerals, which is why it is grouped with Gd–Lu despite being a d-block element.

| Element pair | Why similar radii | Same group (d-block) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zr / Hf | Lanthanoid contraction between them | Group 4 |
| Nb / Ta | Same effect | Group 5 |
| Mo / W | Same effect | Group 6 |

#### Oxidation-State Exceptions
NEET tests which element violates the +3 rule. **Ce⁴⁺** is stabilised by a noble-gas core (4f⁰) and exists in ceric ammonium nitrate (NH₄)₂[Ce(NO₃)₆]. **Eu²⁺** and **Yb²⁺** prefer the half-filled (4f⁷) and fully-filled (4f¹⁴) cores, mimicking Ba²⁺ and Sr²⁺ in their sulphates and carbonates.

#### Colour and Magnetism
Ln³⁺ colours arise from **f–f transitions** (4fⁿ → 4fⁿ), which are Laporte-forbidden and therefore faint. The magnetic moment follows μ_eff = √[4J(J+1)] BM using Russell–Saunders J = L + S; Gd³⁺ (⁸S₇/₂) records the highest value at ≈ 7.94 BM.

#### Why Actinoids Behave Differently
5f, 6d and 7s orbitals in the actinoids lie close in energy, so oxidation states span **+3, +4, +5 and +6**. Uranium forms the volatile UF₆ (used in isotope enrichment) with U in +6, and plutonium forms the plutonyl cation PuO₂²⁺ also in +6. All actinoids are radioactive; only Th, Pa and U occur naturally in appreciable amounts (U-238 being the most abundant).

#### Common Examiner Traps
1. Marking Ce as a d-block element because of its 4f¹5d¹6s² configuration — Ce is a lanthanoid; the differentiating electron entered 4f.
2. Treating 5f shielding as comparable to 4f — it is weaker early in the series, giving wider oxidation-state variability.
3. Attributing Ln³⁺ colour to d–d transitions — the transitions are f–f, intra-configurational and Laporte-forbidden.
4. Listing Pm (Z = 61) as a naturally occurring lanthanoid — it has no stable isotope.

#### Practice Prompts
1. "Why are Zr and Hf difficult to separate?" — Answer using lanthanoid contraction producing nearly identical ionic radii.
2. "Why is Ce(IV) stable in aqueous solution but Pr(IV) is not?" — Ce⁴⁺ reaches the 4f⁰ noble-gas core; no other lanthanoid +4 ion enjoys that closed-shell stability, while Pr⁴⁺ (4f¹) is a strong oxidiser that oxidises water.
