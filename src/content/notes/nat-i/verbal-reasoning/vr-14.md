# Inference and Conclusion

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Inference** is a deduction that the reader must draw from given statements because the link is implied, not written. A **conclusion** is the specific end-point of that deduction — the statement the premises force you to accept. Two grades exist in NAT-I items: a **necessary conclusion** (true in 100% of cases permitted by the premises) and a **possible conclusion** (true in at least one permitted case, but not forced). The golden rule: an option that needs any fact, definition, or assumption not present in the passage is automatically wrong, even if it sounds sensible. Always check quantifiers — *some, all, most, none* — because shifting one word turns a necessary conclusion into a possible one. In the NAT-I Verbal Reasoning section, expect 3–5 such MCQs, usually with a short 2–4 line passage and four options asking "which follows", "which does NOT follow", or "what must be assumed".

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Core Definitions
A **premise** is a stated piece of evidence. An **inference** is the mental step that connects premises to a hidden fact. A **conclusion** is the final deduced statement. An **assumption** is the unspoken premise that bridges the two — without it, the jump is invalid. The examiner's task is to test whether you can spot a conclusion that the premises **necessarily** support, not one that merely **plausibly** fits.

#### Necessary vs Possible Conclusion
- **Necessary conclusion**: must hold true in every world consistent with the premises. If even one counter-example is allowed by the statements, the option fails.
- **Possible conclusion**: holds in at least one such world. It cannot be contradicted, but it is not guaranteed.

#### The Three Tests for a Valid Conclusion
1. **Support test** — does the passage actually provide evidence, or are you smuggling in outside knowledge?
2. **Necessity test** — is the option forced, or only likely? "Likely" fails when "must" is asked.
3. **Scope test** — does the quantifier (*all / some / most / none*) match the premises exactly? "All" cannot be downgraded to "most" without losing necessity.

#### Standard NAT-I Item Format
A short passage (2–4 sentences) is followed by four conclusions labelled I, II, III, IV. Common stems: *"Which conclusion(s) follow(s)?"*, *"Which does NOT follow?"*, *"Which is a valid assumption?"*, *"Which is a possible conclusion?"*. Options often mix one necessary answer with one possible-but-not-necessary distractor and one factually appealing but unsupported distractor.

#### Quick Elimination Heuristics
- Reject any option containing a number, name, or reason not in the passage.
- Reject options with absolute words (*always, never, only*) when premises use softer terms.
- If two options say the same thing, both are usually wrong.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Distinguishing Fact, Inference, and Judgment
- **Fact** — directly stated. Read it off the page.
- **Inference** — not stated, but the passage forces you toward it; the writer's intent points there.
- **Judgment** — an opinion or evaluation that requires a value system outside the passage. Judgments are almost never correct answers in NAT-I Inference items.

#### Why Plausible ≠ Necessary
The most common trap exploits **conversational implicature**. A passage stating *"Most employees left before 6 pm"* makes *"All employees left before 6 pm"* a tempting inference, but the quantifier *most* permits exceptions, so the universal claim is not forced. Likewise, *"Some A are B"* permits *"Some A are not B"* — both are possible, but only the first is necessarily true. Train yourself to treat words like *some, several, often, tends to* as **weakening operators** that block universal conclusions.

#### Assumptions vs Conclusions
An **assumption** is a hidden premise. To find it, ask: *"What must be true for the stated conclusion to follow from these premises?"* A conclusion flows forward (premise → claim); an assumption flows backward (premise ← unstated link). NAT-I sometimes swaps the labels to test this very distinction.

#### Adjacent Topics
- **Syllogism** — formalises premise–conclusion with Venn logic; useful for "all/some/no" chains.
- **Cause and Effect** — same structure, but the inference identifies a mechanism.
- **Statement–Assumption** — asks for the unstated premise instead of the deduction.

#### Common Mistakes
- Choosing the **strongest-sounding** option rather than the **most-supported** one.
- Treating *possible* as *necessary* because no contradiction is visible.
- Forgetting that a conclusion must be about the **same subject set** as the premises.

#### Practice Prompts
1. Passage: *"All managers attend the Friday review. Aisha attends the Friday review."* — Is *"Aisha is a manager"* a necessary, possible, or invalid conclusion? (Possible — the premise allows non-managers to attend too.)
2. Passage: *"The library was empty. The librarian switched off the lights and left."* — Pick the inference that must follow versus the one that only could follow, and justify using the support and necessity tests above.
