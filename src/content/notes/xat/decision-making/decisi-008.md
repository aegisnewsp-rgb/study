# Ethical Dilemma Analysis

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

An **ethical dilemma** is a "right-versus-right" choice where two valid moral principles or duties collide, so every available option leaves a residue of harm. XAT tests this through short business, HR, or governance passages and asks for the **most ethically defensible** course of action, not the legal one. The working definition: a situation in which following one core value (honesty, loyalty, fairness, life) forces you to break another.

- Identify the **issue** and map every **stakeholder** with their legitimate claim.
- Apply at least two lenses: **deontological** (duty/rules) and **consequentialist/utilitarian** (outcome for the largest group).
- Justify the trade-off explicitly; transparency of reasoning carries more marks than a "clean" answer.

| Lens | Core question |
| --- | --- |
| Deontological | Which duty or rule binds me here? |
| Utilitarian | Which option maximises overall good? |
| Rights-based | Whose fundamental right is at stake? |
| Virtue | What would a person of practical wisdom do? |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### What makes a dilemma "ethical"
A genuine ethical dilemma requires **mutually exclusive moral obligations** with no rule that automatically resolves them. It differs from an ethical *issue* (right-vs-wrong, in most keys with a clear norm) and from a legal *problem* (right-vs-illegal). The conflict is between competing duties such as honesty versus loyalty, fairness versus profitability, or transparency versus confidentiality.

#### Six-step analytical sequence used in XAT
1. **Issue identification** — name the value clash in one sentence.
2. **Stakeholder mapping** — list who is affected and their legitimate interest.
3. **Fact gathering** — separate verifiable facts from assumptions.
4. **Alternative generation** — in most keys 3–4 plausible options.
5. **Lens application** — test each alternative through utilitarian outcomes, deontological duties, rights, virtue, and justice/fairness.
6. **Decision and reflection** — pick the option with the lowest moral residue and articulate the trade-off.

#### Decision-support filters
Two compact tools help compare alternatives without losing rigour:

| Filter | Components |
| --- | --- |
| Four-Way Test | Truth, Fairness, Goodwill/Better Relations, Beneficial to all |
| PLUS filter | Policies, Legal, Universal principles, Self (the "does it sit right with me?" test) |

#### Typical XAT prompt pattern
A 120–180 word case (in many papers HR, supply chain, or public-policy) followed by options such as "report to the board," "resign in protest," "ignore and comply," or "whistleblow externally." The mark scheme rewards **stakeholder completeness**, **multi-lens testing**, and **explicit trade-off language** ("accept short-term revenue loss to preserve long-term customer trust").

- Watch for the trap of equating "legal" with "ethical."
- Look for the stakeholder you almost missed — supply chain workers, future customers, regulators.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Moral intensity and seriousness
Moral intensity (Jones, 1991) determines how much analytical weight a dilemma deserves. Six components — **magnitude of consequences, social consensus, probability of harm, temporal immediacy, proximity, and concentration of effect** — raise the stakes and push the decision toward whistle-blowing or public disclosure rather than quiet compliance. XAT setters embed these cues (numbers of people affected, regulatory visibility, time pressure) so you can rank seriousness across options.

#### Whistleblowing threshold
External whistle-blowing is justified only when (a) the harm to stakeholders is significant, (b) internal channels have been exhausted or are clearly compromised, (c) the whistle-blower has evidence, not suspicion, and (d) the disclosure is proportionate to the risk. Internal escalation always comes first on the XAT answer key unless the scenario states the channel is dead.

#### Common mistakes that drop marks
1. Picking the **legal** option without ethical justification.
2. Naming one stakeholder and ignoring indirect ones (society, future employees).
3. Applying a single lens, in most keys profit or rule-following.
4. Searching for a clean option and freezing — XAT expects an explicit trade-off.
5. Conflating cultural norms, personal values, and corporate policy with universal principles.

#### Worked micro-example
A supplier admits child labour in a subcontracted unit; cancelling the contract will bankrupt 200 local families. **Option A** (cancel immediately) protects rights but harms the families. **Option B** (continue sourcing) sustains livelihoods but tolerates the violation. **Option C** (give a 90-day remediation plan with independent audit, exit only if failed) balances rights, consequentialist welfare, and procedural fairness — in most keys the defensible XAT choice.

#### Practice prompts
1. A senior colleague takes credit for your analysis; raising it formally can derail a project. Apply the PLUS filter and rank four options.
2. Your company is quietly lobbying against a safety regulation. Stakeholder map and justify whether to escalate externally.
