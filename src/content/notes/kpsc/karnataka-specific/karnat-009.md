# KPSC KAS Negative Marking: How Marks Are Deducted

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

KPSC KAS Prelims carries negative marking. The Commission's own wording, as recorded in the official exam scheme, is that "Under the official Commission scheme, negative marking deducts typically 0.25 of the marks assigned to each question (0.50 mark deducted per incorrect answer)."

Read that twice, because 0.25 is a fraction and not a flat number. The deduction is one quarter of the marks assigned to the question. In the prelims as notified, each of the 200 questions carries 2 marks (400 marks across 200 questions, two papers of 100 questions and 200 marks each), so a quarter of 2 is 0.50. That is why the scheme quotes 0.50 mark deducted per incorrect answer.

| What you need | The notified figure |
|---|---|
| Prelims papers | 2 objective MCQ papers |
| Time | 4 hours in all, 2 hours each |
| Marks | 400 across 200 questions |
| Paper I | General Studies (national and international) + Humanities, 100 questions, 200 marks |
| Paper II | General Studies (state significance, science, technology, environment) + General Mental Ability, 100 questions, 200 marks |
| Marks per question | 2 (400 marks over 200 questions) |
| Wrong answer | typically 0.25 of the mark assigned = 0.50 on a 2-mark question |
| Blank answer | no deduction; the rule is written per incorrect answer |

Three things follow from that table. A blank question costs you nothing, because the scheme deducts per incorrect answer and an unattempted question is not an incorrect one. A wrong answer on these papers costs 0.50. And since every question is worth 2 marks, one wrong answer gives back a quarter of what one right answer wins.

> 💡 **Memory Hook:** "**Q**uarter of the mark" means 0.25 × 2 = **0.50**. Write the multiplication every time and you will never misplace the decimal.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### The exact wording, and why the word "typically" matters
The scheme sentence is short, and every clause in it earns its place. "Under the official Commission scheme" tells you the penalty is set by the Karnataka Public Service Commission as part of the exam design, not inherited from some other board's rule. "Typically 0.25" is the qualifier: the Commission states the figure as its standing scheme, and schemes are set cycle by cycle. The exam data for KPSC KAS cites the GP 2026-27 notification (kpsc.kar.nic.in, 31 July 2026) as the live advertisement for the current cycle, and section 10.1 of that notification is where the age limits are set out. The negative-marking line itself is part of the preliminary exam scheme, so read it in the live advertisement on kpsc.kar.nic.in for the cycle you are actually sitting. A rule that is written as "typically" is exactly the rule you should not carry in from memory.

#### Where the deduction lands: 1-mark and 2-mark questions
Because the scheme says 0.25 *of the marks assigned to each question*, the deduction changes with the value of the question.

| Marks assigned to the question | Deduction (0.25 of the assigned mark) |
|---|---|
| 1 mark | 0.25 mark |
| 2 marks | 0.50 mark |
| 4 marks | 1.00 mark |

The KPSC KAS prelims papers as notified carry 2 marks per question, since each paper is 100 questions for 200 marks. So in practice you lose 0.50 for every wrong answer. The 0.25 figure is the rate, not the amount. Students who quote "one quarter mark penalty for KPSC" in a mains answer lose the marks for not having applied the rate to the question's value.

#### The cost of a guess, done honestly
Treat the guess as a small bet and price it. A correct answer on a 2-mark question pays you 2 marks. A wrong one costs you 0.50. A blank pays you 0 and costs you 0. If you think your chance on a question is *p*, the expected value of answering it is:

> Expected gain = (p × 2) − ((1 − p) × 0.50)

Set that above zero and you get 2p − 0.5 + 0.5p > 0, so 2.5p > 0.5, so **p > 0.2**. In plain words: guessing beats leaving a question blank as soon as you believe you have better than a one-in-five chance on it. That is a low bar, which tells you something important about the prelims. The exam is not testing your nerve. It is testing how many questions you can clear in the time you have, because the prelims gives you 240 minutes for 200 questions, or 72 seconds a question on average. At that rate you can afford to mark and revisit, but only if you are not wasting the margin on coin-flips in the last ten minutes.

#### Reading a mock-test score honestly
A raw score hides your attempt profile, and this is where most candidates over-read a mock result. Take Paper I: 100 questions, 200 marks, 2 marks a question, 0.50 deducted per wrong answer.

- Profile A: 60 correct, 0 wrong → 60 × 2 = **120**
- Profile B: 61 correct, 4 wrong → 61 × 2 = 122, minus 4 × 0.50 = 2, net **120**

Same score, same paper, different candidate. Profile B attempted 65 questions and gave away 2 marks to the penalty; Profile A attempted 60 and gave away nothing. Which one is stronger? On the day, when the paper gets harder, Profile B has answered more questions, which is the bankable advantage, and it has also proved it can survive the penalty. Profile A has left five fewer questions on the table.

Now make the penalty visible. Answer all 100 questions in Paper I with 70 correct and 30 wrong: 140 − 15 = **125**. Leave the same 30 blank and keep the 70 correct: **140**. Attempting everything cost 15 marks. That is the number that should change how you treat the last ten minutes of the paper: every question you flip at random when the clock is running down is not free, and if you are wrong it is a quarter of a mark you had already banked.

So when you read a mock score, demand three numbers, not one: correct, wrong, and blank. Then check the arithmetic yourself. If a test report says 154 out of 200 in Paper II and you attempted 82 questions, the only possible profile is 78 correct and 4 wrong, because 78 × 2 = 156 minus 4 × 0.50 = 2 gives 154. If the report cannot be reconstructed like that, treat the number as suspect.

#### What the prelims score does to the final list
The exam description is clear that the preliminary is a filter: selection runs through a Preliminary Examination that is objective, a Main Examination that is written and descriptive, and a Personality Test at the end, and the preliminary paper is "a filter rather than a result in itself". The arithmetic in the exam pattern supports that. The Main carries two qualifying language papers (Kannada and English, 150 marks each, typically with a 35% minimum passing threshold) plus five compulsory scoring papers of 250 marks and 3 hours each, Essay, GS-I, GS-II, GS-III and GS-IV, for 1250 marks, and the Personality Test adds 50, bringing the final merit aggregate to 1300. That is 1250 + 50. The prelims' 400 marks are not part of the 1300.

Practically, then, treat the prelims as a pass-or-eliminate event with a ranking attached to it. You need to clear it, you need to clear it comfortably, and you do not get those 400 marks added to your name at the end. The marks you can still add are in the written papers, which is where preparation time is worth more.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### The full prelims structure and the arithmetic it forces

| Element | Notified figure | Derived |
|---|---|---|
| Papers | 2 objective MCQ papers | Paper I and Paper II, same length |
| Time | 4 hours in all, 2 hours each | 120 minutes per paper |
| Questions | 200 | 100 per paper |
| Marks | 400 | 200 per paper |
| Marks per question | — | 400 ÷ 200 = 2 |
| Time per question | — | 240 min ÷ 200 = 72 seconds |
| Deduction | typically 0.25 of the assigned mark | 0.25 × 2 = 0.50 per wrong answer |

Both papers are 120 minutes for 100 questions, so the pressure is identical in each, and the penalty is identical in each. A candidate who protects the last fifteen minutes of Paper II because Paper I felt fast has misunderstood the structure: the second paper gets the same 72 seconds a question as the first, with the same 0.50 hanging on every coin-flip.

#### The descriptive Main: there is no deduction to compute
The exam description says the negative-marking rule applies to the objective papers. The Main Examination is written and descriptive: two qualifying language papers in Kannada and English at 150 marks each, five compulsory scoring papers of 250 marks and 3 hours each (Essay, GS-I, GS-II, GS-III, GS-IV, 1250 marks in total), then the Personality Test at 50 marks, for a final merit aggregate of 1300. Written answers are marked on what you wrote, so there is no per-question deduction and no guessing calculus in that stage. Three consequences:

1. Do not carry prelims habits into the answer sheet. Skipping a mains question because it is "not worth the risk" is a mistake that costs nothing in a written paper and everything in the merit list. Write the answer.
2. The language papers are qualifying, not scoring. At the typical 35% threshold on a 150-mark paper, that is 52.5 marks, so a candidate who treats Kannada as an afterthought can be disqualified from the Main stage on a language paper alone. The threshold is written as typical, so confirm the number in the live advertisement.
3. The essay carries 250 marks in one 3-hour paper, a fifth of the 1250 marks you can add across the five scoring papers. That is where the aggregate actually moves.

#### Error budget: what a careless prelims sheet costs

| Wrong answers | Marks deducted | Share of the 400-mark prelims |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 5 | 1.25% |
| 25 | 12.5 | 3.1% |
| 50 | 25 | 6.25% |
| 100 | 50 | 12.5% |

The last row is the one to sit with. A hundred wrong answers means the whole paper was answered blind, and it costs 50 marks, which is 12.5% of everything the prelims measures. Blanks cost nothing, so a rough paper that marks 60 questions well and leaves 40 blank scores 120, while the same 60 correct with the other 40 rushed in scores 100. Strategy for the last stretch: only mark a question if you can name one option you are reasonably sure of, or if you have eliminated two of the five.

#### Traps that catch KPSC candidates

1. **Reading 0.25 as a flat deduction.** It is a rate applied to the marks assigned to the question. On these papers it lands on 0.50.
2. **Assuming the prelims marks enter the merit aggregate.** The aggregate is 1300, which is the 1250 written-paper total plus the 50-mark Personality Test. The prelims filters, it does not add.
3. **Applying the penalty to the Main.** The scheme covers the objective papers. The Main is written and descriptive.
4. **Ignoring "typically".** The Commission sets the scheme per cycle. The live advertisement on kpsc.kar.nic.in is the version that binds you.
5. **Quoting a prelims cut-off from memory.** Our exam data records the preliminary as a filter and does not record a cut-off figure for it. Read the qualifying condition in the live notification rather than quoting a number you have not seen.
6. **Borrowing another exam's penalty.** Several state commissions use a flat 1-mark penalty and several use none. The KPSC KAS figure is a quarter of the assigned mark, and only that.
7. **Forgetting the 2-mark weight.** A wrong answer gives back a quarter of what a right answer wins, which changes how you feel about guessing. On a 1-mark question with a flat 1-mark penalty the trade-off is different. Here it is 2 marks up against 0.50 down.

#### Practice prompts
1. A candidate attempts 82 questions in Paper II and scores 154. How many were wrong? Check: with c correct and w wrong, c + w = 82 and 2c − 0.5w = 154, which gives w = 4 and c = 78.
2. Your mock reports 150 out of 200 in Paper I. Write two different attempt profiles that both produce 150, then explain which one you would rather carry into exam day.
3. Your mock marks 70 correct and 30 wrong in Paper I. Compute the score, then compute the score if the 30 wrong answers had been left blank, and decide what your exam-hall rule will be for the last ten minutes.
4. Explain to a friend, in two sentences, why "a wrong answer costs a quarter of a mark" is wrong for KPSC KAS Prelims.

> 📌 **Formula Check:** Score = (correct × 2) − (wrong × 0.50). Deduction per wrong answer = 0.25 × marks assigned to the question = 0.50 on a 2-mark preliminary question. Blank = 0 marks, no deduction. Guess beats blank when p > 0.2. Time per question = 240 min ÷ 200 questions = 72 seconds. Marks per question = 400 ÷ 200 = 2. Final merit aggregate = 1250 (Main) + 50 (Personality Test) = 1300, with the prelims acting as the filter.
