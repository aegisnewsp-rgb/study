# Random Number Allocation

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your CAO application.

**Random Number Allocation** — Quick Facts for Irish Leaving Certificate students

**What is random number allocation?**
Random number allocation is the **tie-breaker mechanism** that CAO uses when two or more candidates are tied on points for the last available place in a course. Each candidate is assigned a unique random number when they submit their CAO application; the candidate with the HIGHEST random number receives the offer.

**Key facts:**
- Random number is generated when the application is submitted (not when the offer round runs)
- The number is unique to each application — no two candidates share the same random number
- The highest random number wins ties
- Random number is INDEPENDENT of grades and INDEPENDENT of order of course choices
- Used only at the cut-off boundary; candidates who are not tied are not affected
- Random number allocation runs at every round (Round 1, Round 2, Round 3, etc.)

**Why it matters:**
Random number allocation is the only mechanism that decides between tied candidates. Two candidates with identical 550 points will not always both get offers if only one place is available — the random number breaks the tie.

**Practical implications:**
- Apply early is NOT a strategic advantage (the random number is independent of timing relative to other applicants; the order of receipt is irrelevant)
- Order of course choices is NOT a strategic tie-breaker (the random number beats preference order — see cao-008)
- Tied candidates with higher random numbers receive offers; tied candidates with lower random numbers are placed on waiting lists

⚡ **Quick rule:** Two candidates with identical points at the cut-off are NOT guaranteed both offers — random number breaks it, and the **highest** random number wins. Order of choices does NOT affect random-number tie-breaking.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

**Random Number Allocation** — CAO Points Study Guide

**When does random number allocation apply?**
Random number allocation applies **only when two or more candidates are tied on points** for the last available place in a course. Specifically:

- Two or more candidates have **identical CAO points** (six-best + HL Maths bonus if applicable)
- The course's published cut-off is that exact points value
- There are fewer remaining places than the number of tied candidates

If a candidate's points exceed the cut-off by even 1 point, they receive the offer regardless of any tied candidates. Random number allocation only kicks in at the boundary.

**How is the random number generated?**
The random number is generated **at the time the candidate submits their CAO application**. The exact algorithm is published by CAO; the number is unique to each application. Some properties:

- The number is in the range 1 to N where N is identified by CAO's random-number system
- Higher numbers win ties (the candidate with the highest random number gets the offer)
- The number is independent of the order in which the application was submitted — two candidates applying minutes or days apart are drawn from the same distribution
- The number is independent of the order of course choices the candidate lists

**What random number is NOT:**
- It is NOT a function of grades (two candidates with identical grades get different random numbers)
- It is NOT a function of the order in which the application was received (early submission does NOT give a lower random number)
- It is NOT a function of the order of course choices (preference order is irrelevant to random-number tie-breaking)
- It is NOT a function of the candidate's school or county
- It is NOT a function of the year of application (a 2024 random number does not influence a 2025 random number)

**The order of evaluation in CAO offers:**
For each round (Round 1, Round 2, Round 3, etc.), CAO evaluates candidates course by course in the order of their listed preferences:

1. **Matriculation check:** Does the candidate meet all subject-and-grade minimums for the course? (see cao-005)
2. **Aggregate check:** Does the candidate's aggregate (six-best + 25 bonus) meet or exceed the course's published cut-off? (see cao-001, cao-002, cao-003)
3. **Random number allocation:** If candidates are tied at the cut-off, the random number breaks the tie — the highest random number wins.

Random number allocation is the **final tie-breaker** in the offer decision. It runs only at the boundary of two candidates with identical points.

**How random number ties are reported:**
After each offer round, CAO publishes the cut-off points for every Level 7/8 course. If random number allocation affected the cut-off, CAO notes that the cut-off was determined by random number rather than by aggregate alone. Candidates who received offers via random number can verify this by examining the published cut-off notes.

**Worked example 1:** Three candidates tied at 500 points for the last place in a course.

- Candidate A: 500 points, random number 1,234
- Candidate B: 500 points, random number 5,678
- Candidate C: 500 points, random number 9,012

Candidate C has the highest random number, so Candidate C receives the offer. Candidates A and B are placed on the waiting list (or considered in subsequent rounds). The intuition that a low number is lucky is exactly backwards — CAO draws down from the top of the range.

**Worked example 2:** One candidate exceeds the cut-off; random number is not invoked.

- Course cut-off: 500 points
- Candidate D: 501 points — exceeds by 1 point, receives the offer
- Candidate B: 500 points, random number 5,678 — does NOT receive the offer because Candidate D took the last place
- Candidate C: 500 points, random number 9,012 — does NOT receive the offer

In this scenario, random number is not invoked because Candidate D's points exceeded the cut-off. The tie-breaker only applies at the exact cut-off boundary.

**Worked example 3:** Two candidates tied at the cut-off after Round 1, both not offered; one receives offer in Round 2.

- Round 1 cut-off: 500 points, 1 place available
- Candidates B and C both have 500 points. Candidate B has random number 5,678; Candidate C has random number 9,012. Candidate C holds the higher number and receives the offer. Candidate B is placed on the waiting list.
- In Round 2, the cut-off for the course might be lower (e.g., 480 points) if no candidates at 490 points have applied, or if places remain unfilled. Candidate C with 500 points would receive a Round 2 offer at any cut-off ≤ 500.

**The random number across rounds:**
A candidate's random number is **constant across rounds**. The same random number is used in Round 1, Round 2, Round 3, etc. Candidates do not get a fresh random number for each round.

**What "random" means here:**
The random number is generated by a deterministic algorithm (the same inputs produce the same output), but the inputs include a per-application seed that is unpredictable in advance. From a candidate's perspective, the number is random — no candidate can predict or influence their random number before application submission.

**Why random number is independent of submission order:**
The random number is generated **at the moment of application submission** and is unique to that application. The order in which candidates submit their applications (e.g., 14:00:01 vs 14:00:02) does not affect the distribution of random numbers. Each candidate's number is independently drawn from the same uniform distribution, regardless of timing.

**Why random number is independent of order of choices:**
The random number is a property of the application, not of any individual course choice. When two candidates are tied on a particular course's cut-off, the random number decides regardless of whether the candidate listed the course as their first or tenth preference. The order of choices (see cao-008) is evaluated separately, after the random-number tie-breaker.

**Why random number allocation is fair:**
Random number allocation is the standard fair-tie-breaker mechanism used by CAO for the last-place allocation problem. It is:

- **Independent of grades:** Two candidates with identical grades get different random numbers
- **Independent of timing:** Submission order does not affect the distribution
- **Independent of preferences:** Order of choices does not affect tie-breaking
- **Not disclosed in advance:** the number is assigned to your application, but CAO does not publish it as a predictor — treat it as unknown rather than as a score you hold

**What to do if you are affected by random number allocation:**
- If you are tied at the cut-off and lose the random number, you may receive an offer in a later round if places remain unfilled
- You may be placed on a waiting list (course-specific)
- Your random number is the same across rounds, so a high random number does NOT give you an advantage in later rounds compared to other tied candidates
- Consider re-applying in subsequent years (you receive a fresh random number each year)

**Frequently asked questions:**
- "Does early submission give me a better random number?" — No. The random number is generated at submission time and is independent of the order in which applications are received. Two candidates submitting seconds apart are drawn from the same distribution, so timing buys nothing.
- "Does my random number change between rounds?" — No. The random number is constant across rounds for a given application. The same number is used in Round 1, Round 2, etc.
- "Can I see my random number?" — Treat it as not knowable in advance. CAO assigns a number to your application and uses it only when a tie occurs; it is not published as something you can look up and plan around. The number is never connected to your CAO application number.
- "Does preference order affect random number allocation?" — No. Random number is a property of the application, not of any individual course. The tie-breaker is invoked regardless of where the candidate listed the course.
- "Can two candidates have the same random number?" — No. Each application receives a unique random number. The system guarantees no duplicates.
- "What happens if I am tied with 100 other candidates?" — The highest random number among the 101 tied candidates receives the offer; the other 100 are considered for waiting lists or later rounds.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> For candidates who already know how the tie-break works and need to know what actually moves you past it.

**The mechanism in one line:** when applicants with identical points compete for the same number of remaining places, CAO allocates the places starting from the highest random number downward; everyone else is placed on a waiting list for that course.

**The random number is a number, not a rank:**
This is the single most common misunderstanding, and it runs the wrong way. A **higher** random number only helps against candidates you are actually tied with. Holding 9,012 when your cut-off neighbour holds 412 changes nothing if you are 30 points above them — they are not tied with you and never enter the comparison. Random numbers are best understood as an absence of information: they settle ties you did not create, and they cannot be read in advance as a good or bad draw.

**What CAO states the number is not:**
In its own guidance, the random number is independent of your CAO application number, does not depend on when you applied, and has no connection to the applicant number issued to you. That rules out the four theories that circulate every cycle:

- that applying early produces a lower number — it does not;
- that a low number is the lucky draw — CAO allocates ties from the highest number downward, so a high number is the one that wins;
- that a number near the top of the range is an advantage you can aim for — it is only ever relevant if you are tied, and it is drawn, not chosen;
- that the number is derived from anything about you, your school or your year — it is not.

**Why submission order is not a lever, stated carefully:**
Each application's number is drawn independently from the same distribution. Two candidates applying an hour apart, or a day apart, have the same expected number. This is a statement about the distribution, not a promise that no candidate will ever receive a low number by chance — obviously some do. The point is that you cannot improve your own draw by changing your timing, and any method claiming to do so is unfounded.

**Preference order does not break a points tie — but it decides whether the tie matters:**
These are separate mechanisms and candidates conflate them constantly. CAO processes offers in order of your stated preferences, so listing order determines which course you are considered for when offers are made. Once two candidates hold identical points for the same final place in a course you are both eligible for, the random number decides. Order of preference has already done its work by that point.

**What later rounds actually do:**
Round 2 offers are made from the new pool of places and new applicants. Candidates on a waiting list may receive an offer if places remain unfilled, but a later round is not a re-run of Round 1 with better odds — the field is different, and the cut-off may move in either direction. Your random number does not refresh between rounds, so there is no "second chance" to draw again. The number you hold for the current application is the number that applies.

**What actually moves you past a cut-off, in order of effect:**

1. Points. The only lever that changes your position at the boundary.
2. Eligibility. Being eligible for a course you were otherwise out of contention for is worth more than a small points gain, because the alternative is not a near miss, it is exclusion.
3. Listing the course lower. It does not improve your ranking, but it changes which course you hold if the tie resolves against you.
4. Your random number. Nothing, unless you are tied.

That ordering is deliberately lopsided because the tie-break is the only element of the offer process that no candidate can influence. Treating it as anything other than the last step in a long chain produces strategies that spend effort where effort has no effect.

**The waiting-list reality:**
A waiting-list place is not a ranked position in most cases — it is a record that the course may contact you if a place arises. Candidates often plan around a waiting list as though it were a queue they can measure their progress against. It is not. If the numbers of places and candidates on the list are not published in a form you can compare, treat it as an opportunity with no probability attached, not as a plan.

**Rehearse against the real cut-off:**
Before offer season, find last year's published cut-off for each course you want, add the random number range to your own points if you are within roughly 20 points of it, and accept that a narrow band will resolve on the draw. That band is worth targeting with extra study; everything below it needs a different strategy entirely.

### 💡 Pro Tips
- Apply by the standard CAO deadline (1 February) — late applications have reduced course availability but the random number is independent of timing relative to other candidates
- Random number allocation is invoked **only at the cut-off boundary** — candidates who exceed the cut-off by even 1 point are not affected by random numbers
- If you are tied at the cut-off and lose the random number, you may receive an offer in a later round if places remain unfilled; your random number is constant across rounds
- The random number is generated at submission time and is unique to your application — no two candidates share a random number
- Random number allocation does NOT depend on order of course choices — preference order is evaluated separately, after random-number tie-breaking
- Re-applying in subsequent years gives you a fresh random number — your previous random number does not carry over
- For non-standard applicants (mature, QQI FET, international), random number allocation applies to their pathway through the same mechanism
