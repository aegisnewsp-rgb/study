# Cloze Test

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

A Cloze Test in SSC CGL Tier 2 presents a single 200–250 word passage with 6–10 numbered blanks, each requiring the selection of exactly one word from four options that best fits the surrounding context. The deletion interval is fixed (in standard papers every 5th or 6th word), so grammar, collocation, and logical flow must all align with the chosen replacement. The English Language section carries 2% weight and a 0.5 mark penalty per wrong answer under the Tier 2 negative-marking rule.

- **Semantic fit** — word meaning must match the idea the author is expressing in that sentence.
- **Grammatical fit** — part of speech, tense, number, voice, article, and preposition all constrain the answer.
- **Collocational fit** — natural partnerships (e.g., *take a decision*, not *make a decision* in formal register).
- **Discourse fit** — connectors like *however*, *therefore*, *moreover* must preserve logical flow.

> 💡 **High-Yield Memory Hook:** **"SCGD"** — **S**emantics, **C**ollocation, **G**rammar, **D**iscourse. Run the four filters in order for every blank; the option that fails any one filter is wrong.

| Filter | Quick Check | Common Eliminator |
|---|---|---|
| Semantics | Does the word's meaning make sense? | Distractor that contradicts author intent |
| Grammar | Right part of speech and tense? | Verb form mismatch with subject |
| Collocation | Does the word pair naturally with its neighbour? | Wrong preposition partner (e.g., *angry on*) |
| Discourse | Does the connector signal the right logic? | *Although* used where *therefore* is needed |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### How the Cloze Procedure Works

Wilson Taylor's original cloze procedure deletes every Nth word (N = 5, 6, or 7) from a coherent passage. SSC CGL Tier 2 follows this format: one passage, ~200–250 words, ~6–10 blanks, four options per blank, exactly one correct. The candidate never supplies the missing word; the work is selection from given options. Because the deletion is mechanical, the surrounding words provide enough co-text for an educated guess even without seeing the full options.

#### The Four-Cue Decision Model

Every blank activates four simultaneous cues. Successful candidates consciously separate them before reading options.

| Cue | What It Tests | Example (blank = X) | Decision Logic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Semantic | Vocabulary in context | "The minister gave a … speech." | "long," "rambling," "boring" — all fit meaning; grammar decides |
| Grammatical | Morphosyntax | "She … to the market yesterday." | Tense demands past (*went*, not *go*) |
| Collocational | Lexical partnership | "He … an apology." | *offered* / *tendered*, not *gave* |
| Discourse | Cohesion across sentences | "… the rain stopped, play resumed." | Temporal link needs *After* / *Once*, not *However* |

#### Common Question Categories in SSC CGL Tier 2

- **Conjunctions & conjunctive adjuncts:** *although, whereas, therefore, consequently, nevertheless, moreover, furthermore*.
- **Prepositions & articles:** distinguishing *in / on / at*; choosing *a / an / the / zero*.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *put off (postpone)* vs *put up with (tolerate)*; *take over (assume control)* vs *take after (resemble)*.
- **Near-synonyms:** *genuine / authentic / sincere*; *affect / effect*; *economic / economical*.
- **Relative pronouns & determiners:** *which / that / who / whose / whatever*.
- **Modal auxiliaries:** *can / can / can / should / would* across probability and obligation registers.

#### Strategy Sequence

1. Read the full passage once for gist and tone; note the author's stance (formal, persuasive, narrative).
2. Skip blanks you cannot solve and circle back after reading options.
3. Apply SCGD filters to each option; eliminate on grammar before meaning.
4. Re-read the completed passage aloud — any rough spot flags a wrong choice.
5. Mark only when the full sentence reads smoothly with the inserted word.

#### 🎯 Exam-Level Worked Problem

#### Question:
*"The committee's report (1)…… a sharp criticism of the existing policy framework, (2)…… it failed to acknowledge the financial (3)…… of the proposed reforms. Critics, (4)……, argued that the recommendations were both ambitious and (5)…… implementable."* 
Options for blank (3): (a) implications (b) implicates (c) implications' (d) implication

#### Solution:
Step 1 — **Grammar filter:** The blank follows the determiner *the* and precedes *of the proposed reforms*, so the required form is a noun in plural: *implications*. Option (b) is a verb, (c) is a possessive plural (would require a possessive context like "the implications' effect"), (d) is singular. 
Step 2 — **Collocation filter:** *Financial implications of reforms* is a standard collocation; *financial implicates of reforms* is not. 
Step 3 — **Discourse filter:** The clause critiques the policy but admits a flaw — "failed to acknowledge" — which logically introduces a noun naming what the report missed. *Implications* fits precisely. 
Answer: **(a) implications**.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Test-makers exploit the noun/verb confusion between *implications* (noun) and *implicates* (verb) to catch candidates who skim grammar. Always check the part-of-speech slot before weighing meaning — a perfectly meaningful word in the wrong grammatical slot is still wrong.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Boundary Conditions

Cloze passages occasionally include blanks inside idiomatic phrases where the missing word is a fixed particle (e.g., *by and large*, *in spite of*, *with regard to*). Here the collocational cue overrides semantic choice — there is no semantic reasoning because the phrase is lexicalised. Distractors in such blanks include near-homophones (*regard* vs *regards*) or words that look plausible in isolation but break the idiom.

A second edge case is the *register-sensitive* blank: a passage written in formal academic tone can require *moreover* where informal speech would prefer *also*. Candidates trained on colloquial English frequently lose marks here because their default register is conversational. Always read two sentences ahead to detect register shift before committing to a connector.

A third edge case appears when the blank controls subject–verb agreement across a clause boundary: *"The list of items … on the table."* The subject is *list* (singular), so *was* — not *were* — regardless of the plural *items*. Distractors exploit this by offering the locally attractive plural.

#### Comparison Matrix of Commonly Confused Connectors

| Connector | Logical Function | Typical Context | Common Trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| *although* / *though* | Contrast + concession | Subordinate clause, unexpected outcome | Confused with *because* |
| *however* / *nevertheless* | Contrast, equal weight | Sentence-initial or after semicolon | Used where cause-effect is needed |
| *therefore* / *consequently* | Cause → effect | Follows a stated cause | Used to introduce contrast |
| *moreover* / *furthermore* | Addition, same direction | Reinforces previous point | Used where a new point starts |
| *whereas* / *while* | Comparison/contrast between two items | Two parallel clauses | Confused with temporal *while* |

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. **Register-shift blank:** Take any SSC CGL Tier 2 passage from 2018–2023, identify the single blank whose correct answer depends on register rather than grammar, and explain in one sentence why each distractor fails the register test.
2. **Idiom-anchored blank:** Construct your own five-sentence paragraph, delete one word from a fixed idiom in sentence 3, and verify that all four options you supply can be ruled out by collocation alone.
