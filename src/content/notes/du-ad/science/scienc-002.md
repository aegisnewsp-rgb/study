# Dalton's Atomic Theory (1808)

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

John Dalton published his atomic theory in 1808, proposing that **matter is composed of tiny, indivisible particles called atoms**. Atoms of one element are identical in mass and properties; atoms of different elements differ; atoms join in fixed whole-number ratios; a chemical reaction only rearranges atoms.

| Postulate | One-line meaning |
|---|---|
| All matter = atoms | Nothing smaller exists chemically |
| Identical atoms in an element | Same mass, same size |
| Atoms cannot be created/destroyed | Conservation of mass follows |
| Different elements → different atoms | Each element has unique mass |
| Whole-number combining ratios | Births Law of Multiple Proportions |

The whole-number combining rule leads directly to the **Law of Multiple Proportions**: when two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other stand in a simple integer ratio.

> 💡 **Memory Hook — "MICWR":** Matter is atoms, Identical in element, Cannot be split, Whole-number ratio, Rearranged in reactions. Read the five postulates off the five letters in order.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)

#### Core Postulates in Context

Dalton's 1808 *A New System of Chemical Philosophy* gave chemistry its first quantitative atomic picture. The five postulates, in modern phrasing:

1. All matter is built from atoms — the smallest unit taking part in a chemical change.
2. Atoms of the same element share identical mass, size, and chemical behaviour.
3. Atoms cannot be created, divided, or destroyed in a chemical reaction.
4. Atoms of different elements differ in mass and properties.
5. Atoms combine in simple, fixed, whole-number ratios to form compounds.

These postulates link straight to four **Laws of Chemical Combination**: Conservation of Mass, Definite Proportions, Multiple Proportions, and Reciprocal Proportions. The first three are essentially corollaries of postulates 3 and 5.

#### Concept Comparison Matrix

| Concept | What Dalton claimed | Modern correction |
|---|---|---|
| Indivisibility of atoms | Atoms cannot be split chemically | Atoms split into electrons, protons, neutrons |
| Identical mass in an element | All atoms of one element have equal mass | **Isotopes** (e.g. ¹H, ²H, ³H) have different masses |
| Different elements, different mass | Mass distinguishes every element | **Isobars** (e.g. ⁴⁰Ar, ⁴⁰K, ⁴⁰Ca) share mass |
| Whole-number combining ratios | Always integer | Holds for molecules, not for non-stoichiometric solids |
| Atoms of one element cannot combine | Implicit | **Allotropes** (O₂ vs O₃, P₃ vs P₄) break this rule |

#### Atomicity and Formula Work

**Atomicity** = number of atoms in one molecule of a free element.

| Atomicity | Examples |
|---|---|
| Monoatomic (1) | He, Ne, Ar, all noble gases |
| Diatomic (2) | H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂ |
| Triatomic (3) | O₃ (ozone) |
| Tetratomic (4) | P₄ (white phosphorus) |
| Polyatomic (≥5) | S₈ |

> 📌 **Formula Check — Percentage Composition:**
> %A = (n × M_A / M_compound) × 100%
> n = number of atoms of A in formula (dimensionless integer), M_A = relative atomic mass of A (u, dimensionless), M_compound = molar mass of compound (g·mol⁻¹ or u).

#### Worked Problem on Multiple Proportions

Two oxides of carbon are analysed. Oxide I contains 42.9 % carbon by mass; Oxide II contains 27.3 % carbon. Show that the data obey the Law of Multiple Proportions.

Take 1 g of carbon in each oxide.

- Oxide I: carbon = 1 g, so oxygen = (100 − 42.9)/42.9 = 1.333 g.
- Oxide II: carbon = 1 g, so oxygen = (100 − 27.3)/27.3 = 2.663 g.

Ratio of oxygen masses = 1.333 : 2.663 ≈ 1 : 2. A clean whole-number ratio — the Law of Multiple Proportions holds, and the two oxides correspond to CO and CO₂.

> ⚠️ **Examiner Trap:** Students often divide the higher percentage by the lower one instead of fixing the same mass of the *other* element. Always hold the mass of element A constant first, then compare the masses of B.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)

#### From Dalton to Sub-Atomic Particles

Dalton's atom was a hard, featureless sphere. J. J. Thomson's cathode-ray experiment (1897) revealed the **electron**, Rutherford's gold-foil scattering (1911) uncovered a dense nucleus, and Chadwick (1932) closed the picture with the **neutron**. Each discovery chipped away at postulate 2 or 3:

| Discovery | Dalton's postulate broken |
|---|---|
| Electron (Thomson) | Atom is divisible |
| Proton, neutron (Rutherford, Chadwick) | Atom has internal structure |
| Isotopes (Soddy, 1913) | Atoms of same element can differ in mass |
| Isobars | Atoms of different elements can share mass |
| Allotropes | Atoms of one element can form distinct molecules |

The 3 % weight in the DU Admission Science section rarely rewards deep history, but the limitations table above is the single most testable cluster — every one of those rows has appeared as a 1-mark MCQ.

#### Edge Cases Worth Memorising

1. **Law of Reciprocal Proportions** — not written by Dalton, but derived from his atom idea. If element A combines with B and also with C, then the ratio of B to C masses (combining with a fixed mass of A) equals the ratio in which B and C combine directly with each other.
2. **Non-stoichiometric solids** — compounds such as FeO (actually Fe₀.₉₅O) violate the fixed whole-number ratio at the bulk level, though the local lattice is still atomic.
3. **Mass spectrometer reading** — isotope distributions are direct experimental proof that postulate 2 fails.

> 📌 **Advanced Recall Anchor:** For a DU MCQ asking "Which is *not* a postulate of Dalton?", pick the option mentioning isotopes, divisibility, or allotropes — these are the three modern exceptions examiners reuse.

#### Advanced Practice Prompts

1. Sulphur forms two oxides. In one, 2.0 g of sulphur combines with 2.0 g of oxygen; in the other, 2.0 g of sulphur combines with 3.0 g of oxygen. Use Dalton's framework to identify the likely formulae and verify the Law of Multiple Proportions.
2. A sample of neon gas is found to contain 90 % ²⁰Ne, 9 % ²²Ne, and 1 % ²¹Ne by number. Calculate the average atomic mass (in u) and explain why this observation forces a correction to Dalton's second postulate.

#### Common Mistake Cluster (exam-frequent)

| Mistake | Correct handling |
|---|---|
| Treating "indivisible" as absolute | Dalton meant *chemically* indivisible |
| Mixing up Multiple vs Definite Proportions | Definite = one compound; Multiple = two compounds, same elements |
| Assuming every element is diatomic | Noble gases are monatomic; ozone is triatomic |
| Using mass ratio where mole ratio is asked | Ratio of masses ≠ ratio of atoms unless atomic masses are equal |
