# Foundations: The Four Laws

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

**Stoikiometri** is the quantitative backbone of every UTBK Kimia problem: it links **mass (m)**, **moles (n)**, **gas volume (V)**, **particle count (N)**, and **solution concentration (M)** through the **mol bridge**. The three conversion formulas you must memorise are:

- **n = m / Mr** (mass → moles; Mr = molar mass in g/mol)
- **n = V / Vm**, with **Vm = 22.4 L/mol at STP (0 °C, 1 atm)** and **Vm ≈ 24 L/mol at RTP (25 °C, 1 atm)**
- **n = M × V**, where **M** is molarity (mol/L) and **V** is in **litres**, not mL

The **limiting reactant** is found by dividing the available moles of each reactant by its **stoichiometric coefficient**; the smallest ratio wins. High-yield traps: confusing STP and RTP volumes, using mL directly in M×V, and assuming the empirical formula equals the molecular formula.

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Foundations: The Four Laws

Stoichiometric calculations rest on four laws that fix the ratios in which substances combine:

1. **Lavoisier (Conservation of Mass):** total mass of reactants = total mass of products; the basis for balancing equations.
2. **Proust (Definite Proportions):** a given compound always contains its constituent elements in a **fixed mass ratio**, regardless of source or preparation method.
3. **Dalton (Multiple Proportions):** when two elements form more than one compound (e.g. CO and CO₂), the masses of one element combining with a fixed mass of the other are in simple whole-number ratios.
4. **Gay-Lussac (Combining Volumes):** volumes of reacting gases (and gaseous products) at the same T and P are in simple whole-number ratios equal to the coefficients in the balanced equation. **Avogadro's hypothesis** then explains *why*: equal gas volumes at identical T and P contain equal numbers of particles.

#### The Mol as Universal Bridge

One **mole** = 6.022 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro's number, **L**). It converts between:

| Quantity | Formula | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Particles | N = n × L | particles |
| Mass | m = n × Mr | grams |
| Gas volume (STP) | V = n × 22.4 | L |
| Gas volume (RTP) | V = n × 24 | L |
| Solution | n = M × V(L) | mol |

#### Limiting Reactant and Yield

For a reaction **aA + bB → products**, compute **n_A / a** and **n_B / b**. The **smaller value** identifies the limiting reactant; the other is in excess. Theoretical yield of any product is then derived from the moles of limiting reactant and its stoichiometric coefficient.

#### Empirical vs Molecular Formula

**Empirical formula** = simplest whole-number mole ratio of elements (from % composition). **Molecular formula** = (empirical formula)ₙ, where **n = Mr_molecule / Mr_empirical** (rounded to nearest integer).

#### Typical UTBK Question Patterns

- Convert grams of a compound to litres of gas at STP, then to particle count.
- Given % composition, find empirical formula and identify the compound from a list of Mr values.
- Mix two solutions of known molarity; determine mass of precipitate using a balanced equation.
- Identify the limiting reactant in a combustion or acid–base problem and calculate % yield.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### Edge Cases and Precision

- **Non-ideal gases:** at high P or low T, the 22.4 L/mol figure drifts; UTBK sticks to ideal conditions, but read the question carefully — "pada suhu 27 °C, 1 atm" still uses 24 L/mol, not 22.4.
- **Hydrates and mixed salts:** when calculating Mr for **CuSO₄·5H₂O**, include the **5 × 18 = 90 g/mol** of water of crystallisation. For double salts like **KAl(SO₄)₂·12H₂O**, sum every atom.
- **Ionic compounds in solution:** the dissolved "particle" is the formula unit; e.g. 1 mol Na₃PO₄ yields 4 mol of ions (3 Na⁺ + 1 PO₄³⁻) for colligative-property extensions.

#### Worked Micro-Example

**Problem:** 8 g Ca(OH)₂ (Mr = 74) reacts with excess HCl. Find (a) moles of Ca(OH)₂, (b) mass of CaCl₂ (Mr = 111) produced.

**Solution:** Reaction: Ca(OH)₂ + 2 HCl → CaCl₂ + 2 H₂O
- n Ca(OH)₂ = 8 / 74 = 0.1081 mol
- Stoichiometry 1:1, so n CaCl₂ = 0.1081 mol
- m CaCl₂ = 0.1081 × 111 = **12.0 g**

#### Connections to Adjacent Topics

Stoichiometry feeds directly into **thermochemistry** (ΔH per mole of reaction), **equilibrium** (Kc and Kp from mole ratios), **electrochemistry** (Faraday's law uses moles of electrons), and **acid–base titration** (equivalence point = stoichiometric mole match).

#### Common Mistakes to Eliminate

1. Using **22.4** at RTP or **24** at STP — a 1-point loss, every time.
2. Forgetting the **stoichiometric coefficient** when selecting the limiting reactant.
3. Equating **Mr of an ionic compound** with the Ar of a single atom.
4. Treating **mass-%** as mole-% when deriving an empirical formula; always convert to moles first.
5. Rounding intermediate mole values prematurely — carry 4 significant figures until the final step.

#### Practice Prompts

1. A 4.0 g sample of an oxide of iron contains 2.8 g Fe. Determine its empirical formula and the oxidation state of Fe.
2. 200 mL of 0.15 M H₂SO₄ is mixed with 300 mL of 0.10 M NaOH. Which is the limiting reactant, and what is the mass of Na₂SO₄ (Mr = 142) formed?
