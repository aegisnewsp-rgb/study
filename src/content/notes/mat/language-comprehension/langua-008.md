# Critical Reasoning

### 🟢 Lite — Quick Review (1h–1d)
> Rapid summary for last-minute revision before your exam.

Critical Reasoning is the skill of dissecting an argument into its premises and conclusion, then judging whether the conclusion is supported, weakened, or assumed. In MAT it appears both as standalone argument-evaluator questions and as embedded items inside RC passages. The argument has three parts: **premises** (the evidence), a **conclusion** (the claim being defended), and an **assumption** (an unstated premise that links the two).

- **Argument skeleton:** Premises → Assumption → Conclusion.
- **Five question families:** Main Point, Assumption, Inference (Must/Can/Cannot Be True), Strengthen, Weaken.
- **Quick traps:** correlation written as causation, true-but-irrelevant facts, qualifier words (some, most, always) that change strength.

| Question type | What to ask yourself |
| --- | --- |
| Main Point | Which sentence does the rest of the passage support? |
| Assumption | What must be true for the conclusion to hold? |
| Must Be True | Which choice is forced by the premises alone? |

### 🟡 Standard — Regular Study (2d–2mo)
> Standard content for students with a few days to months.

#### Anatomy of an argument
Every argument rests on premises that lead, via an assumption, to a conclusion. The conclusion is the sentence the author wants you to accept; premises are offered as support; the assumption is the unstated bridge. Premises can be facts, statistics, examples, or expert opinions. A conclusion in many papers contains signal words like *therefore, thus, consequently, so, hence*. An assumption is rarely signalled and must be inferred by negating the candidate and checking whether the conclusion collapses — this is the **negation test**.

#### The five classic question types
MAT critical-reasoning items cluster into five families. Main-Point questions ask for the central claim; Assumption questions ask what must be true; Inference (Must Be True) questions force a single logically compelled statement; Strengthen questions supply evidence that makes the conclusion more likely; Weaken questions supply evidence that undermines it. A sixth type, **Logical Flaw**, names the fallacy — ad hominem, straw man, false cause, hasty generalisation, circular reasoning, appeal to authority, appeal to emotion, red herring, equivocation, slippery slope, either-or, post hoc.

#### Strengthen vs Weaken — the polarity trap
The most common error is mixing these two. Before selecting an answer, restate the conclusion and check whether the choice adds evidence that *helps* (strengthen) or *hurts* (weaken) that conclusion. A true statement that does not touch the logical gap is a distractor.

| Flaw | Pattern to spot |
| --- | --- |
| False cause | A assumes B because A precedes B |
| Hasty generalisation | Few cases → universal rule |
| Straw man | Distorts opponent's position, then refutes it |
| Ad hominem | Attacks the person, not the argument |
| Either-or | Forces two options when more exist |

#### Exam pattern for MAT
Critical Reasoning carries roughly 3% weight in the IBT/CBT MAT pattern and overlaps with the Intelligence & Critical Reasoning section in the legacy paper. Expect 4–6 standalone argument-evaluator questions, plus several embedded items inside reading-comprehension passages. Average time per question is 60–75 seconds. Read the conclusion first, then the premises, then the question stem — this order saves time.

### 🔴 Extended — Deep Study (3mo+)
> Comprehensive coverage for students on a longer study timeline.

#### The Fact–Opinion–Inference trichotomy
Every sentence in an argument belongs to one of three categories. **Facts** are verifiable claims about the world. **Opinions** are evaluative judgements. **Inferences** are conclusions drawn from facts and are neither stated nor verifiable directly. In MAT, Must-Be-True questions test inference; Strengthen/Weaken questions add new facts that change the probability of the inference. Confusing a fact with an inference, or an opinion with a fact, is the root cause of most wrong answers.

#### Weasel words and qualifiers
Words like *some, many, most, in many papers, in most keys, frequently, sometimes* limit the scope of a claim. Words like *all, every, always, never, none, no one* make it universal. A universal claim is far easier to disprove with a single counter-example; a qualified claim demands proportionally weaker evidence to weaken. Always copy the qualifier into your paraphrase of the conclusion before evaluating answer choices.

#### Worked micro-example
> *Premise:* "Sales of electric scooters rose 40% in 2024." 
> *Conclusion:* "Therefore, consumers prefer electric scooters to petrol scooters." 
> *Hidden assumption:* No other factor (fuel price, subsidy, new models) drove the rise. 
> *Weaken it:* "Petrol scooter sales fell 15% in the same period due to a fuel-price hike." 
> *Strengthen it:* "Consumer surveys report 62% of new buyers chose electric over petrol for environmental reasons."

The negation test confirms the assumption: if consumers *did* shift for other reasons, the preference conclusion fails. The weasel word *prefer* (subjective) versus *bought* (verifiable) is exactly the fact-inference trap MAT exploits.

#### Practice prompts
1. A city reports a rise in ice-cream sales and a rise in drownings in the same month. Identify the assumption required to conclude that ice-cream causes drowning, then write one sentence that strengthens and one that weakens the causal claim.
2. An editorial argues: "Since most students who topped the board exam attended coaching, coaching must be essential for board success." Name the logical flaw, state the missing assumption, and produce a Weaken choice in one sentence.

#### Common mistakes recap
- Confusing assumption with conclusion.
- Mixing Strengthen and Weaken polarity.
- Choosing plausible-but-not-required statements in Must-Be-True items.
- Treating correlation as causation.
- Ignoring qualifiers (some vs all).
- Picking topically matching but logically irrelevant choices.
