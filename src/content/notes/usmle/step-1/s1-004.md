# Pathology: Cell Injury and Inflammation

Step 1 "assesses your understanding of basic sciences crucial for practicing medicine", and pathology is the organising layer for much of it. Almost every disease question in the exam is a question about how tissue was injured and what the body did about it. If you are secure on the patterns in this topic, a large family of clinical stems becomes readable rather than a list to memorise.

## How cells get injured

Reversible injury is where function fails but structure survives, and several of the usual mechanisms operate at once. Hypoxia and ischaemia are the commonest initiator in infarction, and because ATP-dependent pumps fail first, swelling and loss of ion gradients come before any membrane rupture.

Reactive oxygen species do a similar kind of damage more diffusely: superoxide and hydroxyl radicals attack lipid membranes, proteins and DNA, while hydrogen peroxide is comparatively weak and does not cross membranes well. Chemical injury either disrupts the membrane directly or needs metabolic activation to produce a toxic metabolite, which is the route that makes the liver so vulnerable to acetaminophen. Infectious injury may be direct cytolytic damage, or damage mediated by the host response rather than by the organism itself, and immunologic injury happens when antibodies bind their targets or T cells attack cells carrying a foreign antigen.

Irreversible injury is the point past which a cell cannot recover: severe mitochondrial damage, extensive membrane rupture, or DNA damage beyond repair. Before that point, ATP-dependent repair and synthesis buy time.

## Adaptation before injury

Cells respond to stress by changing, and the type of change tells you whether the stimulus was normal.

Hypertrophy makes cells bigger without changing how many there are, and it answers a mechanical demand — the enlarging left ventricle of hypertension, or the gravid uterus. Hyperplasia raises the cell count instead, answering a hormonal or proliferative demand: the change in the endometrium across the cycle, or the liver growing back after loss. Atrophy runs the other way, from reduced supply or demand or the loss of a trophic stimulation — disuse atrophy, denervation atrophy, and the cachexia of serious illness.

Metaplasia is different in kind: one mature cell type is replaced by another better suited to a new environment. Smoking-induced squamous metaplasia of the respiratory epithelium is the archetype. It is protective in origin, which is precisely why it carries an increased cancer risk. Dysplasia is disordered growth with loss of normal architecture; it is not reversible, and it is the step that precedes malignancy.

The distinction doing the most work in questions is hypertrophy against hyperplasia. Both increase tissue bulk, but only hyperplasia changes cell number, which is why hyperplasia requires cells capable of division.

## Inflammation as a sequence

Inflammation is the coordinated response to injury, and it runs in recognisable stages. Learn the order, because questions often give you a stage and ask you what is happening or what comes next.

The immediate phase is vasodilatation with increased vascular permeability, which produces redness, warmth and swelling. Because permeability rises, protein-rich fluid leaves the vessels — which is why an inflammatory exudate is protein-rich while a transudate is not.

The cellular phase brings neutrophils first, and neutrophils dominate acute inflammation. Monocytes arrive next and become macrophages, which dominate when inflammation persists. The ordering is not arbitrary: neutrophils are fast responders with limited capacity, macrophages are slower and long-lived, and macrophages are what you find in chronic inflammation.

Chemical mediators tie it together. Histamine drives the early vasodilatation and permeability change; cytokines such as IL-1 and TNF drive endothelial activation and the systemic effects; prostaglandins and leukotrienes mediate vascular effects and chemotaxis.

## Acute versus chronic

Acute inflammation is short, dominated by neutrophils, and typically follows a clear trigger. Chronic inflammation persists, is dominated by macrophages, lymphocytes and fibroblasts, and is where tissue destruction and repair happen at the same time.

Acute runs on neutrophils, arrives quickly, and resolves into repair, scarring or an abscess. Chronic runs on macrophages, lymphocytes and fibroblasts, builds up gradually, and destroys tissue while replacing it. The common causes differ too: infection, injury or a foreign body on one side, persistent infection, autoimmune disease and irritants on the other.

Granulomatous inflammation is a chronic pattern worth recognising on sight. Macrophages transform into epithelioid cells and multinucleate giant cells, and the aggregate is a granuloma. Tuberculosis, sarcoidosis, leprosy and fungal infection are the classic causes, and caseating necrosis inside a granuloma points specifically to tuberculosis.

## The sequelae questions ask about

Resolution is complete recovery with restoration of normal architecture, possible when the injury is mild and the tissue survives. Repair by fibrosis goes the other way: granulation tissue deposits collagen and replaces lost tissue with scar rather than restoring function, and it is the same process — granulation tissue — that produces both repair and loss of function.

An abscess is a localised collection of pus walled off by granulation tissue, and it needs drainage rather than antibiotics alone. Organisation is an unresolved exudate turning fibrous. Caseous necrosis is the cheese-like debris found inside a granuloma. Gangrene is coagulative necrosis modified by saprophytic bacterial infection, while liquefactive necrosis — classically in an abscess — is what you get when the dead material becomes liquid.

## Common mistakes

Swapping hypertrophy and hyperplasia, or forgetting that atrophy can follow the loss of a trophic supply rather than only disuse.

Treating metaplasia as a mistake by the body. It is an adaptive response, and its significance comes from the increased cancer risk, not from being pathological in itself.

Assuming chronic inflammation is simply a worse version of acute inflammation rather than a different pattern with a different dominant cell.

Describing lipid, protein and cell-poor fluid when you mean a transudate — that pattern is increased hydrostatic pressure or low oncotic pressure, not inflammation.

And forgetting that granulation tissue is also the tissue that forms scar, so one process produces both repair and loss of function.
